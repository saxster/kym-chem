:::scene Karlsruhe, Germany, September 1860

In the autumn of 1860, about 140 chemists from all over Europe crowded into a hall in the German town of Karlsruhe. It was the first international meeting of chemists ever held, and it had been called because chemistry was in a muddle. Everyone agreed that substances were made of atoms. Almost nobody agreed on *how many* atoms were in anything. Was water HO or H₂O? One textbook published the next year listed nineteen different formulas that chemists had proposed for acetic acid, the acid in vinegar. If you cannot agree on the recipe, you cannot cook.

For three days they argued and got nowhere. Then, as people were leaving, a friend of the Italian chemist Stanislao Cannizzaro handed out copies of a little booklet Cannizzaro had written. Cannizzaro was no ordinary professor: as a young man he had fought in the Sicilian revolution of 1848 and had to flee into exile, and he wrote his booklet to help his own students make sense of a subject he thought was being taught as a jumble. It showed how an idea almost everyone had forgotten, published half a century earlier by Amedeo Avogadro, a lawyer from Turin who had given up the law for physics and spent his life teaching quietly, largely ignored, could sort the whole mess out. One young German chemist, Lothar Meyer, read it on the journey home and later wrote that it was as if the scales fell from his eyes. Another person in that hall was a young Russian called Dmitri Mendeleev, the youngest child of a big family from Siberia, whose widowed mother had travelled across Russia with him so that he could study. He would use the same clear atomic weights a few years later to build the periodic table.

:::story avogadro

This chapter is about the problem those chemists solved: *how much*. Chapters 4 and 5 were about what happens in a reaction. But if you want to make 10 grams of something, how much of each ingredient should you weigh out? A cook can measure cups and spoons, because flour and sugar are big enough to see. A chemist's real ingredients are molecules, and the recipe in a balanced equation counts molecules. So the whole chapter is one translation: from counting particles, which is what nature does, to weighing grams and measuring litres, which is what we can do.

## 6.1 Ratios tell a story

:::scene London, May 1800

Weeks after Alessandro Volta in Italy announced the first battery, two Englishmen, William Nicholson and Anthony Carlisle, made one of their own and dipped its two wires into water. Bubbles streamed up from both wires. When they collected the gases, there was always about twice as much gas from one wire as from the other. The bigger bubble of gas burned with a pop: hydrogen. The smaller one was oxygen, the gas that makes a glowing splint burst into flame. Water, the most ordinary thing in the world, had come apart into two gases, and always in the same proportion.

That 2 : 1 is not an accident. A few years later Joseph Gay-Lussac in Paris found that gases always combine in simple whole-number volumes, and in 1811 Avogadro guessed why: equal volumes of gas, at the same temperature and pressure, hold equal numbers of molecules. So twice the volume of hydrogen means twice the number of molecules. The formula H₂O says it in letters: two hydrogen atoms for every one oxygen atom. One molecule has 2 H and 1 O. Five molecules have 10 H and 5 O. A glassful has an enormous number of each, but always exactly twice as many hydrogens as oxygens. Chemists call this an ==atom ratio==.

:::note Atom ratio
The ratio of the *numbers* of atoms of each element, such as 2 hydrogen : 1 oxygen in water.
:::

Now for the trap that caught chemists for decades. If water has twice as many hydrogen atoms, surely most of its mass is hydrogen? It is the other way round. An oxygen atom has about 16 times the mass of a hydrogen atom. In each water molecule, the two hydrogens together weigh about 2 units and the one oxygen about 16. By mass, water is roughly 1 part hydrogen to 8 parts oxygen. Weigh the two gases that Nicholson and Carlisle collected, and the small bubble of oxygen is about eight times heavier than the big bubble of hydrogen. Almost nine-tenths of the mass of every glass of water you drink is oxygen.

:::fig c6_ratio_vs_mass() | Five water molecules counted two ways. By number, there are twice as many hydrogen atoms as oxygen atoms. By mass, the oxygen atoms outweigh the hydrogen atoms about eight to one, because each oxygen atom is about 16 times heavier.

Both numbers are right. They answer different questions: *how many?* and *how heavy?* A ratio only means something once you say what you are counting: atoms, molecules, or grams. Most mistakes in this chapter, and most of the muddle at Karlsruhe, come from mixing them up.

:::

The atom ratio is 1 : 2 (one carbon, two oxygens). The mass ratio is 12 : 32, which simplifies to 3 : 8. So although carbon dioxide is "one-third carbon" by atoms, it is only about 27% carbon by mass.

## 6.2 Counting beyond what we can see

In Chapter 0 you met the ==mole==: a counting unit, like a dozen, but holding 6.022 × 10²³ things. This is where it earns its keep, so let us take it slowly.

The point of a dozen is that it is the same number whatever you are counting. A dozen eggs and a dozen watermelons are both twelve things, even though the watermelons are far heavier. A mole works in exactly the same way. A mole of carbon atoms and a mole of water molecules contain the same number of particles, 6.022 × 10²³ each. But a carbon atom and a water molecule have different masses, so the two piles weigh different amounts: about 12 grams for the carbon, about 18 grams for the water.

:::fig c6_mole_piles() | One mole each of three substances. Each pile holds the same number of particles, 6.022 × 10²³. The masses differ because the particles themselves have different masses.

**Predict first:** a tablespoon and a bit of water (18 grams) is one mole of water molecules. If you could line those molecules up side by side across the width of the spoon, roughly how many would fit: a thousand? a million? a thousand million? Write your guess, then zoom in.

:::fig i_molezoom() | A set of separate reference sizes. Read the enlargement factor on each arrow; the factors differ. This is not a continuous trip inside one object or evidence that the smaller examples lie inside the larger ones. A water molecule is roughly 0.3 nanometres across.
:::live molezoom {}

Every view is drawn at its true size, and the box in the corner counts how many water molecules would fit side by side across it. Across the spoon it is roughly two hundred million. Shrink the view to the width of a hair and it is still a quarter of a million. Only at the very bottom, a few nanometres across, can you count them on your fingers.

Now the real surprise. Two hundred million across sounds enormous, but the spoon is three-dimensional. Pour the water into a cube 2.6 centimetres wide and about 84 million molecules sit along each edge, so the whole cube holds 84 million × 84 million × 84 million: about 6 × 10²³, one mole. Multiplying the same big number by itself three times is what makes a mole so unimaginably large. And yet it is only a tablespoon and a bit of water. That is how small molecules are.


Here is the clever part, the trick that made the whole of quantitative chemistry possible. Because a carbon atom is 12 units and an oxygen atom 16, then 12 grams of carbon and 16 grams of oxygen must contain *the same number of atoms*, whatever that number is. Weigh out any element's relative mass in grams and you always get the same count. Chemists used this trick for decades before anyone knew what the count actually was. It was a bit like buying eggs by the dozen without knowing that a dozen is twelve, and it worked, because what matters in a recipe is that the number is the same for everything.

The number itself was finally pinned down in the early 1900s by the French physicist Jean Perrin, who measured it in several completely different ways and got the same answer each time. He suggested naming it after Avogadro, and today it is ==Avogadro's constant==, fixed since 2019 at exactly 6.022 140 76 × 10²³.

:::note Avogadro's constant
The number of things in one mole: 6.022 140 76 × 10²³, exactly. Named after Amedeo Avogadro; measured accurately by Jean Perrin and many others.
:::

:::story mole

This is why chemists use moles rather than grams when they think about reactions. Reactions happen particle by particle: one carbon atom meets one oxygen molecule. If you want equal *numbers* of particles, you want equal numbers of moles, not equal numbers of grams.

There is one rule you must never skip: always say what the "things" are. One mole of water *molecules* contains three moles of *atoms*, because every molecule has three atoms. One mole of oxygen gas, O₂, contains two moles of oxygen atoms. Saying "a mole of oxygen" without saying which is like saying "a dozen shoes" when you mean a dozen pairs.

:::careful
> "One mole of water contains more molecules than one mole of carbon contains atoms, because water is heavier."

The mole is a *number*, so one mole of anything contains exactly the same number of particles: 6.022 × 10²³. Water's extra mass comes from each molecule being heavier (about 18 units against carbon's 12), not from there being more of them.
:::

:::home Count dal without counting
You need a kitchen scale, a bowl, and a packet of dry dal or lentils. Count out exactly 100 grains and weigh them (if your scale only shows whole grams, count 200 or 500). Work out the mass of one grain. Now fill a cup with dal, weigh it, and divide by the mass of one grain: that is how many grains are in the cup. Check by counting a small spoonful. You have just counted thousands of things by weighing them, exactly as chemists count atoms with moles.

Then build a mole display on the kitchen counter. Measure out 18 g of water (about a tablespoon and a bit), 58 g of salt (a little over three level tablespoons) and 342 g of sugar (about one and three-quarter cups). Three very different heaps, and each holds exactly the same number of particles: one mole, six hundred thousand million million million.
:::

:::five The mole
? What is it?
A counting unit for very small things, like a dozen, but much bigger: one mole is 6.022 × 10²³ particles. Its magic is that one mole of any substance has a mass in grams equal to its formula's mass.
? Why is it called this?
From the German *Mol*, which Wilhelm Ostwald coined in the 1890s as a short form of *Molekül*, molecule. It has nothing to do with the animal.
? What problem did it solve?
Reactions happen particle by particle, but chemists can only weigh things, never count atoms. The mole turns a kitchen-scale reading into a count of particles.
? Why does it work?
Atoms of different elements have different masses in fixed ratios: carbon 12, oxygen 16. So 12 grams of carbon and 16 grams of oxygen hold the same number of atoms. Pick that number as your unit, and weighing becomes counting.
? How does it help?
It tells you exactly how much of each ingredient a reaction needs and how much product you will get, from a medicine factory to a rocket's fuel tanks.
?? How do we know?
Jean Perrin, in the early 1900s, measured the number in several completely different ways, including watching tiny grains jiggle in water, and they all agreed.
?? What it isn't
It is not a mass. A mole is a number of things, and a mole of gold weighs far more than a mole of hydrogen.
?? Where does it stop working?
It is only useful for huge numbers of tiny things. For a handful of molecules, or anything you can count by eye, just count.
:::

:::check
You have 1 mole of oxygen gas, O₂. How many O₂ molecules is that, and how many oxygen atoms?
???
6.022 × 10²³ molecules of O₂. Each molecule has two atoms, so there are 2 × 6.022 × 10²³ ≈ 1.2 × 10²⁴ oxygen atoms.
:::

## 6.3 From moles to mass

:::scene Stockholm, around 1810 to 1818

In a small, cramped laboratory in Stockholm, Jöns Jacob Berzelius, the symbol-maker you met in Chapter 4, spent years doing something heroically patient. He took compound after compound, around two thousand of them, purified each one, and weighed exactly how much of each element it contained. From those numbers he worked out how heavy the atoms of dozens of elements are compared with each other, and many of his values were remarkably close to the ones we use today. Every molar mass in this chapter rests on work like his.

Here is why it matters. You cannot set a balance to "count out half a mole". A balance measures grams. So you need a conversion factor between moles and grams, and it is different for every substance. It is called the ==molar mass==: the mass of one mole, in grams per mole (g/mol). For water it is about 18.0 g/mol; for carbon dioxide, about 44.0 g/mol; for table salt, sodium chloride, about 58.4 g/mol.

:::note Molar mass
The mass of one mole of a substance, in grams per mole (g/mol). For water it is about 18.0 g/mol.
:::

Working one out is just adding up the atoms in the formula, using the average atomic masses on the periodic table (Section 1.3). Let us do water slowly:

:::work
Water is H₂O: two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom.
Hydrogen: 2 × 1.008 = 2.016
Oxygen: 1 × 15.999 = 15.999
Add them: 2.016 + 15.999 = 18.015 g/mol
:::

Then the conversion is one multiplication, or one division:

:::eq
mass (g) = moles (mol) × molar mass (g/mol)
:::

:::fig c6_unit_chain() | The bridge between what you can weigh and what you want to count. Divide grams by the molar mass to get moles; multiply moles by Avogadro's constant to get the number of particles. Go the other way by doing the opposite.

Here is a habit that will save you over and over: write the units on every number and watch them cancel, like fractions. How much carbon dioxide is in half a mole of it?

:::work
0.50 mol × 44.0 g/mol = 22 g
(mol × g/mol: the "mol" on top cancels the "mol" underneath, leaving grams.)
:::

And the other way round: how many moles are in 36 g of water?

:::work
36.0 g ÷ 18.0 g/mol = 2.0 mol
(g ÷ g/mol: the grams cancel, leaving moles.)
:::

If the units do not cancel to what you want, you have multiplied when you should have divided. Units are a free check on your arithmetic, and good chemists never leave them off.

:::careful
> "The equation says 2 H₂O, so I use a molar mass of 36 for water."

The molar mass belongs to the *substance*, not to the equation. Water is 18.0 g/mol whether an equation says 2 H₂O or 10 H₂O. The number in front tells you how many moles you have; each of those moles still has a mass of 18.0 g.
:::

:::careful
> "18.015 is the mass number of water."

Mass numbers belong to single nuclei and are always whole numbers (Section 1.5). The 18.015 g/mol is an *average*: it comes from the average atomic masses of hydrogen and oxygen, which already blend in their natural mixtures of isotopes. That is why it has decimals.
:::

:::try Investigation 6.3
Use 18.015 g/mol and compare 1 mol with 2 mol. Before you look, say what the mass will be. Then try a substance with a molar mass of 44 g/mol: what mass is half a mole?
:::

:::check
A chemist weighs out 36.03 g of pure water. How many moles is that, and how many molecules?
???
36.03 g ÷ 18.015 g/mol = 2.000 mol. That is 2 × 6.022 × 10²³ ≈ 1.204 × 10²⁴ molecules. The grams cancel, leaving moles: g ÷ (g/mol) = mol.
:::

## 6.4 Which reactant runs out first?

:::scene Launch pad 39A, Florida, 1981 to 2011

For thirty years, the Space Shuttle's three main engines burned liquid hydrogen with liquid oxygen, the same reaction Nicholson and Carlisle had run backwards in 1800: 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O. The perfect recipe, by mass, is about 8 kilograms of oxygen for every 1 kilogram of hydrogen. But the engineers deliberately loaded the engines to burn only about 6 kilograms of oxygen per kilogram of hydrogen. That meant the oxygen always ran out first, and roughly a quarter of the hydrogen shot out of the nozzle unburnt. It was not waste: a hydrogen-rich exhaust is lighter, so it rushes out faster and pushes the rocket harder. The engineers had chosen which reactant would run out.

In chemistry, the ingredient that runs out first is called the ==limiting reactant==. Whatever is left over is in ==excess==.

:::note Limiting reactant
The reactant that gets used up first, which stops the reaction and limits how much product can form.
:::

:::note Excess reactant
A reactant left over once the limiting reactant has run out.
:::

Here is the idea with something easier to picture than a rocket. A bicycle needs one frame and two wheels. A workshop has 5 frames and 6 wheels. How many bicycles can it build? Not five: it runs out of wheels after three bicycles, with two frames left over. The wheels are limiting; the frames are in excess.

A complete-set token puzzle works like this. A balanced net equation gives proportional amounts; it does not say these molecules collide all at once. For hydrogen burning in oxygen:

:::eq
2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O
:::

each "set" needs two hydrogen molecules and one oxygen molecule, and makes two water molecules. Suppose you start with 5 H₂ and 2 O₂. The two oxygen molecules can make two complete sets, using 4 of the H₂. That makes 4 H₂O and leaves 1 H₂ with no oxygen to react with. Oxygen is the limiting reactant.

:::fig c6_limiting() | Five hydrogen molecules and two oxygen molecules, grouped into complete sets of the recipe 2 H₂ + O₂. Two full sets fit, making four water molecules; one hydrogen molecule is left over.
:::live limiting {}

Now add one more O₂, so you have 5 H₂ and 3 O₂. You might expect more water. But a third set needs two more H₂, and only one is left. So you still make 4 H₂O, and now one H₂ *and* one O₂ are left over. Neither token stock is exhausted: the puzzle only permits complete written groups. This rule does not establish the fate of an actual handful of reacting molecules.

:::work
**Sets possible from each reactant** (for 5 H₂ and 3 O₂)
From H₂: 5 ÷ 2 = 2.5, so only 2 complete sets.
From O₂: 3 ÷ 1 = 3 sets.
The smaller number wins: 2 sets, making 2 × 2 = 4 H₂O.
:::

For **bulk amounts in moles**, compare n(H₂)/2 and n(O₂) without rounding down. With 5 mol H₂ and 3 mol O₂, the extent is min(2.5, 3) = 2.5 mol: the theoretical yield is 5 mol H₂O, leaving 0 mol H₂ and 0.5 mol O₂. This assumes complete conversion to the stated products under suitable conditions. An obtained yield requires measurement. Three moles of reactant molecules need not remain three moles of product molecules; each element and total chemical mass are conserved.

:::home Balloons that stop growing
You need four small bottles, four balloons, vinegar, baking soda and a funnel (a rolled-up piece of paper will do). Pour the same 50 mL of vinegar into each bottle. Using the funnel, put ¼ teaspoon of baking soda into the first balloon, ½ teaspoon into the second, 1 teaspoon into the third and 2 teaspoons into the fourth. Stretch each balloon over a bottle's neck without letting the powder fall in, then lift them all up together so the soda drops into the vinegar.

*Predict first:* how big will each balloon get? Draw your guess.

The first two grow in step with the soda you gave them. But the third and fourth end up about the same size, and the fourth has white powder left over at the bottom. Fifty millilitres of ordinary 5% vinegar (check the label; weaker vinegar runs out sooner) can only use up about three-quarters of a teaspoon of soda. After that, the vinegar has run out: it has become the limiting reactant, and the extra soda is in excess, with nothing left to react with.
:::

:::


:::five Limiting reactant
? What is it?
The ingredient that runs out first in a reaction, and so decides how much product you can make. Whatever is left over is "in excess".
? Why is it called this?
It sets the *limit* on how much product can form, just as the number of wheels limits how many bicycles a workshop can build.
? What problem did it solve?
It explains why adding more of one ingredient sometimes makes no difference at all, and lets chemists and engineers stop wasting the ingredient they have too much of.
? Why does it work?
A balanced equation is a recipe in whole particles. Once one ingredient has been used up, no more complete "sets" of the recipe can be made, however much of the others is left.
? How does it help?
You can predict the most product a reaction can give, and choose which ingredient to put in extra on purpose, as the Space Shuttle's engineers did with hydrogen.
?? How do we know?
Add more and more baking soda to the same vinegar: the balloons grow, then stop growing, and powder is left over at the bottom.
?? What it isn't
It is not always the ingredient you have the least of. It is the one that runs out first *for that recipe*, so you have to compare against the equation.
?? Where does it stop working?
Real reactions do not always go all the way to the end, and some ingredients get used in side reactions, so the actual amount of product is often less than this prediction.
:::

:::check
For 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O, you start with 6 H₂ and 2 O₂. How many water molecules can form, and what is left over?
???
Each O₂ needs two H₂, so 2 O₂ use 4 H₂ and make 4 H₂O. Oxygen runs out first; 2 H₂ are left over.
:::

## 6.5 Bulk limiting amounts: no whole-mole rounding

A small-token puzzle only permits complete written groups. It cannot determine a microscopic mechanism or justify rounding down a bulk amount. For 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O, use ξ = min(n(H₂)/2, n(O₂)).

With 5 mol H₂ and 3 mol O₂, ξ = 2.5 mol: 5 mol water forms and 0.5 mol O₂ remains. Using rounded molar masses, 10 g H₂ + 96 g O₂ = 90 g water + 16 g excess O₂. The theoretical yield assumes completion to the stated products under suitable conditions; obtained yield needs a measurement.

## 6.6 How much in each litre?

:::scene Leith, Scotland, 1832

Cholera swept through Britain in 1832. Its victims lost so much water and salt from their bodies that their blood grew thick and dark, and most of the very sick died. A Scottish doctor called Thomas Latta tried something astonishing for the time: he dissolved ordinary salt in warm water and let it run straight into a patient's vein. Some of his dying patients sat up within hours. It took the rest of the medical world many decades to follow him, partly because nobody yet understood exactly *how much* salt the water should hold. Too little or too much does harm. What mattered was not how much salt was in the whole bag, but how salty each drop was.

That "how salty is each drop" is what chemists call ==concentration==: the number of moles of a dissolved substance in each litre of the finished solution.

:::note Concentration
How much of a substance is dissolved in each litre of solution, in moles per litre (mol/L).
:::

:::eq
concentration (mol/L) = moles dissolved ÷ volume of solution (L)
:::

One mole of salt made up to 2 litres of solution has a concentration of 0.50 mol/L. The same mole made up to 4 litres is 0.25 mol/L: the particles are spread twice as thinly. And 2 moles in 4 litres is back to 0.50 mol/L, the same strength as the first, just more of it.

:::fig c6_concentration() | Three solutions. The first and third are equally concentrated, 0.50 mol/L, because the amount of salt and the volume have both doubled. The middle one has the same amount of salt as the first, spread through twice the volume.

:::story concentration

Concentration is like how crowded a room is, not how many people are in the building. Pour a cup from a big jug of nimbu pani and the cup tastes exactly as sour and sweet as the jug, even though it holds far less sugar. That is why a modern hospital saline drip, the great-grandchild of Latta's experiment, is labelled with its concentration: 0.9% salt, which is 9 grams of sodium chloride in every litre, or about 0.154 mol/L. The bag might be large or small; every drop has the same concentration, close to the saltiness of the fluid in your blood.

:::home Same sweetness, different amounts
Make two glasses of sugar water (or sharbat). In the first, stir 1 level teaspoon of sugar into half a cup of water. In the second, stir 2 level teaspoons into a full cup. Taste them both. They should taste equally sweet, even though the second glass holds twice as much sugar: the *concentration* is the same. Now pour half of the first glass away and taste what is left. Still the same. Concentration does not care how much you have, only how crowded it is.
:::

:::careful
> "To make 1 litre of a 1 mol/L solution, add 1 mole of salt to 1 litre of water."

Not quite. The solid takes up some room, so you would end up with a little more than 1 litre of solution, and a slightly weaker concentration. Chemists dissolve the solid in less water first, then add water until the total volume of *solution* reaches exactly 1 litre, using a flask with a line marked on its neck.
:::

:::


:::five Concentration
? What is it?
How much of a dissolved substance is in each litre of solution, usually counted in moles per litre (mol/L). It is how crowded the solution is.
? Why is it called this?
From Latin *com-* ("together") and *centrum* ("centre"): to concentrate is to gather together. A concentrated solution has its dissolved particles packed close.
? What problem did it solve?
Doctors and chemists needed to know not just how much salt or medicine they had, but how strong each drop was. Thomas Latta's salt-water drip in 1832 had to be the right strength to save lives.
? Why does it work?
The effect of a dissolved substance depends on how many of its particles are near you, in each sip or each drop of blood, not on how much is in the whole jug.
? How does it help?
It lets you make a solution of exactly the right strength, from oral rehydration salts to a lab reagent, and work out how much is in any amount of it.
?? How do we know?
A cup poured from a jug of nimbu pani tastes just as strong as the jug: same concentration, smaller amount.
?? What it isn't
It is not the total amount. A bucket and a spoonful of the same sea water have the same concentration.
?? Where does it stop working?
It is "per litre of *solution*", not per litre of water, and volumes shift slightly with temperature, so very careful work measures and calculates with that in mind.
:::

:::check
Bottle A holds 1.0 L of salt solution at 0.50 mol/L. Bottle B holds 4.0 L at 0.25 mol/L. Which tastes saltier? Which contains more salt in total?
???
Bottle A tastes saltier, because its concentration is higher. But bottle B contains more salt in total: 4.0 L × 0.25 mol/L = 1.0 mol, against 1.0 L × 0.50 mol/L = 0.50 mol in A.
:::

## 6.7 From laboratory grams to product grams

You now have every piece the chemists at Karlsruhe were missing. So try a real chemist's question, the one every medicine factory, fertiliser plant and school laboratory asks every day: *if I start with this many grams, how many grams of product can I make?*

The secret is that a balanced equation's numbers count particles, or moles. They are never grams. So the route always goes through moles, in three steps:

:::eq
grams of reactant → moles of reactant → moles of product → grams of product
:::

Take hydrogen burning again, 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O. The equation says 2 moles of hydrogen react with 1 mole of oxygen to make 2 moles of water. In grams, using molar masses of about 2.0, 32.0 and 18.0 g/mol, that is 4.0 g of hydrogen with 32.0 g of oxygen, making 36.0 g of water.

:::fig c6_gram_chain() | The same reaction read in moles and in grams. The mole numbers follow the equation, 2 : 1 : 2. The gram numbers do not, but they still add up: 4.0 g + 32.0 g = 36.0 g, because no atom is lost.

Notice two things. First, the gram numbers (4 : 32 : 36) look nothing like the equation's 2 : 1 : 2. Second, the grams still add up perfectly, 4.0 + 32.0 = 36.0, because atoms are never lost (Lavoisier's great discovery in Chapter 4). Moles follow the equation; grams follow conservation of mass. Both are true at once.

Here is the three-step route with your hand held all the way:

:::work
**How much carbon dioxide comes from burning 8.0 g of methane?** CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O
Step 1, grams to moles: methane is 16.0 g/mol, so 8.0 g ÷ 16.0 g/mol = 0.50 mol.
Step 2, moles to moles: the equation says 1 CH₄ makes 1 CO₂, so 0.50 mol of CO₂ forms.
Step 3, moles to grams: carbon dioxide is 44.0 g/mol, so 0.50 mol × 44.0 g/mol = 22 g.
:::

The answer you get this way is the most you could possibly make, if every molecule of the limiting reactant reacts and nothing is spilt. Real reactions usually give a little less, and chemists call the comparison between what they got and what they could have got the *yield*.

:::careful
> "2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O means 2 grams of hydrogen react with 1 gram of oxygen."

The numbers in an equation count particles, not grams. An oxygen molecule has about 16 times the mass of a hydrogen molecule, so the 1 mole of oxygen weighs far more (32 g) than the 2 moles of hydrogen (4 g).
:::

:::try Investigation 6.7
Compare 1.00 mol of O₂ (32 g/mol) with 2.00 mol of H₂O (18 g/mol). Explain why a 1 : 2 mole ratio does not give a 1 : 2 gram ratio.
:::

:::check
In CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O, how many grams of water form from burning 8.0 g of methane (16.0 g/mol)? Water is 18.0 g/mol.
???
8.0 g ÷ 16.0 g/mol = 0.50 mol of methane. The equation gives 2 H₂O for every 1 CH₄, so 1.0 mol of water forms: 1.0 mol × 18.0 g/mol = 18 g.
:::

## The missing four grams

:::case The missing four grams
A technician puts 12.0 g of hydrogen gas and 64.0 g of oxygen gas into a strong sealed chamber and sets off a spark. The reaction is 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O. She expects 12.0 g + 64.0 g = 76.0 g of water. When she collects and weighs the water, there is only 72.0 g.

1. Molar masses: H₂ 2.0 g/mol, O₂ 32.0 g/mol, H₂O 18.0 g/mol.
2. The chamber stayed sealed, and weighed the same before and after.
3. After the reaction, a colourless gas is still left in the chamber. It burns with a squeaky pop when lit.

Where did the "missing" 4.0 g go? Which reactant was limiting, and how should she have predicted 72.0 g?
???
Nothing went missing: 4.0 g of hydrogen never reacted. Convert to moles first: 12.0 g ÷ 2.0 g/mol = 6.0 mol of H₂, and 64.0 g ÷ 32.0 g/mol = 2.0 mol of O₂. The recipe needs 2 mol of H₂ for each mol of O₂, so 2.0 mol of O₂ can use only 4.0 mol of H₂. Oxygen is the limiting reactant. It makes 4.0 mol of water, 4.0 × 18.0 = 72.0 g, and leaves 2.0 mol of hydrogen, 2.0 × 2.0 = 4.0 g, still in the chamber as gas (clue 3: hydrogen burns with a pop). Clue 2 confirms that no mass was lost: 72.0 g + 4.0 g = 76.0 g. Her mistake was adding grams instead of comparing moles, exactly the kind of muddle the chemists at Karlsruhe set out to end.
:::
