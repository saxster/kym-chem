:::scene A rooftop in India, Diwali night

Rockets climb into the dark and burst: crimson, then emerald green, then a gold that crackles, then a deep blue that is the hardest colour of all to make. Every one of those colours was chosen by a chemist. The crimson comes from strontium compounds packed into the shell, the green from barium, the yellow-gold from sodium, the blue from copper. Heat a compound of any one of these metals hot enough and it glows with its own colour, as reliably as a signature.

In the 1850s two friends in Heidelberg turned that signature into an instrument. Robert Bunsen was a tough, kindly chemist who had lost the sight of one eye in a laboratory explosion and carried on regardless; Gustav Kirchhoff was a younger physicist who had worked out the rules of electric circuits while still a student. Bunsen had a burner with an almost colourless flame (the one still named after him), and Kirchhoff passed the light from it through a glass prism. Each element's glow split into its own pattern of sharp, bright coloured lines, different from every other element's. In 1860 they found a pair of sky-blue lines that belonged to no known element, and named the new metal caesium, from the Latin for sky-blue. A year later deep red lines gave them rubidium, from the Latin for dark red. Light from a flame had become a way of discovering elements nobody had seen.

Light would keep giving up secrets for the rest of the story. In 1921 an Indian physicist, C. V. Raman, sailing home from England, stared at the deep blue of the Mediterranean and refused to accept the usual explanation that the sea simply reflects the sky. Back in Calcutta he and his students spent years shining light through liquids, and in 1928 they found that a tiny fraction of the scattered light comes out with a slightly *different* colour, changed by the vibrating molecules it has met. Each kind of molecule changes it in its own way. Raman won the 1930 Nobel Prize in Physics for it, the first Asian scientist to win a Nobel Prize in science, and chemists still use his effect to identify substances by the light they scatter.

:::story star-stuff

By the 1860s chemists knew about sixty elements, and they were drowning in facts about them. Which belonged together, and why?

:::scene St Petersburg, 1869

Dmitri Mendeleev was the youngest child of a large family in Tobolsk, in Siberia. His father, a headmaster, went blind; his mother kept the family going by running a glass factory, and when it burned down she took her teenage son thousands of kilometres to St Petersburg so that he could study science. She died soon after he got in. By 1869 he was a professor there, wild-haired and famously impatient, and he was writing a chemistry textbook for his students and needed a sensible order to describe the elements in. He wrote each element's properties on cards and arranged them, in order of the masses of their atoms, so that elements that behaved alike fell into the same columns. (A famous story says the final arrangement came to him in a dream after days of shuffling the cards like a game of patience. It comes from a friend's recollection years later, so treat it as a legend.) He did all this without knowing about protons, neutrons or electrons, purely from the way the elements reacted.

His boldest move was to leave gaps. Where the pattern demanded an element nobody had found, he left a space and described what would fill it. Below aluminium, he said, there should be a metal with atoms about 68 times as heavy as hydrogen, a density of about 5.9 grams per cubic centimetre, and a low melting point. In 1875 Paul-Émile Lecoq de Boisbaudran, a largely self-taught French chemist from a family of brandy merchants in Cognac, looking at a zinc ore through exactly the kind of spectroscope Bunsen and Kirchhoff had built, saw two violet lines nobody had seen before. He named the new metal gallium. His first measurement of its density was 4.7. Mendeleev wrote to tell him it should be closer to 5.9; Lecoq purified his sample, measured again, and got 5.9.

:::story mendeleev

A table that predicts things nobody has yet seen must be catching something real. But what? Mendeleev could not say. The answer came in the next fifty years, from the inside of the atom, and you already know the beginning of it: the gold foil of Chapter 1, the nucleus, and the electrons arranged in shells around it. This chapter uses that to explain two of the table's patterns: the sizes of atoms, and how tightly they hold their electrons.

:::story periodic-table

:::home Rainbows from a CD
You can split light into its colours the way Kirchhoff did, using an old CD or DVD instead of a prism. In a darkened room, hold the shiny side of the disc so that it reflects light from a lamp into your eye, and tilt it until you see a band of colours. Try different lights around the house: an old-style bulb, a white LED, a fluorescent tube, a phone screen. Some give a smooth rainbow; others give separate bright stripes of colour with dark gaps between them. If you can, try an orange sodium street lamp at night: older ones give almost nothing but a single strong yellow-orange band, the same yellow that salt gives a flame. (Never look at the Sun, directly or in the disc.) Then, with an adult at the gas stove, ask them to sprinkle a few grains of table salt onto the blue flame while you stand back (the grains crackle and can jump), and look at the flash through your disc: the same yellow as the street lamp. The flash is brief, so it may take a few tries to catch it. That is Bunsen and Kirchhoff's discovery, in your kitchen.

## 9.1 Why atoms shrink across a row

:::scene Manchester, 1913

Mendeleev had ordered the elements by mass, and in a few places that order was wrong. Tellurium atoms are heavier than iodine atoms, yet tellurium clearly belongs in front of iodine. Mendeleev simply swapped them, trusting the chemistry over the masses. In 1913 Henry Moseley, a young physicist working in Rutherford's laboratory in Manchester, found out why. He fired electrons at targets made of different elements and measured the X-rays that came off. The X-ray frequency rose in a perfectly regular staircase, one step per element, and the steps counted the positive charge on the nucleus: the number of protons. That number, the atomic number, is the true order of the table, and tellurium (52) comes before iodine (53). Moseley was killed two years later, at Gallipoli in the First World War, at the age of 27.

:::story atomic-number

So the table is built one proton at a time. Across the second row, from lithium to fluorine, every step adds one proton to the nucleus and one electron around it. You might expect each atom to be a little bigger than the one before. Measurements say the opposite. A lithium atom is the largest in the row; a fluorine atom is less than half its width.

How can anyone measure something so small? You cannot put a ruler on an atom, but you can measure the distance between two atoms joined by a bond, by bouncing X-rays off crystals or by studying the light that gases absorb. Half the distance between two bonded atoms of the same element gives a rough size for one atom. The distance changes a little from one compound to another, so chemists average thousands of measurements from many different molecules and crystals. Those averages are the sizes in the next figure.

:::figwide c9_radii() | Atom sizes, measured as the distance from the centre of an atom to the edge of its share of a bond (in picometres, pm: millionths of a millionth of a metre). Across row 2 the atoms shrink; going down column 1 they grow. Circles are drawn to scale with each other.

To see why, think about the electrons as two teams. Lithium has 3 protons and 3 electrons: 2 electrons in the inner shell and 1 in the outer shell. Fluorine has 9 protons and 9 electrons: the same 2 inner electrons, and 7 outer ones. In every atom of row 2 the outer electrons are in the same shell, and the inner shell is the same two electrons.

The outer electrons are pulled inwards by the protons, and pushed outwards by the other electrons (remember from Chapter 2: like charges repel). The two inner electrons sit between the nucleus and the outer shell, so they cancel out a large part of the nucleus's pull. This is called ==shielding==. Think of the nucleus as a bright lamp and the inner electrons as a screen around it: an outer electron sees the lamp dimmed. (The screen is not a solid wall; it is made of electrons that are themselves moving. But on average it sits between.) Electrons in the *same* shell are no better placed than each other, so they shield one another only a little.

Now take one step across the row, from lithium to beryllium. Every proton pulls on every electron, so the extra proton pulls on *each* outer electron, not just on "its own". And the extra electron does not share out the nucleus's pull more thinly, as if the nucleus had a fixed amount of pull to divide up; it only adds a small push from a neighbour that is no closer to the nucleus. More pull on each outer electron, hardly any more screening: so with every step the outer shell is held in more tightly, and the atoms get smaller.

:::note Shielding
The way inner electrons reduce the pull of the nucleus on outer electrons, by repelling them. Electrons in the same shell shield each other only weakly.
:::

:::fig c9_shielding() | Lithium and fluorine, as simple shell models. Both have the same two inner electrons screening the nucleus. Fluorine has six more protons pulling on its outer shell, but its extra outer electrons barely screen each other, so its outer shell is held closer.

Going down a column is different. Sodium has one more full shell than lithium, and potassium one more than sodium. Each new shell sits farther out, and has more inner electrons screening it. Electric attraction gets weaker with distance, so the outer electron is held more loosely and the atom is bigger.

:::careful
> "More electrons always take up more room, so atoms must grow across a row."

Electrons are not marbles, each needing its own space. The size of an atom is set by how strongly its outer electrons are pulled in. Across a row, the pull wins: the protons added are not cancelled by the electrons added to the same shell.
:::

Turn the landscape and you can see the whole of this section at once. Each row after the first begins with a skyscraper on the left, an alkali metal, and the towers step down towards the right, with a few bumps along the way. Every step adds one proton, which pulls the same shells in a little tighter. Then the next row begins with a new, taller skyscraper, because it has a whole new shell. Press *Look along the rows* and all seven rows line up behind each other: the same downhill slope, again and again. (Row 1, with only hydrogen and helium, is too short to join in.) That repeating shape is what "periodic" means.

:::five Shielding
? What is it?
The way an atom's inner electrons stand between the nucleus and the outer electrons and cancel part of its pull, so an outer electron feels a much weaker tug than the full charge of the nucleus.
? Why is it called this?
Because the inner electrons act like a shield, or like a screen in front of a lamp; chemists also call it *screening*. These are plain everyday words, chosen because the picture works, not technical names with a hidden meaning.
? What problem did it solve?
Across a row, atoms gain protons and electrons, so you might expect them to grow; down a column they gain many more protons, so you might expect them to shrink. Measurements say the opposite both times. Shielding explains why.
? Why does it work?
Electrons repel each other. Inner electrons spend most of their time between the nucleus and the outer shell, so they push outwards on the outer electrons and cancel much of the protons' pull. Electrons in the same shell are no closer to the nucleus than each other, so they shield one another only a little.
? How does it help?
It explains the sizes of atoms, why ionisation energies rise across a row and fall down a column, and so why metals on the left give electrons away readily while non-metals on the right hold on to them.
?? How do we know?
Remove lithium's electrons one by one. The outer one, behind its two-electron shield, costs 520 kJ/mol; the next, with no shield in front of it, costs 7,298.
?? What it isn't
Not a fixed amount of pull shared out among the electrons. Every proton pulls on every electron; shielding is the push of the other electrons partly cancelling that pull.
?? Where does it stop working?
The inner electrons are not a solid wall: they move, and outer electrons spend some of their time close to the nucleus. Exact numbers need quantum mechanics, and the simple picture can't explain small details such as the dips at boron and oxygen.
:::

The shell model has done well here. But it is not the end of the story, and you can already see why in Chapter 1's graph of ionisation energies (Figure 1.7). Across row 2 the line climbs, but with two small dips, at boron and at oxygen. Shells alone cannot explain those. Nor can they explain the sharp coloured lines from Bunsen's flame: why should sodium glow at one exact yellow and not at every colour? In the 1920s, physicists such as Erwin Schrödinger and Wolfgang Pauli worked out a new kind of mechanics for electrons, quantum mechanics, which answered both questions. Electrons in an atom can have only certain energies, so when they drop from a higher energy to a lower one they give out light of only certain colours. And the electrons in a shell are arranged in smaller groups, described by ==orbitals==. An orbital is not a track: it describes the region where an electron is likely to be found, more like a fuzzy cloud with a particular shape. Some orbitals are round; others have two lobes, like a figure of eight. The dips appear where an electron starts a new kind of orbital (boron) or has to share an orbital with another electron (oxygen). You will meet orbitals properly when you study chemistry further; for now, it is enough to know that shells are a simplified first map.

:::note Orbital
A region around a nucleus where an electron is likely to be found, with a definite shape and energy. Shells are made up of orbitals.
:::

## 9.2 Why removing an electron always costs energy

How tightly does an atom hold its outermost electron? The answer is its ionisation energy, which you met in Chapter 1: the energy needed to pull one electron right away from an atom. For every element that has ever been measured, the answer is positive. No neutral atom gives up an electron for free. Even lithium and sodium, the easiest in the first 18 elements, need 520 and 496 kilojoules per mole of atoms.

The trends follow from section 9.1. Across a row, the outer electrons are pulled harder and sit closer, so they are harder to remove: lithium needs 520 kJ/mol, neon needs 2,081. Down a column, the outer electron is farther away and better screened, so it is easier to remove: lithium 520, sodium 496, potassium 419. The pattern in the reactivity series of Chapter 8 has the same roots: metals whose outer electrons are held loosely tend to be the ones that give electrons away readily, although in water other energy changes matter too. It is also why most of the metals in the firework colours come from the left-hand side of the table. A flame is hot enough to lift one of their outer electrons to a higher energy level without tearing it away, and the atom gives out its coloured light as the electron falls back. The colour is set by the size of that drop, which is different for every element.

**Predict first:** sodium in a flame glows yellow, and nothing else. If an electron can fall from many different heights, why does salt not give a whole rainbow? Make a guess, then press *Give it energy* a few times and watch where each colour lands.

:::fig i_spectrum() | An atom's energy levels drawn as a ladder. An electron lifted up by the heat of a flame falls back down, and gives out light whose colour is set by the size of the fall: a big fall gives blue or violet, a small fall red. Because only certain levels exist, only certain colours appear.
:::live spectrum {"atom":"sodium"}

Think of the levels as the rungs of a ladder with fixed, uneven gaps. An electron can stand on a rung, never between rungs, so it can only fall by the exact height of one gap or another. Each gap gives light of one exact colour. Sodium's ladder has one gap that gives far brighter visible light than any other, from its 3p level down to its 3s level, and that light is yellow, at 589 nanometres. Most of its other falls give infrared or ultraviolet light, which is real light that our eyes cannot see, and the few that are visible are very faint. That is why salt in a flame glows an almost pure yellow (so do the low-pressure sodium lamps once used on some roads).

Switch to hydrogen and you will see four visible lines, red, blue-green, blue and violet, all from falls that end on hydrogen's second rung. Switch to *A rainbow* and you will see the difference that Bunsen and Kirchhoff used. A hot solid, like a bulb's filament, gives every colour at once. A hot gas of separate atoms gives only its own few colours, as personal as a fingerprint.


Now try removing a *second* electron from lithium. Lithium's first electron was alone in the outer shell. Once it has gone, the next one must come from the inner shell, which is much closer to the nucleus and has almost nothing screening it. The energy needed jumps from 520 to 7,298 kJ/mol, about 14 times as much.

**Predict first.** Sodium's electrons are arranged 2, 8, 1. Sketch bars for the energy needed to remove its first three electrons, one after another. Where should the big jump come? Then check your idea against lithium (2, 1) below.

:::fig c9_successive_li() | The energy needed to take lithium's three electrons away one by one. Removing the first, outer electron is easy. The second and third come from the inner shell and cost far more. A big jump in this kind of graph marks the edge of a shell.

These ==successive ionisation energies== are some of the strongest evidence that shells are real. For any element, plot the energy needed to remove each electron in turn, and a big jump appears exactly where you run out of outer-shell electrons.

:::note Successive ionisation energies
The energies needed to remove an atom's electrons one after another. A large jump shows that the next electron is in an inner shell.
:::

:::evidence Knocking electrons out with light | from the 1960s
Ionisation energies are measured by shining very energetic light (ultraviolet or X-rays) on atoms of a gas and measuring how fast the electrons fly out. The light's energy, minus the electron's leftover energy of motion, is the energy it took to free that electron. This method, called photoelectron spectroscopy, shows separate groups of electrons with different energies inside every atom: direct evidence for shells, and for the orbitals inside them.
:::

:::five An element's colour signature (its spectrum)
? What is it?
The exact set of colours of light that an element gives out when it is heated, or soaks up when light shines through it. Spread out by a prism, it shows as sharp coloured lines that belong to that element alone.
? Why is it called this?
*Spectrum* is Latin for "an appearance" or "an image". Isaac Newton brought the word into the study of light in the 1670s for the band of colours from a prism. Bunsen and Kirchhoff named two new elements after their spectra: caesium, from the Latin for sky-blue, and rubidium, from the Latin for dark red.
? What problem did it solve?
How to tell which elements are present without weighing or reacting anything: in a flame, in a mineral, even in the Sun. Bunsen and Kirchhoff used it to discover two new elements in 1860 and 1861.
? Why does it work?
An electron in an atom can have only certain energies. Heat lifts an outer electron to a higher level; when it falls back, the atom gives out exactly the energy of that drop as light of one colour. Each element has its own ladder of levels, so its own set of colours.
? How does it help?
Firework makers choose metals for their colours, chemists detect tiny traces of elements, and astronomers read what stars are made of from their light.
?? How do we know?
The lines are the same every time, in every laboratory and in starlight: the two yellow lines that salt gives a gas flame sit exactly where two dark lines cross the Sun's spectrum.
?? What it isn't
Not the element burning or being used up. Its electrons are lifted and fall back, again and again, for as long as the atom stays hot.
?? Where does it stop working?
Shells alone can't predict the exact colours of most elements: that needed quantum mechanics in the 1920s. And molecules and solids give broad, blurred bands rather than sharp lines, which are much harder to read.
:::

:::careful
> "Sodium gives off energy when it loses its electron, because it wants a full shell."

Removing sodium's outer electron costs 496 kJ/mol; nothing about it gives energy out. Sodium does end up as Na⁺ in salt because of what happens afterwards: the oppositely charged ions pull together into a crystal, which gives out more energy than the ionisation cost (Chapter 2). The energy accounting is what decides, not any wish of the atom.
:::

It is the mirror image. Now the noble gases on the far right are the mountain peaks: helium highest of all, at about 2,372 kJ/mol. The alkali metals on the left are the deepest valleys: caesium needs only about 376. Big atoms hold their outer electron loosely, far from the nucleus and behind many shielding electrons; small atoms hold it tightly. Two different measurements, one pattern, and one reason behind both. That is why chemists trust the table.

:::five Ionisation energy
? What is it?
The energy needed to pull one electron right away from an atom, usually given per mole of atoms. It measures how tightly an atom holds its outermost electron.
? Why is it called this?
An atom that has lost or gained electrons is an *ion*, a word Michael Faraday introduced in 1834, on William Whewell's suggestion, from the Greek for "going", because ions travel towards an electrode. *Ionisation* is turning an atom into an ion, and the ionisation energy is what that costs.
? What problem did it solve?
It turned "how readily does this element give electrons away?" into a number you can measure, and it gave some of the strongest evidence that electrons are arranged in shells.
? Why does it work?
The outer electron is pulled in by the protons and pushed out by the other electrons. The closer it sits and the less it is shielded, the more energy it takes to tear it away.
? How does it help?
It predicts which atoms let electrons go (metals on the left, with low values) and which hold on (non-metals on the right), and a big jump in successive ionisation energies tells you how many outer electrons an atom has, as in the element with a cliff.
?? How do we know?
Shine very energetic light on atoms of a gas and measure how fast the electrons fly out. The light's energy, minus the electron's leftover energy of motion, is the ionisation energy (photoelectron spectroscopy).
?? What it isn't
Not energy given out when an atom gets rid of an electron. It is always a cost: no neutral atom gives up an electron for free, not even sodium.
?? Where does it stop working?
It describes a lone atom in a gas. For a metal in water or in a crystal, other energy changes join in, which is why the reactivity series is not simply the order of ionisation energies.
:::

:::home Be Mendeleev
Make your own "cards" the way Mendeleev did. On small pieces of paper, write the name and atomic number of the first twenty elements, from hydrogen (1) to calcium (20), and on each one note one fact you can look up: metal or non-metal, gas or solid at room temperature, and whether it forms ions with a charge of +1, +2, −1 or none. Shuffle them, then lay them out in order of atomic number, starting a new row each time you reach an element that behaves like one you have already placed. When your columns line up (lithium, sodium and potassium together; fluorine and chlorine together; helium, neon and argon together), you have rebuilt the shape of the periodic table from its chemistry alone.
:::

## The element with a cliff

:::case The element with a cliff
A sample of an unknown element X, from row 3 of the periodic table, is turned into a gas and its electrons are removed one at a time. The energies needed are:

| Electron removed | 1st | 2nd | 3rd | 4th |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Energy (kJ/mol) | 738 | 1,451 | 7,733 | 10,543 |

The suspects are sodium (2, 8, 1), magnesium (2, 8, 2) and aluminium (2, 8, 3).

1. Every value is positive.
2. The 2nd value is about twice the 1st. The 3rd is more than five times the 2nd.

Which element is X? Which clue decides it, and what does the other clue tell you?
???
X is magnesium. The cliff comes between the 2nd and 3rd electrons, so X has exactly two electrons in its outer shell before the inner shell is reached: that is 2, 8, 2. Sodium would show its cliff after the 1st electron; aluminium after the 3rd. Clue 1 tells you nothing about which element it is, but it is worth noticing: removing any electron, from any atom or ion, always takes energy.
:::

:::next
Right in the middle of row 2 sits carbon: four outer electrons, held neither very loosely nor very tightly, so that it neither gives them away nor takes extra ones easily. Instead, carbon shares. It shares with hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen, and above all with other carbon atoms, building chains, rings and frameworks of almost endless variety. Chapter 10 is about the chemistry that carbon makes possible, including the chemistry of life.
:::
