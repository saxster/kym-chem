:::scene Bologna, Italy, the 1780s

Luigi Galvani was a doctor who taught anatomy at the University of Bologna, a careful, devout man more at home with nerves and muscles than with machines. In his laboratory, often working alongside his wife Lucia, he was dissecting frogs. One day, so the story goes, a frog's leg twitched when a metal scalpel touched a nerve while an electrical machine was sparking nearby. Galvani spent years on it, and he found that a leg would twitch even with no machine at all, if it was touched by two *different* metals at once. He published in 1791 and concluded that living things make their own "animal electricity".

A few hundred kilometres away in Pavia, the physicist Alessandro Volta read Galvani's work and disagreed. Volta was a restless experimenter who had already discovered methane by poking at the mud of marshes and catching the bubbles, and he trusted instruments more than theories. The electricity, he thought, did not come from the frog. It came from the two metals; the wet frog leg was only a damp path between them. To prove it he left out the frog altogether. He piled up discs of zinc and copper, with cardboard soaked in salt water between each pair, and in 1800 he announced the result: a steady flow of electricity from a stack of metal and wet card. It was the first battery, and in 1801 he was demonstrating it to Napoleon in Paris.

Neither man knew what was really moving. That took another century, and the discovery of the electron. In Chapter 7, acids and bases passed protons from one particle to another. This chapter is about the second great family of reactions, in which the thing passed along is an electron. These reactions rust iron, tarnish silver, burn fuel and release the energy from your food. And because electrons moving through a wire *are* an electric current, they are how every battery works, from Volta's pile to the one in a phone.

## 8.1 When metals and ions trade electrons

Here is a piece of chemistry that looks like a magic trick. Take a strip of dull grey zinc and lower it into a beaker of clear blue copper(II) sulfate solution. Within minutes the grey strip starts to blush: a crumbly reddish-brown coat of copper grows over it, as if the metal were turning into a different one before your eyes. The blue colour of the liquid slowly fades. The beaker gets slightly warm. Test the liquid afterwards and you find zinc dissolved in it that was not there before.

**Predict first.** Where did that copper come from? (a) Some of the zinc turned into copper. (b) It came out of the air. (c) It was in the blue liquid all along. Choose before you read on, and notice the clue you have been given: the blue colour faded.

Nothing magic has happened, and nothing has turned into anything else. The answer is (c). The particle story is a swap. The blue colour comes from copper ions, Cu²⁺, in the water. Each Cu²⁺ ion has 29 protons and only 27 electrons. When one bumps into the surface of the zinc, a zinc atom (30 protons, 30 electrons) hands over two electrons. The zinc atom becomes a Zn²⁺ ion (30 protons, 28 electrons) and drifts off into the water. The copper ion becomes a neutral copper atom (29 protons, 29 electrons) and stays stuck to the strip as metal.

:::eq
Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s)
:::

The letters in brackets say what state each thing is in: (s) for solid, (aq) for dissolved in water, from *aqua*.

:::fig c8_zn_cu() | Zinc meets copper ions. Before: zinc atoms in the strip, Cu²⁺ ions in the solution. Each zinc atom that reacts passes two electrons to one copper ion. After: zinc has gone into solution as Zn²⁺, and copper atoms sit on the strip as metal. Sulfate ions take no part.
:::live zncu {}

The words chemists use for this have a history. In Lavoisier's day, to "oxidise" something meant to combine it with oxygen, as iron does when it rusts. Once the electron was discovered in 1897, chemists saw that what really matters in rusting, burning and this zinc-and-copper swap is the same thing: electrons leaving one substance for another. So the words were widened. Losing electrons is now called ==oxidation==. Gaining electrons is called ==reduction==. They always happen together, because an electron that leaves one particle must go to another; there are no spare electrons floating loose in the water. A reaction like this is a ==redox reaction==: *red*uction plus *ox*idation. A memory trick many chemists use is OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain.

If you meet oxidation described as *gaining oxygen*, that is the same event seen from outside: when magnesium burns, 2 Mg + O₂ → 2 MgO, each magnesium atom gains oxygen *by* handing it two electrons; the electron version is simply wider, because it also covers swaps like zinc and copper, where no oxygen takes part at all.

:::note Oxidation
Losing electrons. Zinc is oxidised when Zn becomes Zn²⁺.
:::

:::note Reduction
Gaining electrons. Copper ions are reduced when Cu²⁺ becomes Cu.
:::

:::note Redox reaction
A reaction in which electrons pass from one substance to another. Oxidation and reduction always happen together.
:::

:::story redox

Notice what stays the same. Every nucleus is untouched: zinc stays zinc and copper stays copper, because their proton numbers never change. The alchemists who dreamed of turning cheap metals into precious ones would have been delighted by the copper coat on the zinc, and disappointed to learn that the copper was in the beaker all along. The beaker also contains sulfate ions, SO₄²⁻, but they come out exactly as they went in. Ions that are present but take no part are called ==spectator ions==, and chemists usually leave them out of the equation.

:::note Spectator ions
Ions that are present in a reacting mixture but come out unchanged, like the sulfate ions in copper sulfate solution.
:::

:::careful
> "The zinc gives the copper two protons, and that turns it into copper."

Chemical reactions never move protons out of a nucleus. Copper ions are already copper (29 protons); they only lack two electrons. What moves is electrons, from the outside of the zinc atoms to the copper ions. Count the protons before and after and you will find 30 and 29 both times.
:::

:::five Redox
? What is it?
A reaction in which electrons pass from one substance to another. The one that loses electrons is *oxidised*; the one that gains them is *reduced*. The two always happen together.
? Why is it called this?
*Red*uction plus *ox*idation. "Oxidation" comes from oxygen, a name Lavoisier made up in 1777 from Greek words meaning "acid-maker" (he wrongly thought every acid contained it). "Reduction" is from the Latin for "leading back": heating an ore led the metal back out of it, and the ore got lighter as oxygen left as a gas. Both names are older than the electron, so they mislead a little: oxidation needs no oxygen at all, as zinc and copper show.
? What problem did it solve?
Rusting, burning, tarnishing, one metal coating another, and batteries all looked like separate puzzles. Once the electron was found in 1897, chemists saw they are one event: electrons moving from one substance to another.
? Why does it work?
When an exchange of electrons gives out energy overall, as zinc meeting copper ions does, the electrons move in that direction and not the other. Every electron one particle loses, another must gain, because there are no spare electrons floating loose in the water.
? How does it help?
You can predict which metal will coat which (the reactivity series), balance equations by counting electrons as well as atoms, and understand batteries, rust and how metals are got out of their ores.
?? How do we know?
Watch and weigh: the zinc wastes away, copper appears and the blue of the copper ions fades. Put the two halves in separate beakers joined by a wire and a salt bridge, and a meter shows the electrons flowing through it, exactly as the half-equations say.
?? What it isn't
Not one element turning into another. Every nucleus is untouched: the copper was in the liquid all along as ions, and only electrons moved.
?? Where does it stop working?
In many reactions electrons are shared rather than handed over completely, as in the covalent bonds of Chapter 2. Chemists still keep redox accounts there, using "oxidation numbers", but then it is careful bookkeeping, not a picture of whole electrons jumping across.
:::

## 8.2 Balancing electrons as well as atoms

:::scene The Royal Institution, London, 1833

Michael Faraday was a blacksmith's son who left school early and became a bookbinder's apprentice, reading the science books he was binding. In 1812 a customer gave him tickets to hear Humphry Davy lecture at the Royal Institution. Faraday wrote up his notes, bound them beautifully, sent them to Davy, and soon afterwards got a job as his assistant, washing bottles. By the 1830s he was the most famous experimenter in Britain, and he wanted to know how much chemistry a given amount of electricity could do. He passed electric currents through solutions of different metals' salts and weighed the metal that formed on the plates.

He found a rule so clean that it surprised everyone. Before you read it, **predict**: suppose a certain amount of electricity deposits 108 grams of silver. Pass exactly the same amount through a copper solution instead. Will you get more copper, the same, or less?

The answer: the same amount of electricity always produced the same number of "chemical portions" (what he called equivalents) of each substance, but not the same mass, and not the same number of atoms for every metal. In today's numbers, the charge that deposits about 108 grams of silver deposits only about 32 grams of copper. That is half as many copper atoms as silver atoms. Faraday never knew why. We do: each copper ion needs two electrons to become an atom, while each silver ion needs only one. Faraday's balance was counting electrons sixty years before anyone discovered them.

Here is the same idea in a beaker. Put a ribbon of magnesium into a solution of silver nitrate, and needles of shiny silver crystals grow on it while the magnesium dissolves. A magnesium atom loses two electrons to become Mg²⁺. But a silver ion, Ag⁺, has a charge of only +1, so it can take only one electron to become a silver atom. Where does the second electron go?

To a second silver ion. For every magnesium atom that reacts, two silver atoms appear:

:::eq
Mg(s) + 2 Ag⁺(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + 2 Ag(s)
:::

A handy way to see this is to split the reaction into two ==half-equations==, one for each side of the swap, with the electrons written in:

:::work
oxidation: Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2 e⁻
reduction: Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag (needed twice: 2 Ag⁺ + 2 e⁻ → 2 Ag)
electrons given = 2, electrons taken = 2, so they cancel when you add the two halves
:::

:::note Half-equation
One half of a redox reaction written on its own, showing the electrons lost (oxidation) or gained (reduction).
:::

The rule is the one from the front of the book, *count everything*, with one extra thing to count. A balanced redox equation must have the same number of atoms of each element on both sides **and** the same total electric charge on both sides. Check it: on the left, Mg has charge 0 and two Ag⁺ ions have +2; on the right, Mg²⁺ has +2 and two silver atoms have 0. Both sides total +2.

:::fig c8_electron_balance() | Matching electrons given with electrons taken. One magnesium atom gives two electrons, so it needs two silver ions. Two aluminium atoms give six electrons, which three copper ions can take. The smallest numbers that make the electrons match are the numbers in the equation.

Try writing it the lazy way, Mg + Ag⁺ → Mg²⁺ + Ag. The atoms balance, one of each. But the charge does not: +1 on the left, +2 on the right. One electron has appeared from nowhere, and electrons, like atoms, are never created or destroyed in a chemical reaction.

The same idea works for harder pairs. Aluminium forms Al³⁺ (losing three electrons), and each copper ion takes two. The smallest number that both 3 and 2 go into is 6, so two aluminium atoms give six electrons to three copper ions:

:::eq
2 Al(s) + 3 Cu²⁺(aq) → 2 Al³⁺(aq) + 3 Cu(s)
:::

:::careful
> "One magnesium atom splits into two silver atoms."

Atoms of one element never turn into another in a chemical reaction. Each silver atom was a silver ion in the solution all along; it was waiting for one electron. The magnesium atom supplies two electrons, and so two silver ions become silver metal. (Silver atoms are also more than four times heavier than magnesium atoms, so a magnesium atom could not possibly "split" into two of them.)
:::

## 8.3 Why doesn't copper dissolve in zinc sulfate?

Alchemists loved to grow glittering crystal "trees" of silver in their flasks, and gave them names like the Tree of Diana. You can grow one with no mystery at all: hang a coil of copper wire in colourless silver nitrate solution, and over an hour a sparkling tree of silver crystals grows on it while the liquid slowly turns blue. Copper is giving electrons to silver ions, and going into solution as blue Cu²⁺.

So try the obvious next experiment. Zinc dropped into copper ions gets coated in copper. What about a piece of copper dropped into colourless zinc sulfate solution? You can write the equation perfectly well:

:::eq
Cu(s) + Zn²⁺(aq) → Cu²⁺(aq) + Zn(s)
:::

It balances for atoms (one copper, one zinc on each side) and for charge (+2 on each side). And yet, if you try it, nothing happens. Leave the copper there for a day, a week, a month: it stays shiny and the liquid stays colourless. Put the silver from your tree into copper sulfate, and nothing happens either.

This is the lesson of [section 4.4](#s4-4) again, now with electrons: a balanced equation tells you what *would* be conserved if a reaction happened. It does not tell you that the reaction happens. Electron transfer has a direction. Electrons move readily from zinc to copper ions, but not from copper to zinc ions, like water that runs down a staircase but never climbs back up on its own. The reason is the total energy change, which includes pulling atoms out of the solid metal and water molecules gathering round the new ions, not just how tightly a lone atom holds its outer electrons. The forward reaction gives out energy (that is why the beaker warmed up), and running it backwards would need that energy to be put back in.

By testing metals against each other's ions, every pairing, chemists in the 1800s found one consistent order, with zinc above copper above silver. They lined the metals up in order of how readily they give away electrons, and called it the ==reactivity series==. A metal higher in the series pushes the ions of a lower metal out of solution and coats itself with that metal; a lower metal does nothing to the ions of a higher one. The same order shows up again as the voltage when the metals are made into cells, as you will see next.

:::note Reactivity series
A list of metals in order of how readily they give away electrons. A metal will push a lower metal's ions out of solution, but not a higher one's.
:::

:::fig c8_series() | Nine experiments, three metals, three solutions. A reaction happens only when a metal meets the ions of a metal below it in the series. Putting a metal into its own ions changes nothing.

:::careful
> "Copper doesn't react with zinc sulfate because the equation can't be balanced."

It balances perfectly, for atoms and for charge. The reason is energy, not bookkeeping: electrons do not pass from copper to zinc ions on their own, because that would be an uphill move. Balance is necessary for any real reaction, but it never proves one will happen.
:::

## 8.4 The complete galvanic circuit

:::scene King's College London, 1836

Volta's pile made electricity, but it was a frustrating thing to use. Within minutes its current faded, because bubbles of hydrogen gas collected on the metal plates and smothered the reaction. John Frederic Daniell, the first professor of chemistry at the new King's College, was a practical man who had already invented an instrument for measuring the moisture in the air. He wanted a battery that would keep going.

Think about what happens when zinc sits in copper sulfate. The electrons jump straight from zinc atoms to copper ions at the surface of the strip. The energy comes out as a little warmth and then it is gone. Daniell's idea was to make those same electrons travel somewhere useful on the way.

He separated the two halves of the reaction. A zinc plate stands in a beaker of zinc sulfate; a copper plate stands in a beaker of copper sulfate. Now the zinc atoms cannot reach any copper ions. Connect the two plates with a wire, though, and the electrons have a road. Zinc atoms give up electrons, which travel through the wire to the copper plate, where copper ions from the solution collect them and become copper metal. Electrons flowing through a wire are an electric current, so a bulb or a small motor in the wire will run. A cell that turns a chemical reaction into electricity like this is called a ==galvanic cell==, after Galvani and his frogs.

:::note Galvanic cell
Two halves of a redox reaction in separate containers, joined by a wire and a salt bridge, so that the electrons travel through the wire as an electric current.
:::

:::figwide c8_daniell() | A Daniell cell. Electrons leave the zinc (where oxidation happens) and travel through the wire to the copper (where reduction happens). In the salt bridge, ions move to keep both beakers electrically balanced: negative ions towards the zinc side, positive ions towards the copper side. With standard solutions the cell gives 1.10 volts.
:::live daniell {}

There is one more part, and it is easy to overlook. As zinc dissolves, the left beaker fills up with extra Zn²⁺ ions, more positive charge than negative. As copper plates out, the right beaker loses Cu²⁺ ions and is left with extra negative sulfate ions. Within a fraction of a second, the build-up of charge would stop the current completely. The fix is a ==salt bridge==: a tube or a strip of paper soaked in a salt solution, such as potassium nitrate, dipped into both beakers. (Daniell's first cells used a barrier of animal membrane, and later ones a porous clay pot, which do the same job.) Its ions drift to cancel the build-up: nitrate ions (negative) move towards the zinc beaker, potassium ions (positive) towards the copper beaker. Electrons carry the current through the wire; ions carry it through the liquids. Only together do they make a complete loop.

:::note Salt bridge
A path of salt solution between the two beakers of a cell. Ions move along it to keep each beaker electrically neutral, completing the circuit.
:::

The two plates have names. The ==anode== is where oxidation happens (here, zinc), and the ==cathode== is where reduction happens (here, copper). As the cell runs, the zinc plate gets thinner and the copper plate gets thicker. In a cell like this one, the anode is the *negative* terminal, which surprises many people: isn't the zinc losing electrons? Each zinc atom that reacts leaves as a Zn²⁺ ion but leaves its two electrons behind in the metal. Electrons collect in the zinc plate faster than anywhere else, which is exactly why they pour out of it along the wire.

:::note Anode and cathode
The anode is the electrode where oxidation happens; the cathode is where reduction happens. In a cell that makes electricity, the anode is the negative terminal.
:::

Daniell's two-liquid design gave a steady current for hours, and it went to work at once: Daniell cells powered many of the early electric telegraphs of the 1840s and 1850s, carrying messages along wires across countries. Measured carefully, a Daniell cell with standard solutions gives 1.10 volts: the size of the push the zinc-to-copper electron transfer can give.

## 8.5 Driving the reverse with electrical work

Can you run a cell backwards? Yes, if you push. Connect a power supply that pushes harder than the cell does (opposing the standard cell voltage, with sufficient additional voltage under the actual conditions; the ideal reversible threshold is 1.10 V under standard conditions) and you can force electrons onto zinc ions to make zinc metal again, and pull them off copper. Using electricity to drive a reaction that would not go on its own is called ==electrolysis==. It was electrolysis that made Humphry Davy famous. Davy was a woodcarver's son from Penzance in Cornwall who had taught himself chemistry, tried breathing laughing gas in the name of science, and become the most dazzling lecturer in London. In 1807, using a giant version of Volta's pile, he passed a current through melted potash and watched tiny globules of a new metal burst out and catch fire: potassium. Days later he made sodium the same way. Both had been locked in their compounds so tightly that no ordinary chemistry could free them.

:::story davy
 And recharging a phone battery is exactly this idea: the charger pushes the battery's reaction backwards, storing energy that the battery gives out again later. (The chemistry inside a phone is different from Daniell's cell, and Chapter 11 looks at it.)

:::note Electrolysis
Using electricity from outside to drive a redox reaction that would not happen by itself.
:::

:::five Electrolysis
? What is it?
Using electricity from outside to drive a chemical reaction that would not happen on its own: pushing electrons where they would not go by themselves.
? Why is it called this?
Michael Faraday introduced the word in 1834, prompted by the scholar William Whewell. It means "loosening by electricity", from the Greek *lysis*, a loosening, because the current pulls a compound apart. The same pair gave us *anode* and *cathode* (Greek for "way up" and "way down") and *ion*, "one that goes".
? What problem did it solve?
Some elements are locked into their compounds so tightly that no ordinary reaction can free them. Electrolysis prised them out: Humphry Davy made potassium and sodium this way in 1807, and it is still how aluminium, sodium and chlorine are made today.
? Why does it work?
A power supply pushes electrons onto one electrode and pulls them off the other, harder than the reaction can push back. Ions drift to the electrode of opposite charge, where they gain or lose electrons and become atoms or molecules again.
? How does it help?
It makes metals and gases we could not otherwise get, coats objects with a thin layer of metal (electroplating), and it is exactly what a charger does to a rechargeable battery.
?? How do we know?
Faraday weighed what the current deposited. In today's numbers, the same amount of electricity that deposits about 108 g of silver deposits only about 32 g of copper: half as many atoms, because each copper ion needs two electrons and each silver ion one.
?? What it isn't
Not the current smashing the compound like a hammer. It delivers electrons one at a time, and the energy goes into moving them uphill.
?? Where does it stop working?
It costs a great deal of energy: making one kilogram of aluminium takes about 15 units (kilowatt-hours) of electricity, as much as a ceiling fan uses running day and night for more than a week. And what forms at each electrode depends on everything in the beaker; in salty water you get hydrogen, not sodium, because water molecules take the electrons more easily.
:::

:::careful
> "The electrons travel through the salt bridge to get back to the zinc."

Free electrons do not travel through water or salt solution. In the wire and the metal plates, current is carried by electrons. In the solutions and the salt bridge, it is carried by ions drifting in opposite directions. The loop is complete, but it is made of two different kinds of traffic.
:::

:::home A lemon battery (with an adult)
You can repeat Volta's argument with Galvani on the kitchen table. You need three lemons, four pieces of thick copper wire (stripped electrical wire is fine; Indian coins are not copper), three galvanised (zinc-coated) iron nails or screws, four short wires with crocodile clips, and a small red LED. If anyone has a cheap multimeter, borrow it too. Roll each lemon on the table to loosen the juice inside.

**Predict first.** Push *two copper pieces* into one lemon, a few centimetres apart, and connect them to the LED or the meter. Will anything happen? Then swap one copper piece for the zinc-coated nail. What changes?

With two coppers, nothing worth measuring (a meter reads close to zero): the same metal on both sides gives no push, however juicy the lemon. With copper and zinc, the meter shows a bit under one volt. The lemon is only the damp path, as Volta said of the frog; the push comes from the two *different* metals. One lemon is not quite enough for most LEDs, so make two or three lemon cells and join them in a chain, the copper of one to the zinc of the next, and look closely in a dim room (if the LED stays dark, turn it round: it only works one way). Plain iron nails work too, but you need five or six lemons, because iron sits lower than zinc in the reactivity series and gives a smaller push.

Now a harder question. Leave the LED glowing for an hour. What has been used up: the electrons, the lemon, or the zinc? Throw the lemons away afterwards rather than eating them, because some zinc will have dissolved into the juice.
:::

:::careful
> "A battery is full of electrons, and it goes flat when they are used up."

Electrons are never used up. Every electron that leaves the zinc goes round the wire, through the LED, and into the copper; the same number come out of one end as go in the other. What gets used up in the lemon is zinc atoms, which go into the juice as Zn²⁺ ions, and hydrogen ions in the juice, which take the electrons at the copper and become hydrogen gas. A battery is a store of *chemicals* that will react as soon as they are given a path; the electrons are only the go-between. When the chemicals run out, the push stops.
:::

:::five The battery
? What does it do?
A battery is a store of chemicals that are ready to react, arranged so that the electrons they exchange must travel through a wire, doing useful work on the way.
? Why is it called this?
Around 1749 Benjamin Franklin called a row of linked electrical jars a "battery", probably because it looked like a battery of cannon lined up to fire together. The word fitted Volta's pile of 1800, a stack of many cells. A single cell like Daniell's is called *galvanic*, after Galvani and his frogs, even though Volta showed Galvani was wrong about where the electricity came from.
? What problem did it solve?
Before 1800, electricity came only in sudden sparks from friction machines and charged jars. Volta's pile gave the first steady current, and Daniell's cell of 1836 kept it steady for hours, enough to run the electric telegraph.
? Why does it work?
Two different materials pass electrons on with different ease. Keep them apart, give the electrons a wire and the ions a salt bridge, and the electrons run downhill through the wire, from the material that lets them go to the one that takes them.
? How does it help?
Every torch, phone and electric car runs on one. And a cell's voltage measures how hard one metal pushes electrons onto another: the reactivity series, read off a meter.
?? How do we know?
Two copper pieces in a lemon give almost no voltage; copper and zinc give nearly a volt. The push comes from the difference between two materials, not from the lemon, just as Volta argued against Galvani.
?? What it isn't
Not a tank of electrons that empties. Every electron that leaves goes round the circuit and back in; it is the chemicals, such as zinc atoms and hydrogen ions, that run out.
?? Where does it stop working?
When a reacting chemical runs out, the push stops. Most cells can't be reversed. Rechargeable ones can be pushed backwards by a charger, but even they slowly wear out as side reactions trap some of their chemicals.
:::

## The cell that would not light

:::case The cell that would not light
Riya builds a cell from a magnesium strip in magnesium sulfate solution and a silver strip in silver nitrate solution, in two separate beakers. She joins the strips with wires through a small bulb and an ammeter (a meter that measures current). To keep her solutions pure, she does not join the beakers with anything else.

1. The bulb never lights. The ammeter reads 0 the whole time.
2. The metal strips are clean and the solutions are fresh. The wires and the bulb work when tested with a torch battery.
3. Magnesium is far above silver in the reactivity series: a magnesium ribbon dropped straight into silver nitrate grows silver crystals within a minute or so.
4. When she dips a strip of filter paper soaked in potassium nitrate solution into both beakers, the bulb glows at once. After an hour, she finds that 0.020 mol of magnesium has dissolved.

Why did nothing happen at first? And how many moles of silver should have formed on the silver strip by the end of the hour?

At first the circuit was not a complete loop. Electrons could travel through the wire, but nothing could carry charge between the two beakers. If even a few magnesium atoms had given up electrons, the magnesium beaker would have gained positive charge and the silver beaker would have been left with extra negative nitrate ions, and that imbalance would instantly have stopped any more electrons from moving. In practice, no measurable current flows at all. Clue 3 shows that the reaction itself is ready to go; clue 2 rules out a broken bulb; clue 4 shows the missing piece was the salt bridge.

Silver: each magnesium atom gives 2 electrons and each silver ion takes 1 (Mg + 2 Ag⁺ → Mg²⁺ + 2 Ag), so 0.020 mol of magnesium makes 2 × 0.020 = 0.040 mol of silver, about 4.3 g.
