:::scene London, 1807

We left Chapter 1 in the basement of the Royal Institution, with Humphry Davy dancing round his laboratory over a few silvery globules of potassium. A few days later he made sodium the same way: a metal so soft you can cut it with a knife, which tarnishes in seconds in air and skates fizzing across water. A few years after that, in 1810, Davy showed that a choking yellow-green gas, first made by the Swedish apothecary Carl Wilhelm Scheele in 1774, was an element too. He named it chlorine, from the Greek for "pale green".

Put those two together and something extraordinary happens. Sodium burns in chlorine with a fierce yellow flame, and what is left behind is a white powder you could sprinkle on your chips: table salt. Two dangerous elements become something you eat every day. That is a clue that when atoms join, they become something new.

Look around the room you are in. The walls, the air, a glass of water, the screen or page in front of you: almost nothing is made of single, separate atoms. Atoms are nearly always attached to other atoms. Sometimes a few of them join into a small group; sometimes trillions lock together into a crystal. This chapter is about what holds them together, and why the same handful of elements can make substances as different as water, salt, pencil lead and diamond.

## 2.1 Element, compound or mixture?

:::scene Spain, the 1790s

In the 1790s a French chemist called Joseph Proust was working in Spain, where the king paid for a fine laboratory. Proust analysed copper carbonate, a green substance, again and again: some he made himself, some was dug out of the ground. However it had been made, it contained the same elements in exactly the same proportions by mass. He argued that every pure compound has a fixed recipe. The most famous chemist in France, Claude-Louis Berthollet, disagreed, and for several years the two argued politely in print. Proust won. His idea, now called the law of definite proportions, became one of the main clues that matter is made of atoms that join in whole-number ratios.

Chemists now sort every material into three kinds. An ==element== you met in Chapter 1: it contains only one kind of atom. Oxygen gas is an element, even though its atoms travel in pairs, O₂, because both atoms in each pair are oxygen.

A ==compound== contains two or more elements joined together, always in the same fixed proportions, just as Proust found. Water is a compound: every water molecule has exactly two hydrogen atoms joined to one oxygen atom. Water from the Arabian Sea, from a monsoon puddle or from a laboratory bottle has the same 2 : 1 ratio, and that is why it can be given one formula, H₂O.

:::note Compound
A substance made of two or more elements joined together in a fixed ratio, such as water, H₂O.
:::

A ==mixture== contains two or more substances that are simply mingled, not joined. Salt water is a mixture: you can stir in a little salt or a lot, and you can get the salt back by letting the water evaporate. A mixture has no single formula, because its proportions can vary.

:::note Mixture
Two or more substances mingled together but not chemically joined. Their proportions can vary, and they can usually be separated by physical means such as evaporating or filtering.
:::

:::fig c2_element_compound_mixture() | Four samples, drawn as particle models. Hydrogen and oxygen are elements: each holds only one kind of atom. Water is a compound: every molecule has the same 2 : 1 ratio of hydrogen to oxygen. A mixture of hydrogen and oxygen holds two separate substances in whatever proportions you put in.

Here is a subtle point. A mixture of hydrogen and oxygen gas contains exactly the same kinds of atoms as water. But it is not water, and it behaves completely differently: it is an invisible gas, and with a spark it explodes. What matters is not just which atoms are present, but how they are joined.

The reverse surprise is just as striking: a compound can be utterly unlike the elements it is made from. Sugar is a sweet white powder, yet it is made of carbon (black, like soot), hydrogen and oxygen (two invisible gases).

:::five Compound
? What is it?
A substance made of two or more elements joined together in a fixed ratio. Water, H₂O, is always two hydrogen atoms to one oxygen atom.
? Why is it called this?
From Latin *componere*, "to put together": a compound is elements put together.
? What problem did it solve?
Chemists argued over whether a substance could hold its elements in any proportions. Proust showed that a pure compound always has the same recipe, a key clue that atoms join in whole numbers.
? Why does it work?
Atoms join in particular whole-number patterns, so every molecule or crystal of the compound has the same ratio of atoms.
? How does it help?
A compound can be given one formula and has fixed properties, so you can predict what a recipe will make, every time.
?? How do we know?
Proust analysed copper carbonate again and again in the 1790s. Made in the lab or dug from the ground, it held the same elements in exactly the same proportions by mass.
?? What it isn't
Not a mixture. A mixture of hydrogen and oxygen gas contains the same atoms as water, but it is not water: it explodes with a spark.
?? Where does it stop working?
Some solid compounds, such as certain minerals and metal oxides, have slightly variable ratios. Chemists call them berthollides, after Proust's rival, who was partly right after all.

## 2.2 How do charges interact?

Remember the balloon that lifted pepper off a plate in Chapter 1? Everything that holds atoms together comes down to the same rule, one of the four ideas at the start of the book: ==opposite charges attract, and like charges repel==. A proton and an electron pull towards each other. Two electrons push each other apart. So do two protons.

:::scene Paris, 1785

How strong is the pull? The first person to measure it was Charles-Augustin de Coulomb, a French army engineer who had spent years building a fort on the Caribbean island of Martinique before turning to physics. He hung a light rod from a very thin wire, with a small charged ball on one end. When he brought another charged ball near it, the rod turned and twisted the wire. The more the wire twisted, the bigger the force, so the twist was a way of weighing a force too small to feel.

By changing the distance between the balls, Coulomb found the rule, now called Coulomb's law. The pull depends on two things: how big the charges are, and how far apart they are. Bigger charges pull harder. And the force gets weaker very quickly with distance. If you double the distance between two charges, the force does not halve. It drops to a quarter. Triple the distance, and it drops to a ninth. Scientists call this an ==inverse-square law==.

:::note Inverse-square law
A rule where a force gets weaker with the square of the distance: twice as far, one quarter of the force; three times as far, one ninth.
:::

:::fig c2_coulomb() | Two pairs of opposite charges. Moving them twice as far apart cuts the attraction to one quarter. Like charges (bottom) push apart instead of pulling together.

Coulomb's law is about tiny charged points, and atoms are not points. When two atoms come very close, their electron clouds start to overlap and a powerful push-back sets in, which is why atoms do not collapse into each other. To describe that properly you need quantum physics. But almost every attraction in this book, between ions, between atoms in a molecule, between neighbouring molecules, is at heart this electric pull.

:::careful
> "If you double the distance, the force halves."

It is much more dramatic than that. Twice the distance means one quarter of the force, because the distance is squared: 2 × 2 = 4. This is why electric forces between atoms matter enormously at close range and fade quickly further away.
:::

:::home See why the distance is squared
Cut a small square hole, about 1 cm across, in a piece of card. In a dark room, hold the torch of a phone (its light is a tiny point, which is what you want) 5 cm behind the hole, and shine it onto a sheet of squared paper 10 cm from the light. Measure every distance from the light itself, not from the card. Count how many squares the patch of light covers. Now move the paper to 20 cm from the light, keeping the card where it was. **Predict first:** twice the distance, so twice as many squares? Count them: about four times as many, because the patch has grown twice as wide *and* twice as tall. The same light is spread over four times the area, so each square gets a quarter as much. Electric pull spreads out from a charge in a similar way, which is why it falls off with the square of the distance.
:::

## 2.3 How can atoms share electrons?

:::scene Berkeley, California, 1916

Salt's ions hold together because they have opposite charges. But what about a hydrogen molecule, H₂, where two identical atoms cling together and neither has a charge? This puzzled chemists until the American chemist Gilbert Newton Lewis, head of chemistry at the University of California in Berkeley, suggested a simple answer in 1916: the two atoms *share* a pair of electrons. Lewis had been doodling atoms as little cubes with electrons at the corners in his notebooks since 1902. His dots-for-electrons drawings are still used in every chemistry class in the world.

:::story bonds

Picture two hydrogen atoms floating towards each other. Each has one proton and one electron. As they get close, each electron starts to feel the pull of *both* protons. The electrons spend much of their time in the space between the two nuclei, and both nuclei are attracted to that shared negative region. This joins the atoms into a molecule of hydrogen, H₂. A link like this, made by shared electrons, is called a ==covalent bond==.

:::note Covalent bond
A link between two atoms that share electrons. Both nuclei are attracted to the shared electrons between them.
:::

A bond is not a stick or a hook. It is a balance. Pull the two nuclei closer, and the two positive nuclei repel each other more strongly. Pull them further apart, and they lose the shared attraction. In between there is a best distance, where the energy is lowest. For H₂, that distance is 74 picometres (0.074 nanometres).

**Predict first.** Two hydrogen atoms drift towards each other. Will they keep getting closer until they touch, stop somewhere, or bounce away? Make your guess, then move them with the slider below.

:::fig c2_bond_energy() | Energy of two hydrogen atoms as they move closer together. Far apart, nothing happens. Closer in, sharing electrons lowers the energy. Too close, the nuclei repel. The bond sits at the bottom of the dip, 74 pm apart. (Shape of curve drawn schematically; 74 pm is a reference equilibrium distance; the 436 kJ/mol scale is the rounded 298 K bond dissociation enthalpy, not the measured electronic well depth De.)
:::live bondwell {}

Because bonded atoms sit in an energy dip, pulling them apart always takes energy. To split one mole of H₂ molecules into separate hydrogen atoms takes 436 kilojoules: about enough to bring a litre of water from room temperature to the boil, and then some. This is worth fixing firmly in your head, because everyday life suggests the opposite (snap a stick, and it seems to let something out): **breaking a bond never gives out energy; it always costs energy.** Energy comes out when bonds *form*, as the atoms drop into the dip.

Try *Let them come together* in the figure. The atoms roll down into the dip and rock back and forth, and they only settle at the bottom if the extra energy is carried away, as heat passed to other molecules. That is the whole secret of a bond: it is the bottom of an energy valley. Nothing holds the atoms with a hook. They stay because climbing out would cost 436 kJ for every mole, and nobody is paying.

How can anyone measure a distance of 74 picometres? With light. Molecules vibrate and spin, and they absorb and give out light only at particular colours that match those movements. From those colours, physicists work out how far apart the nuclei are and how much energy it takes to pull them apart. The same measurements have been made for thousands of other bonds, and X-ray maps of crystals show that, in many of them, electrons really do pile up in the regions between bonded atoms, just where Lewis drew his shared pairs.

:::careful
> "Hydrogen atoms bond because each one wants two electrons to be stable."

Atoms do not want anything. The bond forms because the arrangement with shared electrons has lower energy than two separate atoms: each electron is attracted to two protons instead of one. Energy has to be put in to break that arrangement. That is the whole story, without any wishing.
:::

:::five Covalent bond
? What is it?
A link between two atoms that share a pair of electrons. Both nuclei are pulled towards the shared electrons between them, and that holds the atoms together.
? Why is it called this?
"Co-" means together, and "valence" is chemists' word for an atom's power to combine, from Latin *valentia*, strength. The American chemist Irving Langmuir introduced "covalence" in 1919 for electrons shared between atoms.
? What problem did it solve?
Ions explained salt, but not molecules such as H₂, where two identical atoms with no charge cling together. Lewis's shared pair of 1916 explained how.
? Why does it work?
Each shared electron is attracted by two nuclei instead of one, which lowers the energy. Too close and the nuclei repel; too far and the sharing is lost. The bond sits at the distance where the energy is lowest.
? How does it help?
It explains how molecules hold together, why breaking a bond always costs energy, and where the energy of a reaction comes from when new bonds form.
?? How do we know?
The colours of light that molecules absorb reveal how long and how strong their bonds are: 74 pm and 436 kJ per mole for H₂. X-ray maps of crystals show electrons piled up between bonded atoms.
?? What it isn't
Not a stick or a hook, and not two atoms "wanting" to fill their shells. It is a balance of attractions and repulsions.
?? Where does it stop working?
Real bonds lie on a scale, from equal sharing, through unequal sharing (Section 2.7), to the complete hand-over of electrons that makes ions. The neat sharing picture is one end of that scale.
:::

## 2.4 What is a molecule?

:::scene Turin, 1811

A hundred years before Lewis, chemists had a puzzle about gases. The French chemist Joseph Gay-Lussac, who once rode a hydrogen balloon seven kilometres up into the sky to sample the air, noticed that gases react in simple volume ratios. Two litres of hydrogen react with one litre of oxygen to make two litres of steam (all measured at the same temperature and pressure). If each atom acted alone, you would expect only one litre of steam.

In northern Italy, a quiet professor from Turin called Amedeo Avogadro, then teaching in the nearby town of Vercelli, who had trained as a lawyer before falling in love with physics, explained it in 1811. Equal volumes of gas contain equal numbers of particles, he said, and hydrogen and oxygen gas are made not of single atoms but of pairs, H₂ and O₂. Then 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O fits the volumes exactly. Almost nobody listened. It took about fifty years before most chemists accepted his idea, and by then Avogadro had died.

What Avogadro had seen was the ==molecule==: a small group of atoms held together by covalent bonds, which travels around as one unit. A water molecule is three atoms; an oxygen molecule is two; a sugar molecule is 45. Every molecule of a given substance is the same.

:::note Molecule
A group of atoms joined by covalent bonds that acts as a single unit. All the molecules of one substance are identical (apart from tiny differences between isotopes).
:::

A formula tells you which atoms are in one molecule, and how many. The small number written low after a symbol, called a ==subscript==, counts atoms of that element in one molecule: CO₂ has 1 carbon atom and 2 oxygen atoms. A big number written in front counts whole molecules: 3 CO₂ means three separate carbon dioxide molecules, with 3 carbon atoms and 6 oxygen atoms between them.

:::note Subscript
The small lowered number in a formula. It counts atoms of the element just before it, in one molecule or formula unit.
:::

:::fig c2_three_co2() | Three CO₂ molecules: 3 carbon atoms and 6 oxygen atoms in total, but packaged as three separate molecules. That is not the same as one big C₃O₆ molecule.

The grouping matters. "3 C and 6 O" lists the atoms, but loses the most important fact: they come as three separate CO₂ molecules, each one a straight line of O=C=O.

## 2.5 A formula without a molecule

Now back to Davy's two elements, sodium and chlorine. When sodium metal burns in chlorine gas, each sodium atom passes one electron to a chlorine atom. That makes Na⁺ ions and Cl⁻ ions. Opposite charges attract, so the ions pack together. But the attraction works in every direction at once, so each Na⁺ pulls in Cl⁻ ions on all sides, and each Cl⁻ pulls in Na⁺ ions on all sides. The result is not a set of little pairs but a huge, repeating three-dimensional grid of alternating ions: an ==ionic lattice==. A single grain of salt contains roughly 10¹⁸ of them.

:::note Ionic lattice
A giant, regular, repeating arrangement of positive and negative ions, held together by their attraction in every direction.
:::

:::home Salt is made of cubes
Sprinkle a few grains of ordinary table salt onto a dark plate and look at them through a magnifying glass, or through a phone camera zoomed right in. Many grains are tiny cubes, or broken pieces of cubes, with flat faces and square corners. Now do the same with sugar: its grains are a different, more slanted shape. The square shape of a salt grain is the ionic lattice in the figure below, repeated millions of times in every direction until it is big enough to see.
:::

:::fig c2_nacl_lattice() | A small piece of the sodium chloride lattice. Each Na⁺ (purple) has six Cl⁻ (green) neighbours, and each Cl⁻ has six Na⁺. No ion belongs to any one partner. The real crystal continues in every direction for billions of ions.
:::live lattice3d {}

:::scene Leeds and Cambridge, 1913

How do we know the grid is really there? In 1912 the German physicist Max von Laue showed that X-rays passing through a crystal scatter into a pattern of spots. Within months, a father and son in England turned that pattern into a way of seeing atoms. William Henry Bragg was a professor at Leeds; his son William Lawrence Bragg was a young researcher at Cambridge. Together they shone X-rays through crystals of rock salt and worked out the only arrangement that could make the pattern: a regular grid of alternating ions, with every sodium surrounded by six chlorides and every chloride by six sodiums. No sodium was paired with one particular chloride. Some chemists found this very hard to accept: what had happened to the salt molecule? The father and son shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1915. Lawrence was 25, and he is still the youngest physics laureate.

:::story crystals

So in solid salt there is no such thing as "a NaCl molecule", and no pairs either.

:::careful
> "Each sodium ion is bonded to the chloride ion that took its electron, so salt is really made of Na–Cl pairs."

It is a very natural picture, because the story of salt usually begins with one sodium handing one electron to one chlorine. But look at the lattice: each Na⁺ is surrounded by six Cl⁻ ions, all exactly the same distance away, all pulling equally. Which one is "its" partner? None of them. An ion is held by its charge, not by its history. Here is proof you have already made with your own hands: the salt cubes that grew on your saucer in Section 2.1 formed from ions that were *already* ions, floating in water. Not one of those sodium ions gave an electron to the chloride beside it, and yet the crystal holds together exactly as well as any other.
:::

The formula NaCl tells you the ==ratio==: one sodium ion for every chloride ion, which makes the whole crystal neutral. Chemists call that smallest ratio a ==formula unit==.

:::note Formula unit
The simplest ratio of ions in an ionic compound. NaCl means 1 Na⁺ for every 1 Cl⁻; MgCl₂ means 1 Mg²⁺ for every 2 Cl⁻.
:::

Here is the answer Chapter 1 promised. Pulling an electron off a sodium atom costs energy: 496 kJ for every mole of atoms. A chlorine atom capturing that electron gives some energy back, 349 kJ per mole. That still leaves the pair 147 kJ worse off. If ions just floated apart, salt would never form. But when the ions come together into a lattice, all those opposite charges pulling on each other release a great deal of energy: about 787 kJ per mole. So the lattice ends up far lower in energy than the separate atoms. Sodium does not "want" to lose an electron. Losing it costs energy, and the attraction in the crystal pays that cost back several times over.

**Predict first.** Sodium "wants" to lose an electron, school books often say. If that were true, would pulling the electron off sodium give out energy or cost energy? Decide, then follow the energy one step at a time below.

:::fig c2_salt_energy() | An energy account for making solid salt from sodium and chlorine atoms, per mole. Removing electrons from sodium costs 496 kJ; chlorine capturing them gives back 349 kJ; the ions packing into a lattice releases 787 kJ. Overall: 640 kJ lower than where it started. (All values for gas-phase atoms and ions.) This account starts with separate gaseous atoms, not Na(s) and Cl₂(g); a standard formation account also needs atomisation steps.
:::live saltsteps {}

Watch the ball. It climbs 496 kJ when sodium loses its electron, comes only part of the way back down (349 kJ) when chlorine catches it, and is still 147 kJ uphill. Only the last step, the ions packing together into a crystal, carries it far below where it started: 787 kJ down, 640 kJ below the start overall. Salt forms not because any atom wants anything, but because the finished crystal sits at the bottom of a deep energy valley.

That explains the chemical difference between table salt in ordinary food use and its constituent elements; suitable amounts still matter. The ions in salt are not sodium metal or chlorine gas. Na⁺ ions have different numbers of electrons from sodium atoms, so they behave completely differently: they do not undergo sodium metal’s vigorous reaction with water. The dissolved ions still interact with surrounding water molecules. Same nuclei, different electrons, different chemistry.

:::five Ionic lattice
? What is it?
A giant, regular, repeating grid of positive and negative ions, each held by its neighbours in every direction. In salt, each Na⁺ is surrounded by six Cl⁻, and each Cl⁻ by six Na⁺.
? Why is it called this?
A lattice is a criss-cross framework of thin strips with gaps between them, like a garden trellis; the word comes from an old French word for a strip of wood. "Ionic" because it is built from ions.
? What problem did it solve?
Chemists expected salt to be made of NaCl molecules, and could not explain why it melts only at a very high temperature, grows as cubes, and carries electricity only when melted or dissolved. A grid of ions explains all three.
? Why does it work?
Opposite charges attract and like charges repel. In alternating rows, every ion is surrounded by opposite charges, so the attractions win in every direction and release a great deal of energy: about 787 kJ per mole for salt.
? How does it help?
It explains why the formula NaCl gives a ratio and not a molecule, why salt grains are cubes, and why the energy account for salt runs downhill.
?? How do we know?
In 1913 the Braggs shone X-rays through rock salt and showed that only a grid of alternating ions, each with six neighbours, could make the pattern of spots.
?? What it isn't
Not a collection of Na–Cl pairs. No ion belongs to any one partner.
?? Where does it stop working?
Real crystals have flaws: missing ions, cracks and rough surfaces. The perfect grid describes the inside of a crystal, not every corner of it.
:::

## 2.6 Why composition is not enough

:::scene Paris, 1772

On a sunny day in Paris, a group of chemists gathered round a giant magnifying glass. Among them was Antoine Lavoisier, a wealthy young tax official who spent every spare hour, and much of his fortune, on chemistry. They focused the sunlight onto a diamond sealed inside a glass jar. The diamond grew hot, then simply vanished, and the air in the jar now contained "fixed air", what we call carbon dioxide. Twenty-five years later, in 1797, the English chemist Smithson Tennant burned weighed amounts of diamond and of charcoal, and showed that equal masses give equal amounts of carbon dioxide. The most precious stone in the world was nothing but carbon, the same stuff as soot.

Diamond and graphite, the grey material in a pencil, are both pure carbon. (Pencil "lead" has no lead in it at all: it is graphite mixed with clay.) Burn a gram of either one in oxygen and you get exactly the same amount of carbon dioxide. So their formula is the same: just C. And yet diamond is the hardest natural material known, while graphite is so soft that it leaves a trail on paper. Graphite even conducts electricity; diamond does not.

The difference is entirely in how the atoms are connected. In diamond, every carbon atom is joined by covalent bonds to four others, pointing to the corners of a pyramid-like shape called a tetrahedron. The bonds continue in every direction through the whole crystal, making one enormous, rigid ==covalent network==. In graphite, each carbon atom is joined to only three others, in flat sheets of hexagons like chicken wire. The sheets are stacked in layers, and the attraction between layers is weak, so they slide past each other easily. That sliding is what you see when a pencil leaves a mark: thousands of sheets peeling off onto the paper. Much later, X-rays like the Braggs' showed exactly how differently the carbon atoms are arranged in the two.

:::note Covalent network
A giant structure in which covalent bonds link atoms throughout a whole crystal, with no separate molecules. Diamond is one.
:::

:::note Allotropes
Different forms of the same element, with their atoms joined in different ways. Diamond and graphite are allotropes of carbon; O₂ and O₃ (ozone) are allotropes of oxygen.
:::

:::fig c2_diamond_graphite() | Two ways of joining carbon atoms. Diamond (left): every atom bonded to four neighbours, in a network that runs through the whole crystal. Graphite (right): flat hexagonal sheets, weakly attracted to the sheets above and below.
:::live carbon3d {}

You can test the strangest of these differences yourself.

:::home Draw a wire
Do this with an adult nearby. You need a very soft pencil (6B to 8B; an ordinary HB pencil will not work), a 9-volt battery, a small LED (red works best) and some sticky tape. On paper, shade a short, wide strip, about 4 cm long and 1 cm wide, pressing hard and going over it again and again until it is dark and shiny. Hold one terminal of the battery on one end of the strip. Press one leg of the LED firmly onto the other end of the strip, and touch the LED's other leg to the battery's other terminal, so that the electricity has to travel along the pencil line. (If it does not light, turn the LED round: it only works one way, with its longer leg towards the battery's + terminal.) **Predict first:** can a pencil line carry electricity? It can: the LED glows, faintly, so dim the room to see it, because electrons can travel along graphite's sheets. Make the strip longer or thinner and the LED dims, or goes out. A diamond line, if you could draw one, would carry nothing, though it is the same element. Never touch the LED's legs straight onto both battery terminals: without the pencil line to hold the current back, it will burn the LED out. Use only a small battery like this, never the mains.
:::

Different forms of the same element are called ==allotropes==. Oxygen has them too: ordinary oxygen, O₂, which you breathe, and ozone, O₃, a sharp-smelling gas that high in the atmosphere absorbs much of the Sun's harmful ultraviolet light.

Lavoisier did not live to see any of this. During the French Revolution, tax officials were hated, and in 1794 he was sent to the guillotine. The mathematician Joseph-Louis Lagrange is reported to have said that it took only a moment to cut off that head, and a hundred years might not produce another like it.

## 2.7 Unequal sharing and molecular dipoles

In a hydrogen molecule, both atoms are the same, so the shared electrons are shared equally. But in a water molecule, the oxygen nucleus, with 8 protons, pulls on the shared electrons much more strongly than a hydrogen nucleus with only 1 (even after oxygen's inner electrons screen off part of that pull). So the shared electrons spend more time near the oxygen. The oxygen end becomes slightly negative, written δ⁻ ("delta minus"), and the hydrogen ends slightly positive, δ⁺. These are ==partial charges==: much smaller than the full charge of an ion.

:::note Partial charges (δ⁺, δ⁻)
Small charges on atoms in a molecule, caused by electrons being shared unequally. They are fractions of a full charge.
:::

:::scene Pasadena, California, 1932

How hard does each atom pull? In 1932 Linus Pauling, a young chemist at the California Institute of Technology who would later win two Nobel Prizes, one for chemistry and one for peace, worked out a scale by comparing the energies of many different bonds. How strongly an atom pulls shared electrons is called its ==electronegativity==. On the scale that grew from Pauling's, hydrogen scores 2.20, carbon 2.55, nitrogen 3.04 and oxygen 3.44. The bigger the difference between two bonded atoms, the more unequal the sharing. A bond with unequal sharing is called a ==polar bond==.

:::note Electronegativity
How strongly an atom in a bond pulls shared electrons towards itself. Fluorine, oxygen and nitrogen are among the strongest.
:::

Now the interesting part. Carbon dioxide has two polar bonds too: oxygen (3.44) pulls harder than carbon (2.55). But a CO₂ molecule is perfectly straight, O=C=O, so the two oxygen ends pull in exactly opposite directions and cancel out, like two equally strong children in a tug of war. The molecule as a whole has no positive end and no negative end. Water is bent, with an angle of 104.5° between its bonds, so its two pulls do not cancel. Water has a negative oxygen side and a positive hydrogen side: it is a ==polar molecule==, with a ==dipole==.

:::note Dipole
A molecule (or bond) with a slightly positive end and a slightly negative end.
:::

:::fig c2_dipoles() | Bond pulls (thin arrows, pointing towards the atom that pulls the electrons harder) and the molecule's overall pull (thick arrow). In straight CO₂ the two bond pulls cancel. In bent H₂O they add up, so the molecule has a positive side and a negative side.
:::live molecule3d {"set":"dipoles"}

So a molecule's polarity depends on two things at once: its bonds, and its shape. Methane, CH₄, also comes out non-polar: its four slightly polar bonds point to the corners of a tetrahedron and cancel. Ammonia, NH₃, is a squat pyramid, and its bonds do not cancel.

:::fig c2_shapes() | The three shapes as measured. Methane's four bonds point to the corners of a tetrahedron, 109.5° apart, and cancel. Ammonia's three bonds form a squat pyramid and water's two a bent line, so their pulls do not cancel.
:::live atlas_shapes

:::evidence Measuring a molecule's lopsidedness | 20th century
Polar molecules twist to line up in an electric field, and this changes how a substance responds to the field and how its molecules absorb microwaves. The Dutch-born physicist Peter Debye worked out how to turn such measurements into each molecule's dipole moment, a measure of how lopsided its charge is; the unit is named the debye after him. Water's is 1.85 debye, ammonia's 1.47 and hydrogen chloride's 1.08. Carbon dioxide and methane come out at zero, just as the shapes predict. These same measurements also confirmed the shapes themselves.
:::

:::careful
> "A thin stream of water bends towards a charged comb, so that proves water molecules are polar."

It is a lovely demonstration, and you may see it in a textbook. But careful experiments in 2000 showed that the stream bends mainly because the charged comb pulls extra charge onto the water as it flows from the tap, and then attracts that charge. How much a liquid bends depends mostly on how easily charge can flow onto it, not simply on whether its molecules are polar. So the bending stream is not good evidence that water molecules are polar. The real evidence is the dipole-moment measurements described above. It is a good example of why scientists keep re-checking even familiar experiments.
:::

So what *can* you see at home? Not dipoles themselves, but one of their most important consequences: polar substances mix with polar substances, and non-polar with non-polar.

:::home Fireworks in a glass
Fill a tall glass nearly to the top with water. In a cup, stir three or four drops of food colouring into two tablespoons of cooking oil (the drops will not mix in; they break into little beads). Pour the oil gently onto the water. **Predict first:** what will the coloured beads do? They sink slowly through the oil. Some rest on top of the water for a little while (give them a few minutes), but when each one breaks through it bursts into a streaming cloud of colour. Food colouring is dye dissolved in water. It mixes into water, whose molecules are polar, but not into oil, whose molecules are almost non-polar. This shows "like mixes with like"; it does not measure a dipole, but it is a real effect of one.
:::

:::five Polar molecule
? What is it?
A molecule with a slightly positive end and a slightly negative end, because its electrons are shared unequally and its shape does not let the pulls cancel. Water is polar; carbon dioxide is not.
? Why is it called this?
From "pole", the name for each end of a magnet, which comes from the Greek *polos*, a pivot or axis. A polar molecule has two ends with opposite charges, so it is also called a dipole, "two poles".
? What problem did it solve?
It explained why some substances mix and others refuse, and why molecules with the same kinds of bond can behave so differently: CO₂ and H₂O both have polar bonds, but only water is a polar molecule.
? Why does it work?
A more electronegative atom, such as oxygen, pulls shared electrons towards itself, making small partial charges. If the molecule is lopsided, like bent water, the pulls add up; if it is straight or evenly balanced, they cancel.
? How does it help?
It predicts what dissolves in what ("like mixes with like"), and helps explain how strongly molecules stick to each other, which Chapter 3 uses to explain boiling points.
?? How do we know?
Measured dipole moments: water 1.85 debye, ammonia 1.47, carbon dioxide and methane zero, just as their shapes predict.
?? What it isn't
Not an ion. The charges are only partial, small fractions of a full charge, and the molecule as a whole is neutral.
?? Where does it stop working?
Electronegativity is a handy scale, not a single measured thing, and different scales give slightly different numbers. It tells you roughly how unequal the sharing is, not exactly.
:::

## The case of the three unlabelled jars

:::case The case of the three unlabelled jars
A laboratory has three jars whose labels have fallen off. The labels on the floor say *water*, *carbon dioxide* and *sodium chloride*. Here is what the technician finds.

1. Jar A holds a colourless liquid that boils at 100 °C. Measurements show its molecules have a dipole moment of 1.85 debye.
2. Jar B holds a white solid at −80 °C, which turns straight into a colourless gas at −78 °C without melting first. Its molecules have polar bonds, but a dipole moment of zero.
3. Jar C holds hard, white, cube-shaped crystals that melt only at 801 °C. As a solid it does not conduct electricity; dissolved in water, it does.

Which label belongs on each jar? For each one, say what kind of particles it is made of and which clue settles it.
???
- **Jar A is water.** It is made of bent H₂O molecules. The bent shape means the two polar O–H bonds do not cancel, giving a dipole moment (clue 1); and water boils at 100 °C.
- **Jar B is carbon dioxide**, frozen ("dry ice"). It is made of straight O=C=O molecules: polar bonds whose pulls cancel exactly, so the dipole moment is zero (clue 2).
- **Jar C is sodium chloride.** It is an ionic lattice, not molecules. The very high melting point (clue 3) shows how strong the attractions in the lattice are. It cannot conduct as a solid because the ions are locked in place; dissolved, they are free to move.
:::
