Start with a little magic. Take two glowsticks, snap them both so they light, and drop one into a mug of hot tap water and the other into a glass of iced water. Within a minute the one in hot water is blazing brightly, and the one in iced water has dimmed to a ghostly glow. Swap them over and the brightness swaps too. Nothing has been added or taken away. The same chemicals are reacting inside each plastic tube, giving out their energy as light. The only difference is the temperature, and it changes how fast they react.

Chapter 4 kept count of atoms. This chapter keeps count of something just as strictly conserved, energy, and then asks two new questions that atom-counting cannot answer. How fast does a reaction go? And when does it stop? The answers explain the glowsticks, why a matchstick needs striking, why cooking speeds up when the flame is turned up, why enzymes in your body matter so much, and why some reactions never quite finish.

## 5.1 Where does the energy go?

Light a gas burner and hold your hand above it (not too close). The reaction between methane and oxygen is warming the air around it. Now squeeze an instant cold pack from a first-aid kit: inside, a solid dissolves in water, and the pack gets cold enough to sting your fingers. Some changes give energy out; others take energy in.

To keep track, chemists split the world into two parts. The ==system== is the chemicals you are studying. The ==surroundings== are everything else: the water around them, the beaker, the air, your hand. Energy is never created or destroyed, so any energy the system loses, the surroundings gain, and the other way round.

:::note System and surroundings
The system is the substances you are studying. The surroundings are everything else, which can give energy to the system or take it away.
:::

A change that gives energy to the surroundings is ==exothermic==: the surroundings warm up. A change that takes energy from the surroundings is ==endothermic==: the surroundings cool down. In an energy diagram, exothermic means the products sit lower than the starting substances, and the difference has gone out into the surroundings. (Where does that energy actually come from? Not, as most people guess, from bonds breaking. Section 5.6 shows it is the other way round.)

:::note Exothermic, endothermic
Exothermic: energy flows out of the system and the surroundings get warmer. Endothermic: energy flows into the system and the surroundings get colder.
:::

:::fig c5_energy_levels() | Two energy diagrams. Left: an exothermic change. The products are lower in energy, and the difference warms the surroundings. Right: an endothermic change, like a cold pack. The products are higher, and the energy is taken from the surroundings, which get colder.

:::evidence You can't put a thermometer in a bond
We cannot measure the energy of a molecule directly, so we measure what happens around it. In a ==calorimeter==, a reaction happens inside a container surrounded by a known amount of water, with a thermometer in the water. Warming 1 gram of water by 1 °C takes about 4.2 joules. So if 100 g of water warms by 10 °C, about 100 × 10 × 4.2 = 4,200 joules came out of the reaction. A cold pack works in reverse: its temperature drop tells you how much energy the dissolving took in. The first ice calorimeter was built in Paris in the winter of 1782–83 by Lavoisier, the man who weighed everything in Chapter 4, and the mathematician Pierre-Simon Laplace: they measured heat by how much ice it melted, using Joseph Black's discovery from Chapter 3.
:::

:::note Calorimeter
A container that measures the energy a reaction gives out or takes in, by measuring the temperature change of water around it.
:::

:::home A reaction that feels cold
*Predict first:* most people expect a fierce, fizzing reaction to get hot. Write down what you think will happen to the temperature.

Pour about half a cup of vinegar into a glass and wrap your hand around it for a moment to feel its temperature. Stir in a heaped teaspoon of baking soda (stand the glass in a bowl or the sink: it fizzes up). While it fizzes, hold the glass again, or put a kitchen thermometer in it. The mixture gets a few degrees cooler. This reaction is endothermic: to make its products it takes energy from its surroundings, and the nearest surroundings are the vinegar, the glass and your hand.

For a twin that does the opposite, dampen a pad of plain steel wool with vinegar, squeeze it out, and seal it in a zip bag with a thermometer if you have one. Over a few minutes the bag grows slightly warm as the iron quietly rusts. One reaction fizzes and cools; the other sits still and warms. Drama tells you nothing about which way the energy flows.
:::

You may have read at school that when an acid and a base neutralise each other, heat is given out. That is true for many of them; this one is an exception, because breaking up the baking soda into carbon dioxide gas costs more energy than the rest of the reaction gives back.

:::careful
> "In an exothermic reaction the energy is used up, so afterwards there's less energy in the universe."

The energy has moved, not vanished. It left the chemicals and went into the surroundings, mostly as faster-jiggling particles of air and water (which is what "warmer" means). Count system and surroundings together and the total never changes.
:::

:::

## 5.2 Why is there an energy barrier?

:::scene Sweden, 1889

Svante Arrhenius was not supposed to be a success. Five years earlier, his doctoral thesis at Uppsala, which claimed that salts break up into charged particles when they dissolve, had been given nearly the lowest passing grade: his examiners thought it was probably nonsense. Arrhenius, a big, cheerful, stubborn young Swede, sent it to the best chemists in Europe anyway, and some of them, especially Wilhelm Ostwald in Riga, thought it was brilliant. (He was right about the ions, and won a Nobel Prize for them in 1903. In 1896 he also became the first person to calculate how much carbon dioxide in the air could warm the Earth.)

In 1889 he turned to a different puzzle. Chemists had long noticed that many reactions speed up dramatically with only a small rise in temperature, often roughly doubling for every 10 °C (this is only a rule of thumb). Why should a small warming make such a large difference?

Start with the bigger puzzle. Burning paper gives out plenty of energy, so the products, carbon dioxide and water, are lower in energy than paper and oxygen. Downhill, you might think, so it should just happen. And yet the book in your hands has been sitting in air for its whole life without catching fire. Why?

Because to get from the start to the finish, the particles have to pass through an awkward in-between stage: old bonds stretched and partly broken, new ones not yet formed. That in-between arrangement is higher in energy than either the start or the end. To get over it, colliding particles must bring enough energy with them. The height of that hump, measured from the starting level, is called the ==activation energy==. The idea is Arrhenius's.

:::note Activation energy
The extra energy that colliding particles need to get over the hump between reactants and products. It decides how easily a reaction starts, not how much energy it releases overall.
:::

:::fig c5_barrier() | An energy profile (schematic, in made-up units). The reactants start at 50, the products end at 20, so 30 units are released overall. But first the reaction must climb to the peak at 100: a barrier of 50 going forward, and 80 going backward. The horizontal axis is progress of the reaction, not time.
:::live barrier {}

Think of a ball in a dip on a hillside, with a deeper valley on the other side of a ridge. The valley is lower, but the ball stays put until something kicks it over the ridge. A struck match is the kick for paper: the flame heats a small patch until its molecules are colliding hard enough to get over the barrier. The energy given out then heats the next patch, and the fire spreads by itself.

Now Arrhenius's answer about temperature. At any temperature, particles have a spread of energies: most are moving at middling speeds, and a few are moving very fast. Only the few that collide with more than the activation energy can react. Warm the mixture slightly, and the whole crowd speeds up a little, but the number of those rare, very fast particles goes up a lot. So the rate jumps. That is why the glowstick in hot water blazes, why food spoils faster in heat and keeps longer in a fridge, and why Cavendish's hydrogen and oxygen in Chapter 4 waited for a spark: at room temperature, almost no collisions were hard enough.

Notice two separate numbers in Figure 5.2. The overall energy change (30 units released) depends only on where the reaction starts and ends. The barrier (50 units forward) depends on the peak. Raise or lower the peak, and the overall change stays exactly the same.

:::careful
> "A reaction that releases lots of energy must happen quickly."

How much energy a reaction releases and how fast it goes are separate questions. Paper burning releases a lot, yet it does not start at room temperature, because its barrier is high. Some reactions release almost nothing and are very fast. And warming speeds up *every* reaction, whichever way its energy flows: the glowstick gives energy out, and it still blazes faster in hot water.
:::

:::try Investigation 5.2
Keep the reactants and products where they are, and raise the peak. What happens to the forward barrier, the reverse barrier, and the overall energy change? Predict first.
:::

:::check

Forward barrier = 100 − 40 = 60. Reverse barrier = 100 − 10 = 90. Overall change = 10 − 40 = −30 (30 units released). The reverse barrier is bigger because the products sit lower, so they have further to climb.
:::

## 5.3 How quickly does change happen?

A ==rate== is a change per unit of time. Your heartbeat is a rate (beats per minute); so is the speed of a car (kilometres per hour). The rate of a reaction is how much of a substance is used up, or made, per second or per minute. A glowstick's brightness is a rate you can see: the brighter it shines, the more molecules are reacting each second.

:::note Rate of reaction
How quickly a reaction goes: the amount of a substance used up or made per unit of time.
:::

Rates depend on how often particles collide, and how hard. You can speed a reaction up by heating it (harder, more frequent collisions), making the solutions more concentrated (more particles to hit), or cutting a solid into smaller pieces (more surface where collisions can happen). An iron nail in a flame just glows; steel wool, which is iron in very thin strands, burns with sparks.

:::home Glowsticks, fast and slow
Snap two identical glowsticks and leave them whole (never cut one open). Put one in a mug of hot tap water and one in a glass of water with plenty of ice, and turn the lights off. Which is brighter? Next morning, which is still glowing? The warm one reacts faster, so it shines more brightly but uses up its chemicals sooner. The cold one is dimmer but lasts much longer. (A glowstick kept in the freezer can still glow faintly the next day.) Same reaction, same amount of chemicals, different rate.
:::

To see rates clearly, the app uses a simple made-up reaction in which a substance A turns into B, and B can also turn back into A:

:::eq
A ⇌ B
:::

The double arrow means the change goes both ways. In this model, A turns into B at a rate of 2 × (the amount of A) per second, so in each hundredth of a second about 2% of the A changes; we say its forward rate constant is 2 per second. B turns back at 1 × (the amount of B) per second, about 1% of the B in each hundredth of a second (reverse constant 1 per second). We start with 10 units of A and no B.

:::figwide c5_rate_curves() | The made-up A ⇌ B reaction, starting with 10 units of A. At first A turns into B quickly, because there is lots of A and no B to turn back. The change slows down and the amounts level off at about 3.33 units of A and 6.67 of B. The dashed lines show the same reaction with both rate constants doubled: same end point, reached twice as fast.
:::live equilibrium {}

At the start, A is turning into B at 2 × 10 = 20 units per second and nothing is turning back. After one second the amounts are about 3.67 of A and 6.33 of B. A is now changing into B at 2 × 3.67 ≈ 7.3 units per second, while B is turning back at 1 × 6.33 ≈ 6.3 per second. The overall change has slowed to about 1 unit per second. After a few seconds the amounts barely move at all.

Here is the important thing: how fast it gets there and where it ends up are different questions. Double both rate constants (dashed lines) and the reaction reaches the same 3.33 : 6.67 mixture in half the time.

How do chemists draw curves like these for real reactions? They follow something that changes as the reaction goes: the colour of a solution (with a meter that measures how much light gets through), the pressure of a gas, or the mass of a flask as a gas escapes, just like your vinegar on the kitchen scale in Chapter 4. Reading it every few seconds gives a curve steep at first and flattening out. The steepness of the curve at any moment is the rate.

:::

## 5.4 A different pathway

:::scene London, 1817

Humphry Davy, who had danced round his laboratory over potassium in Chapter 1, was by now the most famous scientist in Britain. In 1815 he had invented a safety lamp for coal miners, who were being killed by explosions of methane gas underground. Two years later, still experimenting with methane, he noticed something strange. A coil of warm platinum wire, held in a mixture of methane and air, began to glow red hot, and stayed glowing, with no flame at all. The gas was reacting on the surface of the metal, quietly and steadily, and the platinum was not used up.

In 1835 Berzelius, the inventor of chemical symbols from Chapter 4, gave such effects a name: ==catalysis==. A ==catalyst== is a substance that makes a reaction faster without being used up. It works by giving the reaction a different route, one with a lower hump to climb. The start and the finish are exactly the same as before; only the path between them changes.

:::note Catalyst
A substance that speeds up a reaction by providing a route with a lower activation energy. It is not used up overall.
:::

:::fig c5_catalyst() | A catalyst opens a lower route (dashed) between the same start and finish. In this made-up example the peak drops from 130 to 90 units, so the forward barrier drops from 80 to 40 and the reverse barrier from 110 to 70: both by the same 40 units. The overall energy change stays at −30.
:::live barrier {"catalyst":true}

Because the start and finish do not move, lowering the peak lowers the barrier in *both* directions by the same amount. So a catalyst speeds up the forward and the reverse reactions together. It makes a reaction reach its end point sooner, but it cannot change where that end point is.

:::story catalysts

Catalysts are everywhere. The catalytic converter in a car's exhaust uses metals like platinum, Davy's metal, to turn poisonous carbon monoxide into carbon dioxide before it leaves the exhaust pipe. Your body runs on catalysts called ==enzymes==: without them, the reactions that digest your food and keep your cells alive would be far too slow to keep you alive.

:::note Enzyme
A catalyst made by a living thing, usually a protein.
:::

Here is one at work right now. Your blood carries carbon dioxide from your muscles to your lungs, and part of the trip involves carbon dioxide reacting with water. On its own, that reaction is too slow for the job. An enzyme in your red blood cells, carbonic anhydrase, speeds it up by more than a million times, in both directions. In the muscles, where carbon dioxide is plentiful, the reaction runs forward; in the lungs, where carbon dioxide is breathed away, it runs backward. One catalyst, both directions, exactly as Figure 5.4 predicts.

:::home A helper that is never used up
You need an adult, a small bottle of 3% hydrogen peroxide from a chemist's shop (sold for cleaning cuts; if the label says 6% or "20 volume", the adult should mix one part with three parts water instead of half and half below), a packet of dried yeast and a raw potato. Peroxide slowly breaks down by itself into water and oxygen, far too slowly to see: 2 H₂O₂ → 2 H₂O + O₂. Living things carry an enzyme that speeds this up enormously.

Mix a little peroxide half and half with water in two glasses. Into one, sprinkle a pinch of yeast; into the other, drop a slice of raw potato. Both froth with bubbles of oxygen, and the glass may feel faintly warm. When the frothing dies down, pour fresh diluted peroxide onto the *same* yeast. It froths again: the catalyst was not used up.

*Predict first:* what will a slice of *boiled* potato do? Then try it. (Almost nothing happens: cooking wrecks the enzyme's shape, and with it the lower route.) Keep the peroxide away from eyes, taste nothing, and pour everything down the sink afterwards.
:::

:::careful
> "A catalyst makes a reaction give out more energy, or makes more product."

A catalyst does not change the start or the finish, so it cannot change the overall energy change or the final mixture. It only changes how fast you get there.
:::

:::try Investigation 5.4
Lower the peak without moving either end. Write down the forward and reverse barriers before and after. Then go back to the A ⇌ B model and double both constants. How is that like adding a catalyst?
:::

:::check
At a fixed temperature, could a catalyst lower the forward barrier by 30 units while lowering the reverse barrier by only 10? Why or why not?
???
No. Both barriers are measured up to the same peak. The reactants and products stay where they were, so if the peak drops by 30, both barriers drop by 30.

## 5.5 A balance that keeps moving

:::scene The salt lakes of Egypt, 1799

Claude-Louis Berthollet was one of the scientists Napoleon took with him when he invaded Egypt, a chemist who had worked alongside Lavoisier in Paris. Exploring the Natron Lakes in the desert west of the Nile, he saw crusts of soda (sodium carbonate) forming along the shores. In the laboratory, everyone knew the reaction went the other way: soda and calcium chloride react to make limestone and salt, and that was that. Yet here, in lakes thick with salt and lined with limestone, the reaction was running backwards. Berthollet's conclusion, published a few years later, was bold: reactions are not one-way streets. With enough of the "products" crowded together, a reaction can run in reverse.

Look at the end of Figure 5.3 again. After a few seconds, the amounts of A and B stop changing. Has the reaction stopped? No. A is still turning into B, and B is still turning into A. They are simply happening at the same rate, so the amounts no longer change. This is called ==dynamic equilibrium==: "equilibrium" because nothing seems to change, "dynamic" because underneath, everything is still moving.

:::note Dynamic equilibrium
The state of a reversible reaction when the forward and reverse rates are equal, so the amounts stay constant even though both reactions continue.
:::

Think of a crowded platform at a Mumbai local station in the rush hour. Every minute, people pour in from the stairs and people squeeze onto trains. If they arrive as fast as they leave, the number of people on the platform stays the same, even though it is never the same people for long.

In the A ⇌ B model, the end point is 3.33 units of A and 6.67 units of B. Check the rates: forward is 2 × 3.33 = 6.67 units per second, and reverse is 1 × 6.67 = 6.67 units per second. Equal rates. But notice that the *amounts* are not equal at all: there is twice as much B as A. Equilibrium means equal rates, not equal amounts.

:::fig c5_equilibrium_rates() | The two rates in the A ⇌ B model. The forward rate falls as A is used up; the reverse rate rises as B builds up. Where they meet, the amounts stop changing: dynamic equilibrium. Both reactions are still going at 6.67 units per second.

How do we know a reaction at equilibrium is still running both ways, if nothing seems to change? Chemists use atoms that can be told apart. Make two identical mixtures of nitrogen, hydrogen and ammonia, and let both settle at equilibrium (at high temperature, with a catalyst). In one, use ordinary hydrogen; in the other, use deuterium, a heavier isotope of hydrogen that behaves almost exactly the same. Then mix the two. The total amounts of nitrogen, hydrogen and ammonia stay just as they were. But soon ammonia molecules turn up carrying a mixture of ordinary and heavy hydrogen atoms. The only way that can happen is if ammonia was still being broken apart and made again, all the time.

:::home Two glasses, two straws
You need two identical glasses, one wide straw (a bubble-tea or milkshake straw) and one ordinary thin straw. Fill one glass about three-quarters full of water with a drop of food colouring; leave the other empty. Call them A and B.

Now take turns. Dip the wide straw to the bottom of glass A, close the top with your thumb, lift out the water trapped inside and let it go into B. Then dip the thin straw to the bottom of B, carry its water back to A. Repeat, one scoop each way, over and over.

*Predict first:* when the levels stop changing, will A and B be level?

Keep going until they stop changing. Each scoop is small, so this takes patience: fifty rounds or more, which is easier with a friend holding the second straw. They do not end level: B, which started empty, ends up *fuller* than A. Yet every round you are still moving water both ways. The levels settle where the wide straw's scoop from the shallower glass carries exactly as much as the thin straw's scoop from the deeper one. Equal flows, unequal amounts: that is a dynamic equilibrium, and you just made one with your thumbs.
:::

:::story equilibrium

:::careful
> "At equilibrium, there must be equal amounts of reactants and products."

Equal *rates*, not equal amounts. With a forward constant of 2 and a reverse constant of 1, B ends up twice as plentiful as A. The amounts only come out equal if the two constants happen to be equal.
:::

Set the forward constant to 1 and the reverse constant to 3, starting with 10 units of A. At equilibrium, A = 7.5 and B = 2.5. Show that the rates are equal, and explain why A ends up three times as plentiful as B.

## 5.6 Breaking bonds costs; making bonds pays

Here is one of the most important ideas in the book, and one that many adults get wrong. **Breaking a bond always takes energy. Making a bond always gives energy out.**

It follows from Chapter 2. A bond is an attraction between atoms. Pulling two attracted things apart is like stretching a rubber band or lifting a magnet off a fridge: you have to put energy in. Letting them snap together gives energy out.

So where does the energy of burning come from? A reaction is a two-step account. First, bonds in the reactants break, which costs energy. Then new bonds form in the products, which pays energy back. If the new bonds are stronger, more is paid back than was spent, and the reaction is exothermic.

:::note Bond enthalpy
The energy needed to break one mole of a particular bond in gas molecules. Tables give average values, so calculations with them are good estimates, not exact answers.
:::

How do we know how strong a bond is? It can be measured by shining light or other radiation on molecules and finding the lowest energy that splits them, or worked out from many calorimeter measurements. Average values for each kind of bond are collected in data tables. Using average ==bond enthalpies== in kilojoules per mole (H–H 436, O=O 498, O–H 463), hydrogen burning works out like this:

:::work
**2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O** (all as gases)

Break 2 H–H and 1 O=O: 2 × 436 + 498 = **1,370 kJ in**

Make 4 O–H: 4 × 463 = **1,852 kJ out**

Overall: 1,370 − 1,852 = **−482 kJ**, so 482 kJ is released.

Measured value: −484 kJ. Close, because average bond enthalpies are estimates.
:::

:::fig c5_bond_ledger() | The energy account for 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O. Climbing to separate atoms costs 1,370 kJ; forming four O–H bonds gives back 1,852 kJ. The difference, about 482 kJ, comes out as heat. (Real reactions don't actually pull everything into separate atoms first; the account works because energy depends only on where you start and finish.)

Landing within a few per cent of the measured answer, as here, is a good test that the "break, then make" account is right. Now a reaction that goes the other way. In lightning and in hot car engines, nitrogen and oxygen from the air join to make nitrogen monoxide, NO:

:::work
**N₂ + O₂ → 2 NO**

Break 1 N≡N and 1 O=O: 945 + 498 = **1,443 kJ in**

Make 2 N=O (631 kJ each in NO): 2 × 631 = **1,262 kJ out**

Overall: 1,443 − 1,262 = **+181 kJ**, so energy is taken in. Measured value: +181 kJ.
:::

This one is endothermic. The triple bond in N₂ is one of the strongest bonds there is, and the bonds in NO do not pay back enough. That is part of why the nitrogen in the air (78% of it) is so unreactive, and why it takes something as fierce as lightning or a hot engine to make NO.

:::careful
> "Energy is stored in bonds, and released when the bonds break."

This is the wrong way round, though you will hear it often. Breaking any bond takes energy in. The energy released by burning fuel comes from *making* the new, stronger bonds in carbon dioxide and water. Fuels are useful because their bonds, together with oxygen's, are weaker than the bonds in the products.
:::

:::try Investigation 5.6
Step through the three stages for 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O: reactants, separated atoms, products. Then do the same for N₂ + O₂ → 2 NO. In which reaction is more paid back than spent?
:::

:::check
Methane burns: CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O. Using average bond enthalpies (C–H 413, O=O 498, C=O in CO₂ 799, O–H 463 kJ per mole), estimate the overall energy change. Is it exothermic or endothermic?

:::

## 5.7 Disturbing an equilibrium

:::scene Paris, 1884

Henry Le Châtelier came from a family of engineers: his father helped build France's railways, and the family had long worked with lime and cement. He trained at the École Polytechnique and the School of Mines, and much of his work was practical and urgent: why coal dust and methane explode in mines, how cement sets, how to measure the fierce temperatures inside furnaces. In 1884, thinking about how chemical balances respond when conditions change, he wrote down a rule that chemistry students have used ever since.

What happens if you change a mixture that has already settled at equilibrium? Suppose the A ⇌ B mixture has reached 3.33 units of A and 6.67 of B, and you suddenly add 4 more units of A.

Think about the rates. There is now much more A, 7.33 units, so the forward rate jumps to 2 × 7.33 ≈ 14.7 per second. The amount of B has not changed yet, so the reverse rate is still 6.67. Forward is faster than reverse, so overall A turns into B. As it does, the forward rate falls and the reverse rate rises, until they are equal again. This is Berthollet's salt lakes in miniature: crowd in more of one side, and the reaction is pushed the other way.

:::figwide c5_perturb() | Adding 4 units of A to the settled A ⇌ B mixture at 2 seconds. The forward reaction briefly outruns the reverse one, so some of the added A turns into B. The mixture settles again at about 4.67 of A and 9.33 of B: the total is 14, and B is once more twice A.
:::live equilibrium {"perturb":true}

Where does it end? The total is now 10 + 4 = 14 units, and the rates balance when B is twice A again. So A settles at 14 ÷ 3 ≈ 4.67 and B at 28 ÷ 3 ≈ 9.33. Some of the added A has turned into B, but not all of it.

Taking B away works the same way in reverse. Remove half the B, and the reverse rate drops, so the forward reaction wins until the 2 : 1 balance is restored. Le Châtelier's rule, called ==Le Châtelier's principle==, says: if you disturb a reaction at equilibrium, the mixture shifts in the direction that partly undoes the disturbance. That sounds as if the mixture is fighting back on purpose. It is not. The shift is just the rates becoming unequal for a while, then equal again.

:::note Le Châtelier's principle
A rule of thumb: when a system at equilibrium is disturbed, it shifts in the direction that partly undoes the change. For changes in amounts, it follows from the rates.
:::

You can watch it happen in a bottle of soda water. Sealed, it shows no bubbles at all: carbon dioxide is escaping from the liquid into the gas at the top exactly as fast as it dissolves back in. Open the cap and the gas above rushes out. Now the dissolving-back direction has almost nothing to work with, so escaping wins, and bubbles stream up. Screw the cap back on and the bubbling soon stops, as the gas above builds up until the two rates match again. (Keep it in the fridge and it stays fizzy longer: carbon dioxide dissolves better in cold water.)

Your own body does it too. Climb high in the mountains, in Ladakh say, and there is less oxygen in each breath, so less oxygen joins onto the haemoglobin in your blood: the balance has shifted. You feel breathless. You breathe faster at once, and over the following weeks your body makes extra red blood cells, packed with haemoglobin, which pushes the balance back. Trekkers call it getting used to the height; a chemist sees an equilibrium being nudged.

Two honest limits. First, our A ⇌ B model is the simplest possible reaction; real reactions often involve several steps and more complicated rate rules, though the idea of balancing rates still holds. Second, changing the *temperature* is different. It changes the rate constants themselves, unequally, so it moves the balance point. Adding a catalyst does not: it speeds both directions equally, so the mixture stays where it was.

:::careful
> "Adding more A changes the balance point for good. The equilibrium constant goes up to make room for the extra A."

The rate constants, 2 and 1, have not changed (the temperature is the same), so the balance point is still B : A = 2 : 1. Adding A only changes the amounts; the mixture moves until it reaches that same ratio again.
:::

:::try Investigation 5.7
Let the A ⇌ B mixture settle, then try each disturbance: add 4 units of A, remove half of B, or add a catalyst (double both constants). For each one, predict which rate is bigger just afterwards, and where the mixture will settle.
:::

:::check
The A ⇌ B mixture (forward 2, reverse 1) has settled at 3.33 of A and 6.67 of B. You remove half of B. Which rate is bigger immediately afterwards, and where does the mixture settle?
???
After removing half the B: A = 3.33, B = 3.33. Forward rate = 2 × 3.33 ≈ 6.7, reverse = 1 × 3.33 ≈ 3.3, so the forward reaction is faster and A turns into B. The total is now 6.67, and it settles when B is twice A: A ≈ 2.22, B ≈ 4.44.
:::

## The case of the expensive catalyst

:::case The case of the expensive catalyst
A (made-up) chemical factory turns a substance A into a useful product B, in a reversible reaction A ⇌ B that gives out heat. At the factory's working temperature, the mixture always settles at 2 parts B to 1 part A, so only about 67% ends up as B. The manager buys an expensive catalyst, hoping to push the yield up to 95%.

1. The catalyst lowers the peak of the energy profile without moving the start or the finish.
2. With the catalyst, the forward and reverse rate constants both double, from 2 and 1 to 4 and 2.
3. A chemist on the staff points out that the reaction is exothermic, and that its balance shifts towards B at lower temperatures, where it is unfortunately very slow.

Will the catalyst raise the yield to 95%? If not, what does it do, and what could the factory actually change to get more B?
???
The catalyst will not change the yield. Doubling both rate constants keeps their ratio at 4 : 2 = 2 : 1, so the mixture settles at the same 67% B, just faster (clues 1 and 2).

Two things could help. The factory could keep removing B as it forms: the reverse rate then stays low, so the forward reaction keeps winning and A keeps turning into B. Or it could run the reaction at a lower temperature, where the balance favours B more (clue 3). On its own, a lower temperature would make the reaction painfully slow, and that is where the catalyst earns its price: it lets a cooler reactor still reach its balance in a sensible time. Real chemical plants, such as the ones that make ammonia for fertiliser, use exactly this combination of catalyst, temperature and removing product.
:::
