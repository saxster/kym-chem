:::scene Paris, 1774

In his private laboratory in Paris, Antoine Lavoisier and his wife, Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier, were weighing a glass vessel with a piece of tin sealed inside it. Lavoisier was thirty, trained as a lawyer, and earned his fortune as a partner in the company that collected the king's taxes; chemistry was what he did before breakfast and after work, and on one whole day each week. He was rich, careful and slightly obsessed. He owned some of the most accurate balances in Europe, and he had come to believe that if you wanted to understand a change, you had to weigh everything, before and after, including the parts you could not see. Marie-Anne kept the notes, and later drew the engravings of his apparatus that chemists still reproduce today.

A hundred years earlier, Robert Boyle (the same Boyle who squeezed air in Chapter 3) had heated tin in a sealed flask, opened it, and found that the tin had become heavier. He thought particles of fire had soaked through the glass and stuck to the metal. Lavoisier repeated the experiment with one small change, and the change was everything. He did not open the vessel before weighing it.

So far, every change in this book has left the particles themselves alone. Ice melted, water boiled, salt dissolved, and the same molecules or ions were there at the end as at the start. Now something new happens. When wood burns, or iron rusts, or a cake bakes, the substances at the end are different substances from the ones you started with. This chapter is about what changes in a chemical reaction, and, just as important, what never does. It is also the chapter where chemistry, thanks largely to Lavoisier, became a science of measuring.

:::story lavoisier

## 4.1 What counts as a chemical change?

Put an ice cube in a glass and wait. It melts into water, which still freezes at 0 °C, still boils at 100 °C, and is still H₂O. Nothing new has been made: the molecules have only moved further apart and started sliding past each other. That is a ==physical change==.

:::note Physical change
A change of state, shape or mixing in which no new substance is made. The particles keep their identity.
:::

Now leave an iron nail outside through a Mumbai monsoon. A flaky, reddish-brown crust grows on it. The crust is not iron: it is brittle where iron bends, it is not attracted to a magnet the way iron is, and it does not shine. It is rust, a compound of iron, oxygen and hydrogen (mostly a form of iron oxide with water built into it). New substances have appeared, so this is a ==chemical change==, also called a ==chemical reaction==.

:::note Chemical change (reaction)
A change in which atoms are rearranged into new substances, with new properties. The atoms themselves are kept.
:::

At the particle level, the difference is sharp. In a physical change the same molecules end up in a different arrangement. In a chemical change the bonds inside the starting substances break and new bonds form, so the atoms regroup into different particles.

:::fig c4_physical_vs_chemical() | Left: melting. The same H₂O molecules, just more loosely arranged. Right: hydrogen burning in oxygen. The bonds in H₂ and O₂ break and the atoms regroup into H₂O, a new substance. In both panels every atom is still there.
:::live rearrange

How would you know which kind of change you are looking at, in a real kitchen? Clues like fizzing, a colour change, a smell, or heat given out are hints, but not proof. Sugar dissolving in chai disappears, yet it is still sugar (the tea tastes sweet). A pan of water bubbling on the stove is not making a gas from nothing; it is boiling. The real test is whether the substances at the end have different properties from the ones at the start: a different melting point, a different colour, different behaviour with a magnet or with water. If they do, something new was made.

:::evidence Test the stuff, not the drama | 18th century onwards
Chemists decide that a reaction has happened by testing what is there afterwards. Melted ice, refrozen, behaves exactly like the ice you started with: same melting point, same density, same everything. Rust does not: dissolve it in acid and you get a yellow-brown solution of iron ions; heat it strongly with carbon and you can get iron back, but only by another chemical reaction. When the properties are new, the substance is new.
:::

:::careful
> "The picture changed from H₂ and O₂ to H₂O, so a reaction has happened."

In the app, choosing a different diagram just shows you a different picture. It is not an experiment. In the real world, a mixture of hydrogen and oxygen can sit in a sealed bottle for years without turning into water (you will see why in Chapter 5). A claim that a reaction happened needs evidence that a new substance was formed.
:::

:::

:::check
When a fizzy tablet is dropped into water, bubbles appear and the water cools slightly. Is that enough to prove a chemical reaction has happened? What would you need to test?
???
Not on its own. Bubbles could be air that was trapped in the tablet, and cooling can happen when some things simply dissolve. You would need to show that a new substance was made: for example, collect the gas and test it (carbon dioxide turns limewater milky; air does not), or show that the dissolved substances left behind are different from what was in the tablet.
:::

## 4.2 Reading a chemical formula

:::scene Stockholm, 1813

By the early 1800s chemists had a problem of handwriting. John Dalton in Manchester drew each element as a little circle with a pattern inside: a dot for hydrogen, a plain ring for oxygen, a ring with a cross for sulfur. That worked for twenty elements, and became impossible for fifty, and nobody could print it easily. In Stockholm, the Swedish chemist Jöns Jacob Berzelius, who spent years measuring the masses of elements with fierce precision, proposed something far simpler: use the first letter of each element's Latin name, adding a second letter when two names begin the same way. H for hydrogen, O for oxygen, C for carbon, Cu for copper (*cuprum*), Na for sodium (*natrium*), Fe for iron (*ferrum*). Two hundred years later, every chemist on Earth writes his letters.

:::story symbols

A chemical formula is a recipe for one particle. H₂O says: two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom, joined. The small number written low, the ==subscript==, belongs to the atom just before it. No number means one. (Berzelius himself wrote the numbers high, like H²O; the low position became standard later.)

:::note Subscript
The small low number in a formula. It counts the atoms of the element just before it, in one particle (or one formula unit).
:::

If you want to talk about more than one particle, you put a big number in front, called a ==coefficient==. 3 H₂O means three separate water molecules. Count the atoms by multiplying: 3 × 2 = 6 hydrogen atoms and 3 × 1 = 3 oxygen atoms.

:::note Coefficient
The big number written in front of a formula. It counts how many whole particles there are.
:::

The two numbers do very different jobs. A coefficient changes *how much* you have. A subscript changes *what* you have. H₂O is water, which you drink. H₂O₂ is hydrogen peroxide, a pale liquid that bleaches hair and is used to clean wounds in dilute form; concentrated, it is dangerous. One extra oxygen atom in the recipe, and it is a different substance.

:::fig c4_formula_reading() | Three water molecules (3 H₂O) and one molecule of hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂). The coefficient 3 multiplies whole molecules. The subscript 2 after the O changes the molecule itself.

For ionic compounds (Chapter 2), the subscripts give the ratio of ions, and that ratio comes straight from the charges. A crystal as a whole has no overall charge, so the positive and negative charges must cancel. A magnesium ion is Mg²⁺ and a chloride ion is Cl⁻, so you need two chlorides for every magnesium: MgCl₂. Aluminium oxide needs a bit more thought. Al³⁺ and O²⁻ balance when there are two aluminiums (+6) for every three oxides (−6): Al₂O₃.

:::careful
> "2 H₂O and H₄O₂ are the same thing. They both have four hydrogens and two oxygens."

They have the same *number* of atoms, but they say different things about how the atoms are grouped. 2 H₂O is two separate water molecules. H₄O₂ would claim a single molecule with all six atoms joined together, which is not water (and is not a real substance you can buy). The grouping is the chemistry.
:::

:::try Investigation 4.2
Change the number of water molecules from 1 to 3. Watch how the atom counts change. Then try the ion combiner with Mg²⁺ and Cl⁻, and with Al³⁺ and O²⁻. Before you look, predict the formula from the charges.
:::

:::check
How many hydrogen atoms are there in 4 NH₃? And what is the formula of the compound made from calcium ions, Ca²⁺, and chloride ions, Cl⁻?
???
4 × 3 = 12 hydrogen atoms (and 4 nitrogen atoms). Calcium chloride is CaCl₂: one Ca²⁺ (+2) needs two Cl⁻ (−1 each) for the charges to cancel.
:::

## 4.3 Where did every atom go?

Back to Paris, and to Lavoisier's sealed vessel. He heated the tin inside it until a dull grey crust had formed on the metal, let it cool, and weighed the whole sealed vessel, glass, tin, crust, air and all. The mass had not changed. Then he broke the seal. Air rushed in with a hiss, and only now did the vessel get heavier. The crust had used up part of the air inside, leaving a partial emptiness that fresh air flowed in to fill. Boyle's "particles of fire" had never existed. Boyle had simply weighed his flask after the outside air had got in.

Lavoisier drew a conclusion that has held for every ordinary reaction ever measured: matter is neither created nor destroyed in a chemical change. Burning, rusting, cooking and digestion all rearrange atoms. None of them makes or destroys a single one. So if you count the atoms of each element before a reaction and after it, the counts must match. A chemical equation that shows this is called ==balanced==.

:::note Balanced equation
A chemical equation with the same number of atoms of each element on both sides of the arrow.
:::

Take hydrogen burning in oxygen to make water. A first try might be

:::eq
H₂ + O₂ → H₂O
:::

Count. Hydrogen: 2 on the left, 2 on the right. Fine. Oxygen: 2 on the left, 1 on the right. One oxygen atom has vanished, which is impossible, so this equation is not balanced. You may not fix it by changing H₂O into H₂O₂, because that is a different substance. You may only change the coefficients, the numbers of whole molecules. Two water molecules use both oxygen atoms; then they need four hydrogen atoms, so two hydrogen molecules:

:::eq
2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O
:::

Now there are 4 H and 2 O on each side. Methane, the main gas in a cooking-gas pipeline, burns the same way:

:::fig c4_balance_tiles() | Balancing the burning of methane, CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O. Every atom on the left turns up on the right: 1 carbon, 4 hydrogen and 4 oxygen on each side. The bonds are different; the atoms are the same ones.

So where does a burning log go? Mostly into the air. Wood is made largely of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, and when it burns those atoms join with oxygen from the air to make carbon dioxide and water vapour, which drift away as invisible gases. Only the small amount of mineral matter is left behind as ash. The rusting nail works the other way round: oxygen and water from the air join onto the iron and stay there as solid rust, so the nail gains their mass. Lavoisier's lesson in one line: if the mass seems to change, look for a gas you forgot to weigh.

:::home Lavoisier's experiment in a fizzy-drink bottle
**Part 1, open.** Put a glass on a kitchen scale, pour in about 100 mL of vinegar, and set one level teaspoon of baking soda on a piece of paper beside it, on the scale too. Note the total. Tip the soda into the vinegar (use a big glass: it fizzes hard), and when the fizzing stops, read the scale again with the paper still on it. The total has dropped by about 2 grams.

**Part 2, sealed.** Now do what Lavoisier did. You need an adult, an empty 1.5 or 2 litre plastic fizzy-drink bottle (made to hold pressure; never a glass bottle or a thin water bottle) and its cap. Pour in 100 mL of vinegar. Wrap *half* a teaspoon of baking soda in a twist of tissue, drop it in, and screw the cap on tight at once. Stand the bottle on the scale.

*Predict first:* while it fizzes, will the reading go down, go up, or stay the same? Write your guess.

Watch the reading as the fizzing runs its course. Then, with the bottle pointing away from everyone's face, unscrew the cap slowly. Listen to the hiss, and read the scale again.
:::

The sealed bottle does not change at all while it fizzes: every atom of the gas is still inside, pressing on the plastic. Only when the cap comes off, and the gas leaves, does the reading fall, by about a gram for half a teaspoon of soda. That hiss is the sound of Lavoisier's broken seal, two and a half centuries later.

:::careful
> "Gases don't weigh anything. They're just air."

Every gas is made of molecules, and every molecule has mass. A litre of the air around you has a mass of about 1.2 grams; the air in a small bedroom has a mass of about 30 kilograms, not far off your own. The bottle proves it: the scale only dropped when the gas was allowed out.
:::

:::careful
> "You can balance an equation by changing the little numbers until the counts match."

Changing a subscript changes the substance, so the equation stops describing the reaction you meant. CH₄ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O₂ might look easier, but hydrogen peroxide is not what comes out of a gas flame. Only the coefficients are yours to choose.
:::

:::try Investigation 4.3
Start with 1 H₂ + 1 O₂ → 1 H₂O and adjust the coefficients until both elements balance. Save one unbalanced and one balanced version. Then balance the formation of ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen yourself before you check.
:::

:::check
Nitrogen and hydrogen react to make ammonia. Why is 1 N₂ + 1 H₂ → 1 NH₃ unbalanced, and what coefficients balance it?
???
Left: 2 N and 2 H. Right: 1 N and 3 H. Neither element matches. Put 2 in front of NH₃ to fix nitrogen (2 N, 6 H on the right), then 3 in front of H₂ to supply 6 H: **N₂ + 3 H₂ → 2 NH₃**. Check: 2 N and 6 H on each side.
:::

## 4.4 What a balanced equation cannot tell us

:::scene London, 1781

Henry Cavendish was so shy that he left notes for his housekeeper rather than speak to her, and he ordered his dinner the same way. He was also one of the most careful experimenters who ever lived. In 1781 he filled a sealed glass vessel with a mixture of hydrogen (which he called "inflammable air") and ordinary air, and set it off with an electric spark. There was a flash, and the inside of the glass misted with dew. Cavendish collected the dew and tested it. It was pure water. Two years later, Lavoisier repeated the work and gave hydrogen its name, from the Greek for "water-maker".

You can write 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O in a few seconds, and it is perfectly balanced. Does that mean hydrogen and oxygen turn into water whenever they meet? No. Cavendish's gases sat quietly in their vessel until the spark. Mix the two gases in a bottle at room temperature, keep it dark and still, and you could wait years without finding a drop of water. Bring a spark near, and the whole mixture reacts in a fraction of a second, with a loud bang.

:::fig c4_spark() | The same balanced equation, two very different outcomes. Left: hydrogen and oxygen mixed at room temperature; nothing measurable happens. Right: a small spark, and the mixture reacts almost instantly. The equation describes the atoms, not the speed or the trigger.

A balanced equation is an accounting statement. It says: *if* these substances react to give these products, *then* the atoms are used in this ratio. It does not say whether the reaction actually happens, how fast it goes, whether it needs a spark or heat or a catalyst, or whether it gives out or takes in energy. Those questions need experiments, and they are what Chapter 5 is about.

The ratio, at least, is fixed. 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O, 4 H₂ + 2 O₂ → 4 H₂O and 6 H₂ + 3 O₂ → 6 H₂O all say the same thing: hydrogen molecules and oxygen molecules are used up two to one. We normally write the smallest whole numbers, which is the first version.

You can even write perfectly balanced equations for reactions that do not happen by themselves at all. Water does not split back into hydrogen and oxygen on its own, even though 2 H₂O → 2 H₂ + O₂ balances. To make that happen you have to push electricity through the water, as Nicholson and Carlisle did in Chapter 3 (and as you will see again in Chapter 8), supplying energy all the time. Whether a reaction goes is decided by energy and by speed, not by whether you can make the numbers match.

:::careful
> "Someone wrote 2 CO₂ → 2 CO + O₂, and it's balanced. So the carbon dioxide in our classroom must be turning into poisonous carbon monoxide."

Balancing only proves that the atoms *could* be accounted for. Carbon dioxide in ordinary air does not break down into carbon monoxide; it would need a great deal of energy to do that. Measuring the air (carbon monoxide detectors exist) is how you would check, not writing equations.
:::

:::home A rusting nail that eats air
*Predict first:* if iron really takes oxygen out of the air, how far will the water climb: not at all, all the way, or somewhere in between? Then push a small pad of plain steel wool (soap-free, from a hardware or paint shop; the shiny stainless-steel scrubbers in most Indian kitchens are made not to rust, so they will not work) into the bottom of a narrow glass, dampen it with a little vinegar (which strips its thin protective coating so it rusts faster), and stand the glass upside down in a saucer of water, so that air is trapped above the water. Mark the water level inside the glass with tape. Over the next few days the steel wool turns rusty brown, and the water slowly climbs up inside the glass, by about a fifth of the trapped air. The iron has joined with the oxygen, about a fifth of air, and taken it out of the gas. (Ask an adult before using steel wool: it can splinter, so handle it with gloves.) It takes days, not seconds: the balanced equation for rusting would never have told you that.
:::

:::check
What does the balanced equation N₂ + 3 H₂ → 2 NH₃ tell you, and what does it not tell you?
???
It tells you that if nitrogen and hydrogen react to make ammonia, they are used up in a 1 : 3 ratio of molecules and make 2 ammonia molecules for every nitrogen molecule, with every atom accounted for. It does not tell you whether the reaction happens at room temperature, how fast it is, whether it needs a catalyst (it does, and high pressure, in industry), or whether it gives out or takes in energy.
:::

## The case of the clever shortcut

:::case The case of the clever shortcut
Three students are asked to balance the burning of methane, CH₄ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O. Each hands in an answer where the atoms match.

- **Asha** writes: CH₄ + O₃ → CO₂ + H₄O
- **Bilal** writes: CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O
- **Chitra** writes: 2 CH₄ + 4 O₂ → 2 CO₂ + 4 H₂O

Clues:

1. Oxygen in the air is O₂ molecules. O₃ is ozone, a different gas.
2. The product you can collect from a gas flame condenses into ordinary water, H₂O.
3. Count the atoms on both sides of each answer.

Which answers are correct? Which is best, and what exactly is wrong with the other one?
???
Count first. All three answers balance: Asha has 1 C, 4 H, 3 O on each side; Bilal has 1 C, 4 H, 4 O; Chitra has 2 C, 8 H, 8 O.

But Asha changed subscripts. She turned the oxygen into ozone (O₃) and the water into "H₄O", which is not water and not anything you would find in a flame (clues 1 and 2). Her equation balances, but it describes a reaction that is not the one happening. It is wrong.

Bilal and Chitra both describe the real reaction. Chitra's is just Bilal's doubled, so the ratio is the same. Bilal's is the best answer because chemists write the smallest whole numbers: **CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O**.
:::
