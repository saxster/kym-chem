:::scene Glasgow, Scotland, around 1761

On a cold winter's day a young professor called Joseph Black set a pan of ice and a pan of ice-cold water side by side in a warm room, and watched two thermometers. The water warmed up steadily, as you would expect. The ice did something strange. Its thermometer sat stuck at the freezing point, hour after hour, while the ice slowly turned to water. Heat was pouring into it from the room, and yet it was getting no warmer. Where was all that heat going?

Black had been born in Bordeaux, in France, the son of a wine merchant from Belfast, and trained as a doctor. He was a quiet, cautious man who hated to publish anything until he was sure, and he lectured to packed rooms of medical students rather than write books. But he trusted his thermometers more than the ideas of his day, and that is what let him see the puzzle at all.

Black's answer was that melting itself swallows heat: the ice needed energy just to stop being ice. He did not know about molecules, and nor did anyone else in Glasgow. But he had caught the first clue to the question this chapter answers. Ask a few people what is inside the bubbles in boiling water, and some will say "air", some "oxygen", and some "hydrogen and oxygen, because the water is splitting up". **Predict first:** before you read on, decide what you think is in those bubbles, and keep your answer in mind. By the end of Section 3.3 you will have seen the evidence for yourself. Follow the particles of a substance as it melts, boils and spreads out, and you find that the particles themselves never change. What changes is how far apart they are, how fast they move, and whether the pulls between them can hold them together.

## 3.1 One substance, three states

Water is the only substance most of us see every day as a solid, a liquid and a gas: ice in the freezer, water in the tap, steam from the kettle. These are the three common ==states of matter==. You can tell them apart without any equipment. A solid keeps its own shape. A liquid flows and takes the shape of its container, but keeps the same volume: a litre of water is a litre whether it is in a bottle or a bowl. A gas spreads out to fill whatever space it is given, and can be squashed into a smaller space.

:::note States of matter
Solid, liquid and gas. The same substance can be in any of them, depending on its temperature and the pressure around it.
:::

The particle model explains all three with one picture. In a solid, the particles are packed closely together and each one vibrates about a fixed spot, held there by the attractions of its neighbours. In a liquid, they are still close together, which is why a liquid is hard to squash, but they move around and slide past each other, which is why it flows. In a gas, the particles are far apart compared with their own size and fly about freely, colliding with each other and with the walls.

Two things in that picture are invisible, and both are easy to get wrong. The first is the space between the particles. It is not filled with air, or with anything else: air is itself made of particles, and between *those* there is nothing at all. The second is the motion in a solid. The molecules in an ice cube are not frozen still; they shiver in place all the time, and the colder the ice, the smaller the shiver.

:::fig c3_states() | The same twelve molecules as a solid, a liquid and a gas. Only their arrangement and motion change. The molecules themselves are identical in all three boxes. (In a real gas the molecules would be much further apart than the box allows.)
:::live states

How much further apart are they in a gas? When water boils at 100 °C and normal pressure, the steam takes up about 1,700 times as much space as the liquid water it came from. A 250 mL cup of water makes roughly 400 litres of steam: enough to fill a bathtub. That enormous swelling is what James Watt and the engineers after him harnessed in steam engines, a few years after Black's ice experiment and in the same university town.

:::careful
> "When ice melts, the water molecules melt too, and when water boils, the molecules expand."

The molecules do not melt, swell or soften, and they do not stop moving when water freezes. Every H₂O molecule in steam is exactly the same size and shape as one in ice. What changes is the space between molecules and how freely they move. Water even shows that a solid is not always more tightly packed than its liquid: ice is slightly *less* dense than liquid water, because its molecules are held in an open, hexagonal pattern. That is why ice floats.
:::

:::home Watch heat vanish into ice
Put a few ice cubes and a little cold water in a glass, stir, and put a kitchen thermometer in it (or just feel the glass). Keep stirring now and then. As long as there is still ice in the glass, the temperature stays at about 0 °C, even though the room is pouring heat into it. Only when the last of the ice has melted does the water start to warm up. You are watching Joseph Black's puzzle: the heat is going into pulling the molecules out of their ice pattern, not into making them move faster.
:::

:::five The particle model
? What is it?
The idea that every substance is made of tiny particles that are always moving and that attract each other. Solid, liquid and gas are the same particles, arranged and moving differently.
? Why is it called this?
"Particle" comes from Latin *particula*, "a little part". It is called a model because it is a simplified picture built to explain what we observe, not a photograph.
? What problem did it solve?
Why does a solid keep its shape, a liquid flow but keep its volume, and a gas fill any space and squash easily? One picture explains all three.
? Why does it work?
In a solid, the attractions hold particles in place while they vibrate. In a liquid, they stay close but slide past each other. In a gas, they are far apart and fly freely. Heating or cooling changes which wins: the motion or the pull.
? How does it help?
It predicts what happens when you heat, cool or squeeze things: why ice melts at a fixed temperature, why gases can be squashed, and why steam takes up about 1,700 times the space of the water it came from.
?? How do we know?
Brownian motion shows the particles jostling (Chapter 0), and the heat that vanishes into melting ice, Joseph Black's puzzle, is just what pulling particles out of their pattern would need.
?? What it isn't
The space between the particles is not filled with air, or with anything else: it is empty. And the particles themselves do not melt, swell or stop moving.
?? Where does it stop working?
Some materials do not fit three neat states, such as glass, gels and toothpaste, and at very high temperatures matter becomes a fourth state, plasma, as in lightning and the Sun.
:::

## 3.2 Temperature and particle motion

:::scene Paris, 1 December 1783

Half of Paris had come to the gardens of the Tuileries Palace. Ten days earlier, two men had floated over the city under a huge paper-and-cloth balloon filled with hot air from a fire, built by the Montgolfier brothers: the first people ever to fly. Now a physicist named Jacques Charles was about to go one better. His balloon was filled not with hot air but with hydrogen, a gas much lighter than air, and he rose into the sky with his engineer Nicolas-Louis Robert, a thermometer and a barometer on board to take measurements. Charles flew for about two hours and came down safely, 36 kilometres away.

Charles spent the following years asking a quieter question: what does heat do to a gas? Warm a gas, he found, and it swells; and every gas he tried swelled by the same fraction for the same warming. To understand why, you need to picture the particles.

The particles in everything around you are always moving. In a gas they are fast. The nitrogen and oxygen molecules in the air of your room are zooming about at an average of roughly 500 metres per second, faster than a passenger jet. They do not get far: each one collides with another molecule billions of times a second.

What we feel as ==temperature== is connected to this motion. The hotter a gas is, the faster its particles move, on average. Not every particle moves at the same speed; some are always faster and some slower. But warm the gas and the whole crowd speeds up.

:::note Temperature
A measure of how hot something is. In a gas it goes up and down with the average energy of the particles' motion.
:::

Remember the two glasses from Chapter 0, with a drop of food colouring in each? Here is the inference you were reaching for. The water molecules are always moving and jostling the colour's molecules about, and in the warm glass they move faster, so the colour is shoved outwards sooner. Nobody stirs it: the molecules do the stirring.

Picture a gas sealed in a strong box. Its particles keep slamming into the walls, and all those tiny collisions add up to a steady push on the walls: the ==pressure== of the gas. Heat the box, and the particles move faster. They hit the walls more often and harder. So the pressure rises, even though the number of particles and the size of the box have not changed. (Let the walls move, as the skin of a balloon can, and instead of the pressure rising, the gas swells: Charles's discovery.)

:::note Pressure
The push of a gas (or liquid) on each square metre of surface. Measured in pascals (Pa); normal air pressure is about 101,000 Pa, or 101 kPa.
:::

:::fig c3_gas_temperature() | One mole of gas in a sealed 10-litre box, at 200 K and at 400 K. Doubling the kelvin temperature makes the molecules move faster (longer arrows) and doubles the pressure, from 166 kPa to 333 kPa. The number of molecules does not change.
:::live gasbox {"mode":"temperature"}

To make this rule work, temperature has to be measured from the right starting point, and finding that starting point was its own small adventure. In about 1702 the French physicist Guillaume Amontons noticed that air in a sealed container pushes harder when it is heated. A century later Charles and Joseph Gay-Lussac measured carefully how the volume (and, later, the pressure) of many gases changes with temperature. Plot either one against temperature, and every gas gives a straight line. Follow the lines backwards, and they all point to zero at the same temperature: about −273 °C. In 1848 William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, proposed a temperature scale that starts right there. Real gases turn to liquid before they get that cold, but the lines still point the way.

The ==kelvin== scale starts at absolute zero, −273.15 °C, the coldest anything can possibly be, where particle motion is as small as nature allows. A kelvin step is the same size as a degree Celsius, so to convert, you add 273.15. Water freezes at 273.15 K and boils at 373.15 K. Doubling the temperature *in kelvin* doubles the pressure of a gas in a sealed box. Doubling it in Celsius does not: going from 10 °C to 20 °C is only going from 283 K to 293 K.

:::note Kelvin (K)
A temperature scale that starts at absolute zero (−273.15 °C). Kelvin = degrees Celsius + 273.15.
:::

:::story temperature

:::home A balloon that breathes
Stretch a balloon over the neck of an empty plastic bottle. Stand the bottle in a bowl of hot tap water, or water an adult has mixed from the kettle (hot, but not boiling), for a few minutes, and the balloon slowly lifts and stands up as the air inside warms and swells. Move the bottle into a bowl of ice water, and the balloon sags again. Nothing has gone in or out: the same air molecules are simply moving faster, then slower. If you have a kitchen scale, weigh the bottle and balloon cold and again when warm, wiping the bottle dry each time. Predict first: will the warm one be heavier? (It weighs the same. Faster molecules take up more room, but there are no more of them, and none of them got heavier.)
:::

:::five Temperature
? What does it measure?
How hot something is. In a gas, it measures the average energy of the particles' motion: the hotter the gas, the faster its particles move on average.
? Why is it called this?
From Latin *temperatura*, "a mixing in the right proportion", from *temperare*, to mix or moderate. Galileo and later Boyle used the word for the degree of heat.
? What problem did it solve?
"Hot" and "cold" are feelings that differ from person to person. A temperature scale turns them into numbers anyone can check, and the kelvin scale gives a true starting point, absolute zero.
? Why does it work?
When a hot thing touches a cold one, energy passes from the faster-moving particles to the slower ones until both are at the same temperature. A thermometer settles at the temperature of whatever it touches, and reads its own.
? How does it help?
It lets you predict how gases behave: double the kelvin temperature of a sealed gas and its pressure doubles.
?? How do we know?
Plot the volume or pressure of a gas against temperature and every gas gives a straight line, all pointing to zero at about −273 °C, the start of the kelvin scale.
?? What it isn't
Not the same as heat. A bucket of warm water holds far more energy than a cup of hot tea, enough to melt much more ice, even though the tea is at a higher temperature.
?? Where does it stop working?
Temperature describes a crowd. A single molecule has a speed, not a temperature, and in any gas some molecules are always faster and some slower.
:::

## 3.3 Changing state is not changing identity

:::scene London, May 1800

News had just arrived from Italy that Alessandro Volta had built the first battery, a tall pile of metal discs and salty cardboard that pushed a steady electric current. Within weeks, two Londoners, William Nicholson and Anthony Carlisle, had built their own. They dipped its two wires into water, and bubbles of gas streamed off both of them: hydrogen at one wire, oxygen at the other, with twice as much hydrogen as oxygen. Electricity had torn water apart into two elements.

So could boiling do the same? Is that what is inside the bubbles in a kettle? The answer is no. Inside the bubbles in boiling water is water: water vapour, made of the same H₂O molecules as the liquid. When water boils, the molecules break away from their neighbours and spread out, but each molecule stays whole. Boiling, melting, freezing and condensing are ==changes of state==. They change how molecules are arranged, not what the molecules are.

:::note Change of state
Melting, freezing, boiling (evaporating) or condensing. The substance stays the same; only the arrangement and motion of its particles change.
:::

One small thing about the word "steam". Look closely at a boiling kettle: right at the spout there is a clear gap, and the white cloud starts a little further out. The clear gap is the real water vapour, and it is invisible, like air. The white cloud is made of tiny droplets of liquid water that have already cooled and condensed. So the bubbles in boiling water are full of something you cannot see at all: water as a gas.

The proof is in any kitchen. When steam from a pan meets a cold lid, drops of liquid form on it. They are water: they look and behave exactly like water. If boiling had split the molecules into hydrogen and oxygen, the steam would be a mixture of two gases that would not turn back into liquid on a cold plate, and that would explode if it met a flame. No amount of ordinary boiling does what Nicholson and Carlisle's battery did.

:::home Boil water without a flame (an adult does this one)
Hot water can scald, so this one is for an adult to do while you watch. You need a plastic syringe from the chemist (20 mL is ideal, no needle) and a blob of Blu-Tack. The adult pours some water that has just boiled into a cup, sucks up about 10 mL into the syringe, pushes out any air with the nozzle pointing down into the cup, and seals the nozzle firmly with the Blu-Tack. Then, holding the syringe in a cloth, they pull the plunger back hard and hold it. **Predict first:** with no flame, and water that is cooling down, can it boil?

It can. The water fills with bubbles and froths, as if it were on the stove. (It works best while the water is still very hot, so do it straight away; as it cools you have to pull harder.) Now let the plunger go: the bubbles vanish, back into liquid. At most a tiny bead of air may be left, which was dissolved in the water. Think about what that rules out. Nothing could get in through the sealed nozzle, and the big bubbles disappeared when squeezed, so they were not air. They turned straight back into liquid when squeezed, so they are not hydrogen and oxygen. And no heat was added, so heat did not "break" anything. Pulling the plunger gave the molecules room, and the fastest ones escaped into it, just as in a kettle. Bubbles in boiling water are water.
:::

So what does break when water boils? The pulls *between* molecules. Inside each molecule there are strong covalent bonds. Between neighbouring molecules there are much weaker attractions, called ==intermolecular forces==. Boiling needs only enough energy to overcome the weak ones. This is what Joseph Black's vanishing heat was doing: pulling molecules away from their neighbours.

:::note Intermolecular forces
Attractions between separate molecules. They are much weaker than the covalent bonds inside a molecule.
:::

:::fig c3_boil_vs_split() | Boiling and splitting are different changes. Boiling (left) separates whole H₂O molecules from each other, at a cost of about 41 kJ per mole: a bubble holds only whole H₂O molecules, with empty space between them. Splitting water into hydrogen and oxygen (right) breaks the bonds inside the molecules and makes new substances, and takes about seven times as much energy, about 286 kJ per mole.

The numbers show how different the two changes are. Boiling a mole of water (18 grams) at 100 °C takes about 41 kJ of energy. Breaking all the O–H bonds in that same mole, two per molecule, would take about 926 kJ, more than 20 times as much. (Splitting water into hydrogen and oxygen costs less than that, about 286 kJ, because new H–H and O=O bonds form and pay some of it back.) Steam at 100 °C is nowhere near hot enough to do that.

## 3.4 Squeezing a gas

:::scene Oxford, England, 1662

A century before Charles's balloon, in a house on the High Street in Oxford, Robert Boyle and his brilliant young assistant Robert Hooke were at work. Boyle was the fourteenth child of the Earl of Cork, one of the richest men in Ireland, and he could have lived an idle life. Instead he spent his money on glassware, furnaces and assistants, and argued, against most scholars of his day, that you learn how nature works by doing experiments and writing down exactly what happens, failures included. He and Hooke were busy with the most exciting machine in England: an air pump, which could suck the air out of a glass globe. Boyle was fascinated by what he called "the spring of the air": squeeze air, and it pushes back, like a spring.

To measure the spring, Boyle used a glass tube bent into the shape of a J, with the short end sealed. He poured mercury into the long open end, trapping a pocket of air in the short end. The more mercury he poured in, the harder it pressed on the trapped air, and the smaller the air pocket became. When he doubled the pressure, the air's volume halved. When he tripled it, the volume fell to a third. The rule is still called Boyle's law.

Push the handle of a bicycle pump while blocking the end with your thumb, and you can feel Boyle's spring of the air. Why does the air push back harder and harder?

Before going on, notice something easy to forget: the air in the pump has mass, like any other matter.

:::home Weigh some air
Find a football or basketball that is soft and needs pumping. Weigh it on a kitchen scale. **Predict first:** after you pump it up hard, will the scale read more, less or the same? Pump it up and weigh it again. A football pumped up from nearly flat gains about 3 to 5 g; one that was only a little soft gains less, perhaps a gram or two, so you need a scale that shows single grams. All of the extra is air you have squeezed in. Squashing a gas does not make it lighter or heavier; adding more of it does.
:::

The temperature has not changed much, so the molecules are moving at about the same speed as before. But now the same number of molecules is crowded into less space. Each bit of wall gets hit more often. Halve the volume, and the molecules hit the walls twice as often, so the pressure doubles. Double the volume, and the pressure halves. As long as the temperature and the amount of gas stay the same, ==pressure × volume stays the same==.

:::fig c3_boyle() | One mole of gas at 300 K. Halving the volume from 10 L to 5 L doubles the pressure; doubling it to 20 L halves the pressure. Pressure × volume stays the same along the whole curve. (Calculated with the ideal-gas rule, which real gases follow closely at these conditions.)
:::live gasbox {"mode":"volume"}

:::story gas-laws

Boyle's squeezing rule and Charles's warming rule can be combined into one statement, called the ==ideal gas law==: *P* × *V* = *n* × *R* × *T*. Here *P* is pressure, *V* volume, *n* the number of moles, *T* the kelvin temperature, and *R* is a fixed number, 8.314. You do not need to memorise it now. What matters is what it says: pressure goes up with more particles and higher temperature, and down with more space.

:::note Ideal gas law
*PV* = *nRT*. It links the pressure, volume, amount and kelvin temperature of a gas. It works well for most gases unless they are very squashed or very cold.
:::

:::work
1 mole of gas at 300 K in a 10-litre box:
*P* = *nRT* ÷ *V* = 1 × 8.314 × 300 ÷ 10 = 249 kPa (with *V* in litres, *P* comes out in kPa).
In 5 litres: 1 × 8.314 × 300 ÷ 5 = 499 kPa, twice as much.
In 20 litres: 125 kPa, half as much.
:::

The ideal gas law treats molecules as dots that never attract each other. That works because gas molecules are usually far apart. Squeeze a gas hard enough, or cool it enough, and the molecules get close enough for their attractions to matter. Then the gas stops following the rule, and eventually it turns into a liquid. That is how the butane in a lighter is stored as a liquid: squeezed under pressure in its little tank.

:::five Gas pressure
? What is it?
The push of a gas on each square metre of the walls around it, made by countless particles hitting the walls. Normal air pressure is about 101 kPa.
? Why is it called this?
From Latin *pressura*, "pressing", from *premere*, to press. Scientists began using the word for a push spread over an area in the 1650s, around the time of Boyle's experiments.
? What problem did it solve?
Boyle wanted to explain "the spring of the air": why squeezed air pushes back harder and harder. Pressure, pictured as particles hitting the walls, explains it.
? Why does it work?
Squeeze the same particles into half the space and each bit of wall is hit twice as often, so the pressure doubles. Heat them and they hit harder and more often, so the pressure rises again.
? How does it help?
With the ideal gas law, *PV* = *nRT*, you can predict the pressure in a tyre, a pressure cooker or a gas cylinder from its volume, the amount of gas and the temperature.
?? How do we know?
Boyle's J-shaped tube of 1662: double the pressure of the mercury on a trapped pocket of air, and its volume halves.
?? What it isn't
Not something a single particle has. One molecule hitting a wall is a tiny tap; pressure is the steady total of billions upon billions of taps.
?? Where does it stop working?
Squeeze or cool a gas enough and its particles get close enough to attract each other. Then the simple rule fails and the gas turns into a liquid, like the butane in a lighter.
:::

## 3.5 Where did the solute go?

:::scene Dandi, Gujarat, 6 April 1930

Early in the morning, after a march of 24 days and nearly 390 kilometres from his ashram at Sabarmati, Mahatma Gandhi walked down to the shore at Dandi and picked up a lump of natural salt left by evaporating seawater. It was a small act that shook an empire: the British government of India taxed salt and forbade Indians to make their own. Gandhi chose salt because everyone needed it, and because anyone could see where it came from. The sea is full of it, though you cannot see a single grain.

Stir a spoonful of salt into a glass of water, and in a minute or so the crystals vanish. The water looks exactly as clear as before. Has the salt gone?

**Predict first:** put a glass of water and a spoonful of salt (in its own little cup) on a kitchen scale together; then stir the salt in and put the empty cup back beside the glass. Will the reading be lighter, heavier or the same?

Taste it and you know the salt has not gone. It has ==dissolved==: its ions have separated and spread out among the water molecules, too small and too evenly mixed to see. The salt is the ==solute==; the water it dissolves in is the ==solvent==; together they make a ==solution==.

:::note Solute, solvent, solution
The solute is the substance that dissolves (salt); the solvent is what it dissolves in (water); the mixture is a solution.
:::

Try it, and the scale reads the same before and after: not one particle has left. The salt is still all there, just spread out too finely to see.

:::careful
> "The sugar melted in the tea."

It dissolved; it did not melt. Melting needs heat and involves one substance: sugar melts on its own in a hot pan at about 186 °C and turns into a sticky liquid, which soon starts to brown as it breaks down. Dissolving needs a second substance, and happens even in cold water: the sugar molecules come away from their crystal and spread out among the water molecules. Nor is dissolving a chemical reaction. Every sugar molecule is still a sugar molecule; let the water evaporate slowly and you get your sugar back.
:::

Dissolved particles are so small that they pass straight through filter paper, which easily stops grains of sand. But they are still there, and they can be counted. Dissolve 5.00 g of salt in water, pour it through filter paper, and nothing is caught. Heat the solution gently in a weighed dish until all the water has gone, and 5.00 g of white crystals are left behind: exactly the mass you put in. The salt pans of Gujarat's coast and the Rann of Kutch do the same thing on a vast scale, with the sun as the heater.

How much solute is in each bit of a solution is called its ==concentration==. Chemists usually measure it in moles of solute per litre of solution (mol/L). One mole of salt dissolved to make 2 litres of solution has a concentration of 0.50 mol/L. Add water to make 4 litres, and the same one mole is spread through twice the volume, so the concentration halves to 0.25 mol/L. This is called ==dilution==. Every sip is weaker, but the total amount of salt has not changed. You do this every time you make nimbu pani or Rooh Afza: a little concentrated syrup from the bottle goes into a big glass of water. All the syrup is still there, but spread through more water, so each sip is less sweet.

:::note Concentration
How much solute there is in each litre of a solution. Concentration = amount of solute ÷ volume of solution.
:::

:::fig c3_dilution() | The same eight solute particles in 1 litre and in 2 litres of solution. Adding water does not remove any solute, but spreads it out, so each litre (and each sip) holds half as much.
:::live dilution

:::eq
**concentration = amount of solute ÷ volume of solution**
:::

One small detail: the volume that counts is the final volume of the whole solution, not the volume of water you poured in. When you dissolve things, volumes do not always add up exactly, which is why chemists make solutions in special flasks with a single line marking an exact final volume.

:::five Dissolving
? What happens?
The particles of a solid such as salt or sugar come away from their crystal and spread out evenly among the water molecules, too small to see. The salt is the solute, the water is the solvent, and together they make a solution.
? Why is it called this?
From Latin *dissolvere*, "to loosen apart", from *solvere*, to loosen or untie. The crystal is loosened apart, not destroyed.
? What problem did it solve?
Where does salt go when it disappears into water? The answer, that every bit of it is still there, explains how the sea can hold salt you cannot see, and how salt pans get it back.
? Why does it work?
Water molecules are polar, so they pull on the charged ions of salt (and on sugar's slightly charged molecules), surround them and tug them away from the crystal. Their constant motion then spreads them through the whole solution.
? How does it help?
It lets you make solutions of any strength and work out their concentration, from a glass of nimbu pani to a hospital drip.
?? How do we know?
Weigh a glass of water and a spoonful of salt before and after stirring: the scale reads the same. Let the water evaporate and the salt comes back.
?? What it isn't
Not melting, which needs heat and only one substance, and not a chemical reaction: every sugar molecule is still a sugar molecule.
?? Where does it stop working?
Water can hold only so much. Keep adding salt and eventually no more dissolves: about 36 g in 100 g of water at room temperature.
:::

## 3.6 Intermolecular forces against thermal motion

Here is a puzzle that bothered chemists for a long time. Methane (CH₄), ammonia (NH₃) and water (H₂O) are all small molecules with almost the same mass: 16, 17 and 18 grams per mole. Each molecule even has exactly 10 electrons. Yet methane boils at −161.5 °C, ammonia at −33.3 °C, and water at +100 °C. Why such huge differences?

Water was the oddest of all. In most families of similar compounds, boiling point rises steadily with molecular size. The compounds of hydrogen with the elements in oxygen's column of the periodic table boil at about −60 °C (H₂S), −41 °C (H₂Se) and −2 °C (H₂Te). Follow that trend down to the smallest member, water, and you would expect it to boil far below zero. Instead it boils at +100 °C. If water followed the pattern, there would be no rivers, no oceans, no monsoon and no you.

Every state of matter is a contest. On one side are the attractions between molecules, pulling them together. On the other side is ==thermal motion==, the jiggling of heat, shaking them apart. A substance boils at the temperature where the jiggling becomes strong enough to tear molecules away from their neighbours. Strong attractions mean a high boiling point.

:::note Thermal motion
The constant random movement of particles. The higher the temperature, the more vigorous it is.
:::

There are three main kinds of attraction between molecules, from weakest to strongest (for small molecules like these):

- ==Dispersion forces==: the electrons in any molecule slosh around, making brief, flickering positive and negative patches that attract the patches on neighbouring molecules. Every molecule has these. Molecules with more electrons and more surface have stronger ones.
- ==Dipole attractions==: in polar molecules (Section 2.7) the permanent positive end of one molecule attracts the negative end of the next.
- ==Hydrogen bonds==: an especially strong dipole attraction. A hydrogen atom bonded to nitrogen, oxygen or fluorine is left very positive, and it is pulled towards a lone pair of electrons on an N, O or F atom of a neighbouring molecule.

:::note Dispersion forces
Weak attractions between all molecules, caused by electrons briefly bunching up on one side. Stronger for bigger molecules with more electrons.
:::

:::note Hydrogen bond
An attraction between a hydrogen atom attached to N, O or F and a nitrogen, oxygen or fluorine atom on a neighbouring molecule. Still far weaker than a covalent bond.
:::

The idea of the hydrogen bond grew up in the laboratory of G. N. Lewis at Berkeley, in California, where in 1920 two young chemists, Wendell Latimer and Worth Rodebush, suggested that a hydrogen atom could be shared, weakly, between two neighbouring molecules. Water's strange boiling point, and ammonia's and hydrogen fluoride's, were among the strongest clues that they were right.

:::fig c3_boiling_points() | Three molecules with 10 electrons each and almost the same mass, but very different boiling points. Methane has only dispersion forces. Ammonia and water form hydrogen bonds (dotted lines, right), and water, with two hydrogens and two lone pairs on each oxygen, forms the most.

Methane is non-polar and has no hydrogen attached to N, O or F, so only weak dispersion forces hold its molecules together: it boils at a very low temperature. Ammonia can form hydrogen bonds, so it boils much higher. Water forms them most of all: each molecule can make up to four, with its two hydrogens and the two lone pairs on its oxygen. So water stays liquid right up to 100 °C. Life on Earth depends on that.

:::story water

Hydrogen bonds sound strong, but remember the scale. Breaking an O–H bond inside a water molecule takes about 463 kJ per mole; a hydrogen bond between molecules is roughly 20 times weaker. That is why boiling separates whole molecules and never breaks them apart.

**Predict first:** almost every substance shrinks when it freezes, because the particles settle closer together. Water does the opposite: ice floats. Before you turn the model, decide what you think the molecules in ice must be doing to take up *more* room than the same molecules in liquid water.

:::fig i_waterice() | The same 72 water molecules as a liquid and as ice. In the liquid, hydrogen bonds keep breaking and re-forming, and molecules crowd close. In ice, each molecule is held by exactly four hydrogen bonds, at the corners of a tetrahedron, in open six-sided rings with empty channels through them, so the same molecules fill more space.
:::live waterice {}

Turn the ice until you are looking straight down the channels. Each oxygen is joined to four others, never more, and the four links point to the corners of a tetrahedron, like the legs of a camera tripod with one more leg pointing up. Four links that stiff cannot fold up tightly: they build a honeycomb with holes in it. In the liquid, the links break and re-form all the time (in real water, a hydrogen bond lasts only a few million-millionths of a second), so the honeycomb keeps collapsing and molecules slip into the holes. The same molecules end up closer together. That is why liquid water is denser than ice (about 1.00 g per mL against 0.917), and why ice floats with about nine-tenths of itself under the surface.

This one odd fact matters enormously. Lakes and ponds freeze from the top down, and the floating ice blanket keeps the water underneath liquid all winter, so fish and plants survive. If ice sank, cold lakes would fill up with ice from the bottom.


:::careful
> "When water boils, the heat breaks the bonds inside the water molecules."

Boiling overcomes the attractions *between* molecules: mostly hydrogen bonds in water. The covalent O–H bonds *inside* each molecule stay intact. Steam is made of whole H₂O molecules.
:::

:::check
Carbon dioxide (CO₂, 22 electrons per molecule) and hydrogen (H₂, 2 electrons) are both non-polar gases at room temperature. Solid CO₂ turns to gas at −78.5 °C, but hydrogen boils at −252.9 °C. Why the difference?
???
Neither has dipole attractions or hydrogen bonds, so only dispersion forces hold their molecules together. Dispersion forces are much stronger for bigger molecules with more electrons. CO₂, with 22 electrons, attracts its neighbours far more strongly than H₂ with 2, so it needs a much higher temperature to break free.
:::

## The case of the quadrupled pressure

:::case The case of the quadrupled pressure
An engineer seals 1.0 mol of methane gas in a steel cylinder closed by a piston, holding 10 litres at 200 K. The pressure gauge reads 166 kPa. Later, the gauge reads 665 kPa: four times as much. A worried colleague says: "The amount of gas must have quadrupled. Either something leaked in, or the methane has broken up into more, smaller molecules."

1. The sealed cylinder, piston and all, is weighed before and after. Its mass has not changed at all.
2. The log shows that the gas was warmed from 200 K to 400 K, and then the piston was pushed in until the gas filled only 5 litres.
3. A sample of the gas, cooled and analysed afterwards, is still pure methane, CH₄. Methane boils at 112 K, so at 200 K and 400 K it is a gas throughout.

Explain the fourfold rise in pressure without any new molecules. Which clue rules out each of the colleague's ideas?
???
Two changes each doubled the pressure. Doubling the kelvin temperature (200 K to 400 K) makes the molecules move faster, so they hit the walls harder and more often: pressure × 2 (Charles's and Amontons's discovery). Halving the volume (10 L to 5 L) crowds the same molecules into half the space: pressure × 2 again (Boyle's). Altogether, 166 × 2 × 2 ≈ 665 kPa, exactly what the gauge shows. Clue 1 rules out a leak: nothing was added. Clue 3 rules out breaking up: the molecules are unchanged. The number of molecules stayed at 1.0 mol the whole time.
:::
