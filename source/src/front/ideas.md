## Four ideas that run through everything

Chemistry can look like an enormous pile of separate facts. It is not. Almost every explanation in this book is built from four ideas, used again and again in different combinations. When something puzzles you, it is worth asking which of the four is at work.

:::raw
<ol class="ideas">
<li><b>Count everything.</b>An atom's number of protons decides which element it is. In ordinary chemical changes, atoms are never created or destroyed, and electric charge is never created or destroyed either. So you can always keep an account, like a shopkeeper's ledger, of where every atom and every charge went.</li>
<li><b>Opposite charges attract.</b>Positive and negative charges pull on each other; like charges push apart; both effects get weaker with distance. This electric attraction is what holds atoms together, holds atoms to each other, and holds molecules near their neighbours.</li>
<li><b>Jiggling against sticking.</b>Every particle is always moving, and the hotter something is, the harder its particles jiggle. Whether a substance is solid, liquid or gas, and whether a reaction gets going, depends on a contest between that jiggling and the attractions or barriers holding things back.</li>
<li><b>Huge numbers make steady averages.</b>Even a drop of water holds more molecules than there are grains of sand on all of Earth's beaches. With numbers that big, the random behaviour of single particles averages out into the steady, measurable things we see: a mass on a balance, the strength of a solution, a reaction that seems to have stopped but is secretly still going both ways.</li>
</ol>
:::

And underneath all four is a fifth habit: keep what you **observe** separate from the **model** you use to explain it. The observation is what any careful person would measure. The model is our best explanation so far, and it can always be improved by a better experiment.
