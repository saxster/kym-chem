## About the models and numbers in this book

### Where the numbers come from

Most numbers in this book are real measurements: boiling points, atomic masses, ionisation energies, bond lengths, the sizes of atoms, the voltage of a cell. They come from the standard reference collections that chemists everywhere rely on, such as the values published by IUPAC (the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry), NIST (the US National Institute of Standards and Technology) and the *CRC Handbook of Chemistry and Physics*. They have been rounded to make them easier to read, so you may find slightly different last digits in another book. That is normal: different sources measure in slightly different conditions, and round differently.

A few numbers are made up on purpose, to make a model easy to follow: the "12 u and 13 u" isotope mixture, the energy profiles drawn "in model units", and the reaction A ⇌ B with its neat rate constants. Whenever a number is invented, the text says so.

### The models, and where they break

**Counting models of atoms.** Drawing protons, neutrons and electrons as little balls is perfect for counting. It is useless for size (the real nucleus is tens of thousands of times narrower than the atom) and wrong about electrons, which are not little balls sitting in particular places.

**Shells.** Grouping electrons into shells (2, then 8, then 8) explains a great deal, from the families of the periodic table to the cliffs in ionisation energies. It works neatly only for the first 18 elements. Beyond that, and to explain the small dips in Figure 1.7, you need orbitals: the regions where electrons are likely to be found, described by quantum physics.

**Ideal gases.** The rule linking pressure, volume, temperature and amount treats gas particles as points that never attract each other. Real gases follow it well at everyday conditions, and badly when squeezed hard or cooled until they are close to turning into liquid.

**Average bond energies.** Adding up the energy to break bonds and the energy given out by making them gives good estimates of the energy of a reaction. But a bond's strength depends a little on the molecule it is in, so the answers are estimates, usually close but not exact.

**The rate picture of equilibrium.** Explaining equilibrium as forward and backward reactions running at equal speeds is true and useful. The simple rate rules in Chapter 5 hold for single-step reactions; most real reactions happen in several steps, and their rates are worked out by experiment.

**Diagrams in general.** Every picture in this book has been chosen to show one thing clearly. Colours, sizes and distances are almost always for clarity rather than accuracy, and atoms themselves have no colour at all. When you meet a new diagram, the best question to ask is the one this whole book is about: what does this show truthfully, and how do we know?
