## Further adventures

When you have finished this book, the adventure does not stop. Here are some places to go next, all free.

**MIT OpenCourseWare.** The Massachusetts Institute of Technology puts many of its courses online for anyone. A few of the ideas in this book were borrowed from them.

- [Chemistry Behind the Magic](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-hs-002-chemistry-behind-the-magic-chemical-demonstrations-for-the-classroom/): short videos of demonstrations that look like magic (glowsticks, colour-changing indicators, elements burning with coloured flames), each followed by the chemistry that explains it. These are for watching, not copying: the real experiments need a lab and a trained chemist.
- [Kitchen Chemistry](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/es-287-kitchen-chemistry-spring-2009/): a whole MIT course in which every week's experiment is something you eat, from bread and meringue to ice cream.
- When you are older: [Introduction to Solid-State Chemistry (3.091)](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/3-091sc-introduction-to-solid-state-chemistry-fall-2010/), where Professor Donald Sadoway tells the history of chemistry alongside the science, and [Principles of Chemical Science (5.111)](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/5-111sc-principles-of-chemical-science-fall-2014/), MIT's first-year chemistry course.

**In the lab app.** Every numbered section of this book has a matching investigation. Build atoms, squeeze gases and balance reactions yourself.

**In the kitchen.** Every *Try it at home* box in this book is a real experiment. Keep a notebook: write what you predicted, what happened, and what surprised you. That notebook is exactly what Lavoisier, Faraday and Marie Curie kept.
