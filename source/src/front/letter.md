## A letter to Kym

:::raw
<p class="salute">Dear Kym,</p>
:::

I should tell you something first: I am not a chemist. I don't know much chemistry at all. What I have tried to do is make the book I would have loved to read when I was your age: one that tells you who found each idea and how, that lets you try things in the kitchen, and that never just hands you a fact to learn by heart. Much of what is in here was new to me too. I also had help: I made this book with the help of AI, which searched out the research, wrote the chapters to my design, and then checked them. The explanations draw on the free courses and books that MIT and OpenStax share with the world, and on what researchers have learned about the ideas students most often get wrong. Every fact, date and number was checked against reliable sources, more than once, by checkers whose only job was to find mistakes, and every number was worked out again from scratch. Even so, a few mistakes may have slipped through. If something here doesn't match what you find somewhere else, don't simply trust the book: tell me, and we will find out together which is right. And where the people who really know chemistry still argue about something, the book says so.

Pick up a pencil and look at the grey tip. Now think of a diamond on a ring, flashing in the light. The pencil tip is so soft that it rubs off onto paper every time you draw. The diamond is hard enough to scratch glass. Yet they are made of the same stuff. The grey part of a pencil is mostly graphite, with a little clay mixed in to make it firmer, and graphite and diamond are both made of carbon atoms and nothing else. What makes them different is not what the atoms are but how they are joined. In graphite the atoms sit in flat sheets that slide over each other, so layers flake off onto the page as you draw. In diamond every atom is locked to four neighbours in a rigid three-dimensional frame, with no layers to slide.

That is the kind of surprise this book is full of. Chemistry is the study of what things are made of and how they change into other things. It explains why salt disappears in water and comes back as tiny cubes when the water dries up, why a cut apple goes brown, why a kettle of water can boil away without turning into anything new, why nimbu pani tastes sour, and how a battery pushes electricity through a wire. It explains you, too. The hydrogen in the water you drink was made in the first few minutes after the universe began. Most of the carbon, oxygen and iron in your body was made inside stars that lived and died before the Sun was born.

The things that do all this are far too small to see. If you could shrink yourself ten billion times, you would be about the size of a single atom. No microscope that uses light will ever show you one clearly, because atoms are thousands of times smaller than the waves of light themselves. So how do we know anything about them? That question, *how do we know?*, is the most important one in this book, and you will see it again and again. Every chapter tells you about real experiments: a beam of particles fired at gold foil, X-rays bounced off a salt crystal, gases weighed before and after they react. Each one taught people something about a world they could not see.

Because we cannot see atoms, every picture of them is a **model**: a careful drawing that shows some things truthfully and leaves other things out on purpose. A map of Mumbai is useful precisely because it is not as big as Mumbai. The diagrams in this book are maps like that. They are good for counting particles and thinking about what pushes and pulls on them. They are wrong about sizes, colours and distances, and I will tell you where each one stops being trustworthy. When a better experiment shows that a model is too simple, we do not throw it away in embarrassment. We improve it. That is how science works.

You will sometimes hear people say things like "sodium wants to lose an electron so it can have a full shell." Be suspicious of sentences like that. Atoms do not want anything. They have no wishes and no plans. Particles move the way they do because of forces (mostly electric attraction and repulsion), because they are always jiggling with heat, and because, in any ordinary chemical change, every atom is kept. This book tries to explain everything in those terms, and I think you will find the real explanations are more interesting than the made-up ones.

This is not a book of facts to memorise. It is an adventure story, and the heroes are real people: a French tax collector who weighed everything and lost his head in the Revolution, a Russian professor who left gaps in his table for elements nobody had found, a young man from a New Zealand farm who fired particles at gold and was astonished when some bounced back. Every idea in chemistry was found by someone who was puzzled, tried things, got them wrong, and tried again. When you see a word with a little book beside it, tap it, and you will get the whole story.

One more thing. Many sections ask you to make a prediction before you find out the answer. Please actually do it, in your head or on paper. A prediction that turns out wrong is not a failure. It is the most useful moment in learning, because it shows you exactly where your picture of the world needs to change. Nobody is grading you here. Take your time, go back whenever you like, and keep asking how we know.

With love,

:::raw
<p class="sign">Amar</p>
