question: Kitchen vinegar contains about 0.8 moles of acetic acid in every litre. Hydrochloric acid diluted to a tenth of that concentration is still far more acidic. How can the weaker-looking solution be the stronger acid?

Start with a trick. Chop a few leaves of red cabbage, soak them in warm water for ten minutes, and strain off the juice. It is a deep purple. Pour a little into three glasses. Into the first, squeeze some lemon: the purple turns a bright pink-red. Into the second, stir a pinch of baking soda: it turns blue, then green. The third you leave alone, and it stays purple. Nothing was heated, nothing was mixed except kitchen food, and yet the colours changed as if by magic.

If you have ever seen a yellow haldi stain on a white kurta turn red-brown when it was scrubbed with soap, you have already met a detector like this. The cabbage is not magic; it is a detector. Its purple colour comes from molecules called anthocyanins, and their colour depends on how many hydrogen nuclei they are carrying. Lemon juice pushes extra ones onto them; baking soda pulls them off. By the end of this chapter you will know exactly what is being pushed and pulled, and you will be able to read the cabbage like an instrument.

:::scene Oxford, 1664

People have used colour detectors like this for a long time. In 1664 the Irish-born scientist Robert Boyle, the fourteenth child of one of the richest men in Ireland, who spent his fortune on laboratories and insisted that every idea be tested by experiment, wrote a book about colours in which he noticed that syrup of violets turns red with sour substances and green with substances such as potash. He used it to sort substances into groups. Later chemists settled on litmus, a purple dye made from lichens, which turns red in acid and blue in its opposite. Your cabbage does the same job with a fuller rainbow.

Lemon juice, vinegar, tamarind and the acid in your stomach all taste sour, turn blue litmus red, fizz with baking soda and slowly eat into some metals. Chemists call substances like these ==acids==. Their opposites, which feel slippery and taste bitter, like soap or baking soda, are ==bases==. Your own blood sits on a knife edge between the two: its acidity has to stay within a very narrow range (a pH of 7.35 to 7.45, as you will learn to say in Section 7.3), even when your muscles pour acid into it during a sprint. This chapter explains what acids and bases actually are, why some are far fiercer than others, and how a solution can resist being made more acidic.

:::note Acid
A substance that can pass a proton (a hydrogen nucleus, H⁺) to another particle. Acids taste sour and turn litmus red. (Never taste anything in a laboratory.)
:::

Do not let "acid" make you think "danger" and "base" make you think "safe". The fiercest chemicals in most homes are bases: drain cleaner, caustic soda, and the chuna that goes into paan can all burn skin and eyes. Strong bases are every bit as dangerous as strong acids.

:::note Base
A substance that can accept a proton from an acid.
:::

## 7.1 What makes a solution acidic?

For a long time nobody knew what all acids had in common. Lavoisier, whom you met weighing everything in Chapter 4, guessed it was oxygen. He even named the gas for it: *oxygène* comes from Greek words meaning "acid-maker". It was a reasonable guess, and it was wrong. Around 1810 Humphry Davy, the discoverer of potassium from Chapter 1, showed that the acid in hydrochloric acid, then called muriatic acid, contains hydrogen and chlorine and no oxygen at all. The common ingredient was hydrogen. But plenty of substances contain hydrogen and are not acids (sugar is full of it), so what was special?

Here is the clue. Hydrogen chloride, HCl, is a gas. Dry, it is made of separate HCl molecules, and liquid HCl hardly conducts electricity at all. But bubble the gas into water and something dramatic happens: the solution, now called hydrochloric acid, conducts electricity very well. Charged particles have appeared that were not there before.

What happened is a transfer. Each HCl molecule hands its hydrogen nucleus, a bare ==proton==, over to a water molecule. The electrons that used to hold the H to the Cl stay behind on the chlorine. So the chlorine becomes a chloride ion, Cl⁻, and the water molecule, now with an extra proton, becomes H₃O⁺, a ==hydronium ion==.

:::note Hydronium ion (H₃O⁺)
A water molecule that has accepted an extra proton. More hydronium ions means a more acidic solution.
:::

:::eq
HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻
:::

:::fig c7_proton_transfer() | A proton passes from hydrogen chloride to a water molecule. The electrons that held it stay with the chlorine, which becomes a chloride ion. The water molecule becomes a hydronium ion. Every atom and every charge is accounted for.
:::live protontransfer {}

:::scene Copenhagen and Cambridge, 1923

In 1923, two chemists who were working separately, Johannes Brønsted in Copenhagen and Thomas Lowry in Cambridge, published the same idea within months of each other. **An acid is a proton donor, and a base is a proton acceptor.** That one sentence is the whole definition. In the reaction above, HCl is the acid and water is the base. The extra hydronium ions are what make a solution acidic: what turns litmus red, reacts with baking soda, and stings a cut. And it is what your cabbage detects: an acid passes protons to the anthocyanin molecules, a base takes them away, and each form of the molecule has a different colour.

The idea also explained something older theories could not: why ammonia, NH₃, which contains no OH, still behaves as a base. In water it takes a proton from a water molecule, making NH₄⁺ and leaving OH⁻ behind. Brønsted and Lowry's idea is still the one chemists use most every day.

Notice that "proton" here does not mean a proton torn out of a nucleus. A hydrogen atom is just one proton and one electron. Take away the electron and a bare proton is all that is left. The chlorine nucleus, with its 17 protons, does not change at all; nor does any other nucleus. Chapter 1's rule still holds.

:::careful
> "A bottle of hydrochloric acid is full of HCl molecules floating in water."

In ordinary dilute hydrochloric acid, almost none are left. Every HCl molecule has handed its proton to water, so the bottle is full of H₃O⁺ and Cl⁻ ions instead. Many people draw whole HCl molecules in the water; it is one of the commonest mistakes in chemistry, and Section 7.2 shows why it matters.
:::

:::careful
> "An acid loses a proton from inside one of its atoms' nuclei."

The proton that moves is the nucleus of a hydrogen atom, which is just a single proton. No nucleus is broken open, and no atom changes element. That would take a nuclear reaction, not an acid in a beaker.
:::

:::five Acids and bases
? What is it?
An acid is a substance that hands protons (bare hydrogen nuclei) to other particles, usually water. A base is a substance that takes them. Lemon juice and vinegar are acids; baking soda, soap and chuna are bases.
? Why is it called this?
*Acid* comes from Latin *acidus*, "sour", because the ones people met first tasted sour (never test one by tasting). *Base* was used by the French chemist Rouelle in 1754 for the substance that gave a salt its solid "base". *Alkali*, the old word for a strong base, comes from the Arabic *al-qalī*, the ashes of saltwort, a plant of salty soils.
? What problem did it solve?
For centuries acids were known only by their effects: sour taste, colour changes, metals fizzing. Nobody knew what they had in common; Lavoisier guessed oxygen and was wrong. The proton idea of Brønsted and Lowry in 1923 finally explained them all, and bases with them.
? Why does it work?
A hydrogen atom stripped of its electron is a single proton, small and easily passed on. Acids let go of such protons; bases have a spare pair of electrons that one can bond to. Every acid–base reaction is a proton changing hands.
? How does it help?
It explains why acids and bases cancel each other out, why your stomach, your blood and the sea care so much about acidity, and how to make, test and handle them safely.
?? How do we know?
Dry hydrogen chloride hardly conducts electricity, but dissolved in water it conducts well: the protons have moved to the water, making ions.
?? What it isn't
It is not "acids are dangerous and bases are mild". Strong bases such as drain cleaner and caustic soda are just as dangerous as strong acids.
?? Where does it stop working?
Proton-passing covers almost everything in this book, but chemists also use a wider idea, built on electron pairs, for reactions with no protons at all.
:::

:::check
When ammonia dissolves in water, NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻. Which molecule is the acid (proton donor), and which is the base (proton acceptor)?
???
Water is the acid: it gives a proton to ammonia, leaving OH⁻. Ammonia is the base: it accepts the proton and becomes NH₄⁺. Water can act as an acid or as a base, depending on its partner.
:::

## 7.2 Strong or concentrated?

:::scene Uppsala, Sweden, 1884

Five years before his energy barrier of Chapter 5, the young Svante Arrhenius spent months passing electric currents through solutions and measuring how well they conducted. He found that, at the same concentration, hydrochloric acid conducted electricity far better than acetic acid, the acid in vinegar. His explanation was bold: in water, some substances split into charged ions, and some split much more than others. This was the thesis his examiners nearly failed, and the idea that won him the Nobel Prize in 1903.

Here is the answer to this chapter's big question. Hydrochloric acid and acetic acid both give protons to water, but they do it to very different extents.

In hydrochloric acid, essentially *every* HCl molecule gives up its proton. Chemists call it a ==strong acid==. Acetic acid, CH₃COOH, is a ==weak acid==: in vinegar, only a few molecules in every thousand have handed over a proton at any moment. The rest stay whole. The transfer goes both ways, molecules giving and taking protons back all the time, and it settles into a balance (the dynamic equilibrium of Chapter 5) in which most acetic acid molecules are intact.

:::note Strong acid
An acid that gives up its protons to water almost completely, such as hydrochloric acid.
:::

:::note Weak acid
An acid that gives up its protons to water only partly, so most of its molecules stay whole, such as acetic acid.
:::

So "strong" and "concentrated" are answers to two different questions. **Concentration** asks how much acid was dissolved in each litre (Section 6.6). **Strength** asks how readily the acid's molecules give up their protons, and that is fixed for each acid: at the same concentration, a strong acid hands over all of them and a weak acid only a small fraction. At fixed temperature and solvent, compare Ka. Percentage ionization depends on concentration too; neither molecules nor atoms have intentions.

Here is another way to hold it, which will pay off in the case at the end of the chapter. A weak acid keeps most of its protons in the bank, locked up in whole molecules. The hydronium ions are the cash in hand. A pH meter (Section 7.3) counts only the cash. A titration (Section 7.4) counts the whole fortune, bank and all.

:::fig c7_strong_weak() | Two acids, seen as particles. In dilute hydrochloric acid every molecule has passed its proton to water. In concentrated acetic acid there are far more acid molecules, but nearly all of them are still whole. The counts are illustrative: in real 1 mol/L acetic acid only about 4 molecules in every 1,000 have given up a proton.
:::live weakacid {}

Put numbers on it, using measured values. Hydrochloric acid at 0.010 mol/L gives 0.010 mol/L of hydronium ions, because every molecule takes part. Acetic acid at 1.00 mol/L, a hundred times more concentrated, gives only about 0.0042 mol/L of hydronium ions, because only about 0.42% of its molecules take part. The dilute strong acid has more than twice as many hydronium ions per litre as the concentrated weak one. Arrhenius's meter was really counting those ions; today a pH meter (Section 7.3) measures the hydronium concentration directly, and for 1.00 mol/L acetic acid it reads that same 0.0042 mol/L.

Strong acids are among the most useful chemicals in the world, for making fertilisers, dyes, medicines and metals, and for a long time India had to import them.

:::scene Calcutta, 1901

Prafulla Chandra Ray, a chemistry professor at Presidency College in Calcutta (now Kolkata), wanted that to change. He had studied in Edinburgh, came home determined that Indian science should serve India, and lived so simply that he is said to have given much of his salary away. In 1892, with a few hundred rupees, he started a small chemical works, and in 1901 it became the company Bengal Chemical and Pharmaceutical Works, which grew into one of the first big Indian companies to make medicines and laboratory chemicals, acids among them, in India itself. He is often called the father of Indian chemistry.

:::story pc-ray

Back to the kitchen. That is why vinegar is safe on your salad. Kitchen vinegar is roughly 0.8 mol/L acetic acid, but it has only about 0.004 mol/L of hydronium ions. Hydrochloric acid at a tenth of vinegar's concentration would have about 0.08 mol/L, twenty times more, and would be dangerous to drink.

:::careful
> "If you add enough water to a strong acid it becomes a weak acid."

Adding water makes an acid more *dilute*, never weaker. Every HCl molecule still gives up its proton; there are just fewer of them per litre. Strength belongs to the acid itself, so concentrated acetic acid is still a weak acid, and diluted acetic acid is still a weak acid too. (Diluting a weak acid actually lets a slightly bigger fraction of its molecules give up their protons, because the balance shifts, but the solution still ends up less acidic than before.)
:::

## 7.3 Every step on the pH scale is tenfold

:::scene Copenhagen, 1909

At the Carlsberg Laboratory, run by the famous beer company, the Danish chemist Søren Sørensen was studying how proteins and enzymes behave during brewing. He found that they were exquisitely sensitive to acidity, and he needed a simple way to write it down. The trouble was the numbers. Strong acid might have 1 mole of hydronium per litre; pure water has one ten-millionth of a mole per litre, 0.000 000 1 mol/L; a strong base might have a ten-millionth of that, 10⁻¹⁴ mol/L. Writing those out is clumsy, so in 1909 Sørensen invented a shortcut: the ==pH== scale. In the same year, Fritz Haber and Zygmunt Klemensiewicz described the glass electrode, the heart of the pH meters still used today.

:::note pH
A number that says how acidic a solution is, by counting the powers of ten in its hydronium concentration. pH 7 is neutral; lower is more acidic; higher is more basic.
:::

The idea is to count powers of ten. Write the hydronium concentration as a power of ten, then take the number on top and drop the minus sign:

:::work
0.1 mol/L = 10⁻¹ mol/L → pH 1

0.01 mol/L = 10⁻² mol/L → pH 2

0.001 mol/L = 10⁻³ mol/L → pH 3

0.000 000 1 mol/L = 10⁻⁷ mol/L → pH 7 (pure water at 25 °C)
:::

Three things follow, and all three surprise people.

**Lower pH means more acidic.** Because of that dropped minus sign, pH 1 has *more* hydronium than pH 3.

**Each step of 1 is a factor of ten.** pH 2 has ten times more hydronium than pH 3, and pH 1 has a hundred times more than pH 3. The pH scale is not like a ruler where each centimetre is the same size; it is like the powers-of-ten scale in Chapter 0.

**In-between numbers are allowed.** Acetic acid at 1.00 mol/L has 0.0042 mol/L of hydronium, which lies between 10⁻³ and 10⁻², so its pH lies between 3 and 2. A calculator gives 2.37. (Mathematicians call this power-counting a *logarithm*; you do not need the word yet.)

:::figwide c7_ph_ladder() | The pH scale with some everyday solutions, and the colours red cabbage juice turns along it. Every step to the left multiplies the hydronium concentration by ten: the dots below count the hydronium ions at pH 7, 6, 5 and 4 against pure water's one. Values are typical and vary from sample to sample.

:::story ph

Pure water is neutral, pH 7, because water molecules pass protons to each other very slightly: a tiny number become H₃O⁺ and an equal number become OH⁻. Acids push the hydronium concentration up and the pH below 7. Bases mop up hydronium, so the pH rises above 7. Your red cabbage juice is a rough pH meter. It is red around pH 2, pink-purple around pH 4, purple near neutral, blue around pH 8, green by pH 10 and greenish-yellow by pH 12. Notice what it reads: pH, not strength. Lemon juice and vinegar are both *weak* acids, yet both turn it red, because both have plenty of hydronium for the cabbage to see.

:::home A red cabbage rainbow
Ask an adult to help you make red cabbage juice: chop a quarter of a red cabbage, cover it with warm tap water, leave it for 10 minutes, then strain the purple liquid into a jug. Set out five clear glasses with a little juice in each. Add, one to each glass: a squeeze of lemon juice, a spoonful of white vinegar, nothing (plain juice), a pinch of baking soda, and a bigger spoonful of baking soda. (More baking soda deepens the blue-green but cannot push the pH much past 8, so do not expect yellow. If an adult has washing soda, the laundry powder, they can add a pinch to a sixth glass for a clear green; it stings eyes and skin, so it is theirs to handle.) Before each one goes in, say out loud what colour you expect. Line them up from most acidic to most basic and you have a pH rainbow. Can you guess where milk, sugar water or salt water will land before you test them?
:::

:::careful
> "pH 2 is twice as acidic as pH 4."

pH 2 has 10⁻² mol/L of hydronium and pH 4 has 10⁻⁴ mol/L. That is a difference of two powers of ten: 10 × 10 = 100 times more hydronium, not twice as much.
:::

:::five pH
? What is it?
A single number that says how acidic or basic a solution is: 7 is neutral, lower is more acidic, higher is more basic. Each step of one means ten times more or less hydronium, the particle that makes a solution acidic.
? Why is it called this?
Søren Sørensen invented it in 1909. The H stands for hydrogen. What the p stood for, he never explained, and people still argue: perhaps "power" (*Potenz* in German, *puissance* in French), perhaps "potential", perhaps nothing at all.
? What problem did it solve?
Acidity was written as clumsy numbers like 0.000 000 1 moles per litre. Sørensen, studying brewing, needed a quick, easy way to record it. Counting the powers of ten gave one.
? Why does it work?
It counts the powers of ten in the hydronium concentration: 10⁻³ mol/L becomes pH 3. A huge range of numbers shrinks to a short, friendly scale.
? How does it help?
Gardeners, doctors, swimming-pool cleaners and chemists all use it to keep things in the right range: soil, blood, pool water, a reaction in a flask.
?? How do we know?
A pH meter, built on the glass electrode of 1909, measures it directly; indicators like red cabbage juice change colour along the scale.
?? What it isn't
It is not an ordinary ruler. pH 2 is not twice as acidic as pH 4; it has a hundred times more hydronium.
?? Where does it stop working?
The scale is not fenced in at 0 and 14: very concentrated acids can go below 0. And "7 is neutral" is exact only at 25 °C; neutral pure water is a little below 7 when it is warmer.
:::

:::check
You have 1.0 L of a strong acid at pH 2. How much water must you add to raise its pH to 4?
???
Going from pH 2 to pH 4 means a hundred times less hydronium per litre, so the acid must be spread through 100 times the volume: a total of 100 L. You need to add 99 L of water. (This works for a strong acid, where every molecule has already given up its proton.)
:::

## 7.4 Neutralization and titration

How do you find out how much acid is in an unlabelled bottle? French chemists worked out the answer in the decades around 1800, when bleach and soda makers needed to know exactly how strong their solutions were. Around 1791 François Descroizilles built an early graduated tube for adding a liquid little by little, and in 1824 Joseph Gay-Lussac, whom you met in Chapter 6 measuring gas volumes, refined it and gave it its name: the burette. You let the unknown acid react with a base whose concentration you already know, adding the base drop by drop from the burette and measuring the pH as you go. This is a ==titration==. The base used is usually sodium hydroxide, NaOH, which gives OH⁻ ions; each OH⁻ grabs one proton from the acid to make a water molecule.

:::note Titration
Adding a solution of known concentration, a little at a time, to measure exactly how much of something is in another solution.
:::

Figure 7.4 shows two titrations, calculated from measured constants. Each flask holds 25 mL of acid at 0.10 mol/L, which is 2.5 thousandths of a mole (2.5 mmol). The burette holds 0.10 mol/L sodium hydroxide, so it takes exactly 25 mL of base to match the acid proton for proton. That point is called the ==equivalence point==.

:::note Equivalence point
The moment in a titration when just enough base has been added to react with all of the acid.
:::

*Predict first:* before you look at Figure 7.4, take a pencil and sketch what you think the pH does as the base goes in, from 0 mL to 40 mL. A straight line climbing steadily? Something else? Most people draw a straight line.

:::figwide c7_titration() | Calculated titration curves: 25 mL of 0.10 mol/L acid with 0.10 mol/L sodium hydroxide. Both acids need 25 mL to reach the equivalence point. Near it, a single drop sends the pH leaping upwards. The weak acid's curve has a long, gently sloping stretch, its buffer region, shaded.
:::live titration {}

It is not a straight line. Both curves stay low at first and then shoot up almost vertically near 25 mL. The flat start does not mean nothing is happening: every drop of base is reacting, but there is so much acid left that each drop's OH⁻ is grabbed at once and the hydronium hardly changes. Only the last few drops find no acid left to react with, so the OH⁻ piles up. But look at the middle of the weak acid's curve. For a long stretch, adding base barely changes the pH. That stretch is a ==buffer==.

## 7.5 Buffers: ratio and reserve

Two equal-ratio buffers can start at the same pH but have different reserves. Compare 3/3 mmol with 1.5/1.5 mmol HA/A⁻: after 1 mmol OH⁻, the amounts are 2/4 versus 0.5/2.5 mmol. The smaller reserve changes more. Starting pH is not capacity.

:::note Buffer
A mixture of a weak acid and the particle it forms when it gives up a proton (here, acetic acid and acetate). It resists changes in pH when small amounts of acid or base are added.
:::

Here is why. Halfway through, half the acetic acid has been turned into acetate ions, CH₃COO⁻, so the flask contains plenty of both. Any OH⁻ you add is grabbed by a whole acetic acid molecule (CH₃COOH + OH⁻ → CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O) before it can pile up. Any extra acid you add is grabbed by an acetate ion. The pH depends on the *ratio* of acetic acid to acetate, and adding a little of either only nudges that ratio. At exactly halfway, when the two amounts are equal, the pH is 4.74, a number that is fixed by how strong acetic acid is.

:::work
**The same base, two different destinations.** Start with 50 mL of an acetate buffer containing 3.0 mmol of acetic acid and 3.0 mmol of acetate (pH 4.74). Add 10 mL of 0.10 mol/L sodium hydroxide, which is 1.0 mmol of OH⁻.

In the buffer: acetic acid falls to 2.0 mmol and acetate rises to 4.0 mmol. The ideal pH rises from 4.74 to about 5.05, a change of about 0.30. Formal HA exhaustion for this buffer is 30 mL; the separate 2.5 mmol acid titrations reach equivalence at 25 mL.

In 50 mL of pure water instead: the 1.0 mmol of OH⁻ has nothing to react with. The pH jumps from 7.0 to about 12.2.
:::

:::scene Boston, 1908

The American physician and chemist Lawrence Henderson wanted to know how blood keeps its acidity so steady. In 1908 he showed that it is a buffer of exactly this kind, built from carbonic acid and hydrogencarbonate ions (the same hydrogencarbonate that is in baking soda), and he wrote down the rule linking the pH to the ratio of the two. It is why a hard sprint, which pours acid into your blood, changes its pH by so little.

:::home A buffer race
You can feel Henderson's buffer at work with the very same pair of particles. Pour a little red cabbage juice into two glasses and top each up with half a cup of water. Into glass B only, stir a small pinch of baking soda (it turns blue). Now add lemon juice to both glasses, one drop at a time, counting the drops.

*Predict first:* which glass will turn pink first, and by how much?

Glass A turns pink within a handful of drops. Glass B drifts from blue to purple and then holds its colour stubbornly for dozens more drops, because each drop of acid is being mopped up by hydrogencarbonate. Only when that runs out does it give way and turn pink. A buffer, its capacity, and the end of its capacity, all in one glass.
:::

:::story buffers

:::careful
> "A buffer stops the pH from changing at all."

A buffer slows the change; it does not stop it. Every bit of base you add uses up some of the weak acid. Once the weak acid runs out, the pH climbs as fast as in plain water. That is exactly what happens near the equivalence point in Figure 7.4. How much a buffer can absorb before it gives way is called its *capacity*.
:::

:::home A kitchen titration
Put a quarter of a cup of red cabbage juice in a glass and stir in half a teaspoon of baking soda: it turns blue-green. Now add white vinegar one teaspoon at a time, stirring and counting. (It will fizz: that is carbon dioxide.) Write down the colour after each spoonful. For the first few spoonfuls the colour hardly moves, but look at the fizz: every spoonful is reacting, using up some of the soda. Then, when the soda is nearly gone, the colour swings through purple to pink. Repeat with a whole teaspoon of baking soda. Does it take about twice as many spoonfuls? You have just done a titration, with a spoon for a burette and a cabbage for a pH meter.
:::

:::five Buffer
? What is it?
A mixture of a weak acid and the particle it forms when it gives up a proton. It soaks up small amounts of added acid or base, so the pH hardly changes.
? Why is it called this?
After the cushioning pads, like the buffers at the end of a railway line, that soften a knock. The French chemists Fernbach and Hubert described the effect as a *tampon* (a cushion or pad) in 1900; it became *Puffer* in German and *buffer* in English.
? What problem did it solve?
Living things only work in a narrow range of acidity. Henderson, in 1908, wanted to know how blood stays so steady when the body keeps making acid. Buffers were the answer.
? Why does it work?
The weak acid mops up any base that arrives, and its partner particle mops up any acid. Both stay in the solution, ready for the next drop.
? How does it help?
It keeps your blood, the oceans and many medicines and foods at a safe, steady pH, and lets chemists hold a reaction at the acidity they need.
?? How do we know?
Add the same drops of acid to plain cabbage juice and to cabbage juice with a pinch of baking soda: the plain one changes colour almost at once, the buffered one holds out.
?? What it isn't
It does not stop the pH from changing at all. It only slows the change.
?? Where does it stop working?
Every buffer has a limit. Once its weak acid or its partner particle is used up, the pH changes as fast as in plain water. Each buffer also works best near its own particular pH.
:::

:::check
Why does a mixture of acetic acid and acetate resist a change in pH when a small amount of sodium hydroxide is added?
???
The added OH⁻ ions react with intact acetic acid molecules, CH₃COOH + OH⁻ → CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O, instead of staying free in the solution. The amounts of acetic acid and acetate only change a little, so their ratio, and hence the pH, changes only a little. It works until the acetic acid runs out.
:::

## The two bottles at pH 3

:::case The two bottles at pH 3
Two unlabelled flasks both read exactly pH 3.00 on a pH meter. One contains hydrochloric acid and the other acetic acid. You take 25 mL from each and titrate it with 0.10 mol/L sodium hydroxide.

1. Flask A reaches its equivalence point after only 0.25 mL of sodium hydroxide, a few drops.
2. Flask B needs 14.1 mL of sodium hydroxide to reach its equivalence point.
3. After 5.0 mL of sodium hydroxide, Flask A reads about pH 12.2 and Flask B about pH 4.5.

Which flask holds which acid? Both have the same pH, so how can one contain about 57 times more acid than the other?
???
Flask A is hydrochloric acid and Flask B is acetic acid.

Equal pH means equal concentrations of hydronium ions, 10⁻³ = 0.0010 mol/L. It does not mean equal amounts of acid. Hydrochloric acid gives up every proton, so it only needs 0.0010 mol/L of acid to reach pH 3: 25 mL holds 0.025 mmol, matched by 0.25 mL of the base (clue 1). Acetic acid gives up only a small fraction of its protons, so to reach the same hydronium concentration it must be about 0.057 mol/L: 25 mL holds about 1.41 mmol, which needs 14.1 mL of base (clue 2). That is about 57 times more acid, almost all of it hiding as whole molecules that the pH meter cannot see.

Clue 3 shows the difference in action. After 5.0 mL, Flask A's tiny amount of acid is long gone and the extra OH⁻ sends the pH soaring. In Flask B the base has turned some acetic acid into acetate, making a buffer, so the pH has only crept up to about 4.5.
