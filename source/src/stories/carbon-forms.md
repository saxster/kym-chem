:::scene Paris, 1772

On a summer day in 1772, in a garden in Paris, a crowd gathered to watch a diamond disappear. Antoine Lavoisier and his colleagues had set up an enormous magnifying glass, a giant burning lens, to focus sunlight onto a small diamond. As the spot of light grew blinding, the diamond slowly vanished. Many people believed diamonds could not be destroyed by fire. They were wrong.

When the scientists repeated the experiment inside a sealed glass jar, they found that the diamond turned into a gas: the same "fixed air" that charcoal makes when it burns, which we now call carbon dioxide. A precious stone and a lump of charcoal seemed to share something. But what?

#### Same mass, same gas

:::scene London, 1797

Twenty-five years later an English chemist, Smithson Tennant, settled it with a careful measurement. He burned diamond and he burned charcoal, weighed each carefully, and measured the carbon dioxide each one made. Equal masses of diamond and of charcoal produced exactly the same amount of carbon dioxide. There was only one possible conclusion: diamond is pure carbon. So is the graphite in a pencil.

That is one of the most surprising facts in all of chemistry. A diamond is the hardest natural substance known, transparent, sparkling, and it does not conduct electricity. Graphite is so soft it rubs off onto paper, it is grey and shiny, it feels slippery, and it does conduct electricity. Same atoms. Completely different substances.

#### Same atoms, different arrangement

The difference, as the X-ray crystallographers of the twentieth century confirmed, is entirely in how the carbon atoms are joined.

1. **In diamond**, every carbon atom is bonded to four others, pointing to the corners of a tetrahedron. The bonds run in every direction through the whole crystal, so a diamond is one giant molecule with no weak places. To scratch it you would have to break strong carbon–carbon bonds, which is why almost nothing can.
2. **In graphite**, every carbon atom is bonded to only three others, in flat sheets of hexagons like chicken wire. Within a sheet the bonds are very strong. But the sheets are held to each other only by weak attractions, 0.335 nanometres apart, so they slide over each other easily. When you draw with a pencil, sheets of graphite slide off and stick to the paper.
3. **Graphite conducts electricity** because each carbon uses only three of its four outer electrons for bonds; the fourth is free to wander along the sheet. In diamond all four are locked in bonds.

Different forms of the same element are called **allotropes**. Oxygen has them too: the O₂ we breathe and ozone, O₃, which protects us from ultraviolet light.

:::fig c2_diamond_graphite() | Two ways of joining carbon atoms. In diamond every atom is bonded to four neighbours in a three-dimensional network. In graphite, flat sheets of hexagons slide over each other.

The name graphite comes from the Greek word for "to write", given in 1789. People had been writing with it for over two hundred years before that, ever since a huge deposit of unusually pure graphite was found in Borrowdale, in the north of England, in the 1500s.

#### A football made of carbon

:::scene Houston, Texas, 1985

For two hundred years chemists believed carbon came in just those two pure forms. Then, in 1985, three scientists, Harry Kroto from England and Richard Smalley and Robert Curl from Texas, were blasting graphite with a laser to study the kinds of carbon molecules that might exist in space. Again and again their instruments showed a very stable molecule with exactly 60 carbon atoms.

What shape could hold 60 carbon atoms with no loose ends? They built paper models, and the answer turned out to be the pattern on a football: 12 pentagons and 20 hexagons, curved into a hollow ball. They named it buckminsterfullerene, after the American architect Richard Buckminster Fuller, whose famous geodesic domes have the same kind of pattern. Most people just call it a buckyball. The three won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1996.

#### A single sheet

:::scene Manchester, 2004

The newest chapter began with a roll of sticky tape. In Manchester, Andre Geim and Konstantin Novoselov liked to spend Friday evenings trying odd experiments. They pressed sticky tape onto graphite, peeled it off, then stuck the tape to itself and peeled again, splitting the flakes thinner and thinner. Eventually they had flakes just one atom thick: a single sheet of graphite, called graphene.

Graphene turned out to be astonishing: about one atom thick, yet much stronger for its weight than steel, and an excellent conductor of electricity. Geim and Novoselov won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2010. You make a little graphene, tangled up with thicker flakes, every time you draw with a pencil.

:::careful
> "Diamonds last forever because carbon cannot burn."

Diamond is carbon, and carbon burns, as Lavoisier showed with his lens. Diamond lasts in everyday life because at room temperature its strong bonds need a great deal of energy to break, so the reaction with oxygen is far too slow to notice. Heated hot enough in air, a diamond burns away to carbon dioxide.
:::

:::home Feel the sheets slide
Scribble hard with a soft pencil (2B or softer) on a scrap of paper until you have a thick, shiny grey patch. Rub it with your fingertip. It feels smooth and slippery: you are feeling sheets of carbon atoms sliding over each other. This is why powdered graphite is used to make stiff locks turn smoothly. Then think of a diamond, made of the very same atoms, which can cut glass.
:::
