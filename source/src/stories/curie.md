:::scene Warsaw, 1870s

Maria Skłodowska was born in Warsaw in 1867, the youngest of five children. Her parents were both teachers. But Poland at that time did not exist as a free country: Warsaw was ruled by Russia, and Polish children had to be taught in Russian. Polish history and language were forbidden in schools. Maria's father lost his good teaching job, partly because the Russian authorities distrusted him, and the family became poor.

Maria was brilliant. She finished school at fifteen with a gold medal. But universities in Warsaw did not accept women.

#### The Flying University

So she and her sister studied in secret. In Warsaw there was a hidden, illegal school known as the **Flying University**. Its classes met in different people's homes and kept moving so the authorities would not find them. Teachers taught science, history and Polish to young people, many of them women, who could not study anywhere else.

#### A deal between sisters

Maria and her older sister Bronisława (Bronya) both dreamed of studying in Paris, where women could go to university. They could not afford it. So they made a deal. Maria would work and send money to Bronya while she studied medicine. Then, once Bronya was a doctor, she would help Maria.

For several years Maria worked as a governess, teaching the children of a rich family in the countryside, sending her money to Paris and studying chemistry and physics from books at night.

#### A garret in Paris

:::scene Paris, 1891

In 1891, aged twenty-four, Maria arrived in Paris and enrolled at the Sorbonne, the University of Paris. She now called herself Marie. She lived in a tiny attic room, so cold in winter that she wore all her clothes to bed, and she sometimes forgot to eat. She came top of her class in physics in 1893 and second in mathematics a year later.

In 1894 she met Pierre Curie, a quiet physicist who had already made important discoveries about crystals and magnets. They talked about science, then about everything. They married in 1895, and for their honeymoon they went touring the countryside by bicycle.

#### Rays from a rock

In 1896 Henri Becquerel had found that uranium gives off invisible rays all by itself (that story is told in *radioactivity*). Marie chose these rays for her doctoral research. Using a very sensitive instrument that Pierre and his brother had invented, she measured how strongly different substances gave off rays.

She made a key discovery: the strength of the rays depended only on how much uranium was there, not on what it was combined with. That meant the rays came from **inside the atoms themselves**. She invented a word for this: **radioactivity**.

Then something odd. A uranium ore called pitchblende gave off *more* rays than its uranium could explain. Marie reasoned that it must contain something else, something far more radioactive, in tiny amounts.

#### The shed

:::scene A shed at the School of Physics and Chemistry, Paris, 1898 to 1902

Pierre dropped his own work to join her. In 1898 they announced two new elements. The first they called **polonium**, after Marie's homeland, which was not on the map. The second they called **radium**, from the Latin for "ray".

But other chemists wanted to see the new elements, not just their rays. So the Curies set out to collect pure radium. They had no proper laboratory, only an old shed with a leaking glass roof, freezing in winter and stifling in summer. Marie stirred boiling vats of pitchblende residue with an iron rod nearly as tall as herself.

It took about four years and *tonnes* of residue (the exact amount is told differently in different accounts) to produce about a tenth of a gram of radium compound: less than a pinch of salt. At night, Marie later wrote, the little glass tubes of radium glowed faintly in the dark.

#### Two Nobel Prizes, and a terrible day

In 1903 Marie, Pierre and Becquerel shared the Nobel Prize in Physics. Marie was the first woman ever to win a Nobel Prize.

In 1906 Pierre was killed in a street accident in Paris: he slipped in the rain and was struck by a horse-drawn cart. Marie was left with two daughters, Irène and Ève. She took over Pierre's teaching post and became the first woman professor at the Sorbonne. In 1911 she won a second Nobel Prize, this time in Chemistry, for discovering polonium and radium. She is still the only person to have won Nobel Prizes in two different sciences.

#### The little Curies

When the First World War began in 1914, Marie saw that X-rays could help surgeons find bullets and broken bones in wounded soldiers. She fitted cars with X-ray equipment and helped set up many more X-ray posts, learned to drive, and went to the front lines herself, often with her teenage daughter Irène. Soldiers called the vans *petites Curies*, "little Curies".

#### The cost

Nobody at the time understood how dangerous radiation is. Marie carried radium in her pockets and kept it in her desk. Over the years her health suffered, and in 1934 she died of a blood illness that was almost certainly caused by long exposure to radiation.

Her laboratory notebooks are still radioactive today. They are reported to be kept in lead-lined boxes in a French library, and people who wish to read them must take safety precautions.

In 1935 her daughter Irène and Irène's husband, Frédéric Joliot-Curie, won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for making new radioactive elements in the laboratory. The Curie family has won more Nobel Prizes than any other.

When you read about the nucleus and radioactivity in Chapter 1, remember the girl who studied in secret, and the glowing tubes in the leaking shed.
