:::scene East London, Easter holiday, 1856

William Perkin was eighteen, and he had turned part of his family's house in East London into a laboratory. In term time he was the assistant of a famous German chemist, August Wilhelm von Hofmann, at the Royal College of Chemistry. Over the Easter holiday he carried on experimenting at home.

Hofmann had set him a dream of a problem. Malaria was killing huge numbers of people across the British Empire, including in India. The only medicine, quinine, came from the bark of the cinchona tree, which grew in South America and was expensive and scarce. Could a chemist make quinine in a flask, from something cheap?

#### The cheap starting point

The cheapest raw material around was coal tar, the black, smelly waste left over when coal was heated to make gas for street lamps. Chemists had found that coal tar contained useful substances. Perkin took one of them, aniline, and treated it with a strong chemical that pulls hydrogen away, hoping to build quinine.

He did not get quinine. Nobody could have, with what was known in 1856: quinine's structure was far too complicated, and it was not made in a laboratory until 1944. What Perkin got was a black, tarry sludge at the bottom of his flask.

#### Looking twice

Most people would have thrown it away. Perkin washed the flask out with alcohol, and the alcohol turned a brilliant purple.

He tested the purple on silk. It stuck. It did not wash out, and it did not fade quickly in sunlight. Purple had always been the colour of emperors, because the old purple dye came from sea snails, thousands of them for a scrap of cloth. Perkin had made purple from coal waste.

#### The gamble

His teacher thought he was throwing away a scientific career. Perkin patented his dye anyway, left college, and, with money from his father and help from his brother, built a factory near London. He was still a teenager. Making it work was hard: they had to work out how to produce aniline by the barrel rather than by the spoonful, and how to fix the dye onto cotton as well as silk.

The colour became known by the French name **mauve**, and in the late 1850s mauve became the most fashionable colour in Europe. Queen Victoria wore mauve to her daughter's wedding in 1858. Magazines joked about "mauve measles" because the colour was everywhere.

#### What came next

Perkin's dye, later called **mauveine**, was the first of the coal-tar dyes, and the first artificial dye to become a worldwide success. Within a few years chemists had made magenta, new blues, greens and blacks, all from coal tar. The great chemical companies of Germany and Switzerland began as dye makers. And the skills they learned, building complicated molecules step by step, led straight to synthetic medicines. So in a roundabout way Perkin did help the fight against disease: just not the way he planned.

Perkin made his fortune, sold the factory at 36 and went back to pure research for the rest of his life. He was knighted in 1906, fifty years after that Easter holiday.

You need a spoonful of turmeric powder, a cup of hot tap water (ask an adult), and a strip of white cotton cloth, such as an old handkerchief. Stir the turmeric into the water, drop in the cloth for ten minutes, then rinse it in cold water. It stays bright yellow. Now dab one corner with a little baking soda dissolved in water: turmeric is also an indicator, and it turns red-brown where it meets something basic. Turmeric stains hands and clothes, so wear old clothes and work on a plate.

The lesson to take from Perkin is not "accidents make you rich". It is that he *noticed*. Lots of people had made black sludge. He looked at it twice.
