:::scene Paris, winter 1782

A guinea pig sits in a small wire cage. Around the cage is a jacket packed with crushed ice, and around that, another jacket of ice to keep out the warmth of the room. Antoine Lavoisier and the mathematician Pierre-Simon Laplace are waiting to see how much ice the little animal's body heat will melt. It sounds like a strange way to spend a winter, but they are asking one of the deepest questions in science: what *is* heat?

#### Heat as a fluid

In the 1700s most scientists believed that heat was an invisible, weightless fluid called *caloric*. Hot things were full of it; cold things had less. Caloric flowed from hot to cold the way water flows downhill, which is why your chai cools down and the air around it warms up. Lavoisier even listed caloric among the chemical elements.

The caloric idea explained a lot, and Lavoisier and Laplace's ice machine, the first ==calorimeter== (heat-measurer), was built to measure it. The melted ice dripped out and was weighed: the more ice melted, the more heat had been given off. They found that the guinea pig, over a few hours, melted about as much ice as burning a small amount of charcoal producing the same amount of carbon dioxide. Their conclusion was beautiful and correct: breathing is a slow, gentle kind of burning. Your body "burns" food with oxygen, and that is where your warmth comes from.

#### The cannon that never ran out of heat

:::scene The army arsenal in Munich, 1798

An American-born soldier and inventor, Benjamin Thompson, who had become Count Rumford in Bavaria, was in charge of making cannons. A cannon barrel was made by spinning a solid metal casting against a sharp drill. Rumford noticed how hot the metal became. So he tried an experiment: he used a deliberately blunt drill, turned by horses, with the cannon sitting in a box of water. After about two and a half hours, the water boiled. The watching crowd was astonished: water boiling without any fire.

What struck Rumford was that the heat never ran out. As long as the horses kept turning, heat kept coming. If heat were a fluid stored inside the metal, surely it would eventually be used up. Instead it seemed to be *made* by the motion. Rumford suggested that heat is not a substance at all, but a kind of motion.

#### Joule's paddle wheel

:::scene Salford, near Manchester, 1840s

The person who proved it was James Prescott ==Joule==, the son of a wealthy brewer, who did his experiments at home. His most famous apparatus was a paddle wheel inside a container of water. A weight hanging on a string slowly fell, turning the paddles, which churned the water. Joule measured the temperature of the water with a thermometer so precise it could detect a few hundredths of a degree.

The water warmed up, very slightly. Lowering a weight through a known height supplied a calculable amount of work; that work increased the water's internal energy. Joule found a reproducible relationship between the work supplied and the warming. In 1850 he published his best value: the energy of 772 pounds falling one foot raises one pound of water by one degree Fahrenheit. In today's units, it takes about 4,200 joules to warm a kilogram of water by one degree Celsius. The unit of energy, the joule, is named after him.

There is a famous story that Joule took a thermometer on his honeymoon in the Alps to test whether the water at the bottom of a waterfall was warmer than at the top. The physicist William Thomson said he met Joule there, thermometer in hand. Whether or not he measured anything, he was right in principle: the water at the bottom of a 100-metre waterfall should be warmer by only about a quarter of a degree, far too little to notice with your fingers.

#### Step 1: what energy is

==Energy== is a quantity we track through moving, warming, lighting and pushing. A raised weight has gravitational potential energy; lowering it can do work. A stretched spring has stored energy. A material's ==internal energy== includes microscopic motion and interactions among its particles. ==Heat== means energy transferred because of a temperature difference; it is not the name for energy stored inside a warm object.

The great discovery of the 1840s, made by Joule and several others at about the same time, is called the ==conservation of energy==: energy is never created or destroyed. It can be transferred and change form. Energy from the horses' chemical processes did mechanical work on the drill; energy then entered the metal and water. The muscles themselves did not turn into water.

#### Step 2: what heat really is

The title's historical phrase “heat is motion” points towards the microscopic account, but modern chemistry distinguishes heat from internal energy. For the same material and phase, warming usually means more energetic microscopic motion. When a hot cup contacts cooler air, interactions at the surface transfer energy from the cup to the air: that transfer is heat. Energy can also be transferred by radiation, without particles colliding across the gap. In melting or boiling, transferred energy can change the arrangement and interactions while temperature stays nearly constant. [IUPAC: heat](https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/H02752), [internal energy](https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/I03103).

:::work
**How much energy to make a cup of chai?** Warming 250 g of water from 25 °C to 100 °C:
Each gram needs about 4.18 joules for each degree. The water warms by 75 degrees.
Energy = 250 × 4.18 × 75 ≈ 78,000 joules, or 78 kilojoules.
Compare Joule's falling weight: to warm a kilogram of water by just one degree, a kilogram weight would have to fall about 427 metres, higher than most skyscrapers.
:::

#### Step 3: chemical reactions and heat

When a reaction gives energy out to its surroundings, they warm up: the reaction is ==exothermic== ("heat out"). Burning gas, a hand-warmer and your own breathing are exothermic. When a reaction takes energy in from its surroundings, they cool down: it is ==endothermic== ("heat in"). An instant cold pack is endothermic. In both cases, Joule's rule holds: whatever energy the chemicals lose, the surroundings gain, and the other way round. Chapter 5 shows where that energy comes from: the breaking and making of bonds.

:::careful
> "Cold is a thing that flows into your hand when you touch ice."

There is no such thing as "cold" flowing. Energy transfers from your warmer hand to the ice, lowering your hand's internal energy. Heat flows from hotter to colder without external work; a refrigerator needs work to move energy the other way.
:::

:::home A rubber band thermometer and a cooling fizz
Take a thick rubber band, hold it against your top lip (which is very sensitive to temperature), and stretch it quickly: it may feel warm. Hold it stretched for a few seconds, then let it go back quickly while touching your lip: it may feel cool. Stretching does work on the rubber and changes its internal energy; temperature differences then allow heat transfer to or from your skin. With a kitchen thermometer, compare the temperature before and after adding a little baking soda to vinegar in an open cup. The coupled dissolving and reacting process can cool the mixture; the observed change depends on amounts, starting conditions and heat exchange.
:::

From a guinea pig in ice to a paddle wheel in a brewery, the evidence connected heat transfer, work and changes in internal energy. The microscopic account includes particle motion and interactions; the total energy account remains conserved.
