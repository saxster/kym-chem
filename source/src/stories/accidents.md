There is a saying, usually credited to the French scientist Louis Pasteur: "chance favours only the prepared mind." Here are five discoveries that began as accidents, and the people who were prepared enough to notice.

:::scene Massachusetts, 1839

#### Rubber that behaves

Natural rubber, from the sap of rubber trees, was a nuisance in the 1830s. In summer it went soft and sticky; in winter it cracked. Charles Goodyear, an American inventor who was often deeply in debt, spent years mixing rubber with everything he could think of.

According to his own account, in 1839 he dropped some rubber mixed with sulfur onto a hot stove. Instead of melting, it charred like leather and stayed firm and springy. (The details come from Goodyear himself and may have been tidied up in the telling.) Heat had made sulfur atoms form bridges between the long rubber molecules, tying them together like the rungs of a ladder. The process was later called **vulcanisation**, after Vulcan, the Roman god of fire. Every car tyre depends on it.

:::scene London, 1928

#### The mould that killed germs

Alexander Fleming, a Scottish doctor, came back from a holiday to find that a dish of bacteria he had left on his bench had grown a fuzzy patch of mould. Around the mould there was a clear ring where the bacteria had died. Something from the mould was killing them.

Fleming called the substance **penicillin**. He could not make enough of it to use as a medicine, and for about ten years not much happened. Then, around 1940, a team at Oxford led by Howard Florey and Ernst Chain worked out how to purify it, and the first patients were treated. Penicillin has since saved an enormous number of lives.

:::scene New Jersey, 1938

#### The gas cylinder that went quiet

Roy Plunkett, a young chemist at the DuPont company, was working with a gas for refrigerators. One morning a cylinder that should have been full of gas would not let any out, though it still weighed as much as a full one. Instead of throwing it away, Plunkett had it sawn open. Inside was a white, waxy, slippery powder. The gas molecules had joined together into long chains, all by themselves.

The new material was incredibly slippery and almost nothing reacted with it. It was named **Teflon**, and today it coats non-stick frying pans.

:::scene Minnesota, 1968 and 1974

#### The glue that did not stick properly

Spencer Silver, a chemist at the company 3M, was trying to make a very strong glue. He made a very weak one instead: it stuck, but peeled off easily and could be stuck again. For years nobody knew what to do with it. Then his colleague Art Fry, who sang in a church choir, was annoyed that his paper bookmarks kept falling out of his hymn book. He remembered Silver's glue. The result, after more years of work, was the sticky note.

:::careful
> "Saccharin was found by a chemist licking his fingers, so tasting things is how discoveries get made."

It is true that in 1879 a chemist named Constantin Fahlberg noticed his bread tasted sweet after a day in the laboratory, traced it to a chemical on his hands, and so found the sweetener saccharin. But many chemists in history poisoned themselves by tasting and smelling unknown substances. Never taste anything from an experiment. Fahlberg was lucky, and lucky is not a method.
:::

#### What they had in common

None of these people was simply lucky. Goodyear had spent years on rubber. Fleming knew exactly what a clear ring around a mould meant. Plunkett weighed his cylinder instead of assuming it was empty. Each had a prepared mind: they knew enough to recognise that something strange was also something important.
