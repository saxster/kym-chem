:::scene Portland, Oregon, 1910

Linus Pauling was born on 28 February 1901 in Portland, Oregon, in the United States. His father, Herman, was a pharmacist who mixed medicines in his own shop. When Linus was nine, his father died suddenly. The family was left with very little money, and Linus grew up quickly. He read everything: the encyclopaedia, books on insects, books on minerals.

When he was about thirteen, a friend showed him a simple chemistry set and did a few experiments with it. Linus was hooked. He borrowed equipment and chemicals from an abandoned smelter near his grandfather's home and set up a laboratory in his basement. He later said he decided then that he wanted to be a chemist.

He left high school without a diploma, after an argument over a missing history credit, and went straight to Oregon Agricultural College. To pay his way he did whatever work he could find: chopping wood, mopping floors, working in a shipyard and checking the materials for new roads. Because the college was so short of teachers, he was even asked to teach chemistry to younger students while still a student himself. In one of those classes was a young woman called Ava Helen Miller. They married in 1923 and stayed together for fifty-eight years. (His high school finally gave him his diploma in 1962, by which time he had already won a Nobel Prize.)

#### How do atoms hold together?

:::scene Pasadena, California, 1920s

Pauling went to Caltech, in California, to study how atoms are arranged inside crystals by firing X-rays through them. Then he travelled to Europe, to Munich, Copenhagen and Zurich, just as the new quantum mechanics was being invented, and he saw immediately what it could do for chemistry.

Chemists had a simple idea, from Gilbert Lewis, that atoms in a molecule are held together by sharing pairs of electrons. Pauling used quantum mechanics to explain *why* that works, and to predict the shapes it gives. One of his ideas explains why carbon's four bonds point to the corners of a tetrahedron, the shape you meet in Chapter 10. In 1939 he put it all together in a book called *The Nature of the Chemical Bond*. It became one of the most important chemistry books of the century, and it shaped how chemists thought for decades.

He also gave chemists a number for each element called its **electronegativity**: how strongly its atoms pull on the electrons in a bond. Fluorine pulls hardest; elements like sodium pull weakly. When two atoms with very different pulls share electrons, the sharing is lopsided, and the bond is polar. That idea is behind the polar water molecule in this book, and behind the **hydrogen bonds** that hold water molecules to each other, which Pauling also helped explain.

#### A helix on a sheet of paper

:::scene Oxford, England, 1948

Pauling next turned to the giant molecules of life: proteins, the long chains that make up hair, muscle and enzymes. Nobody knew how those chains were folded.

There is a well-known story, which Pauling himself told, about how he found the answer. In 1948, while visiting Oxford, he was stuck in bed with a bad cold. Bored, he took a sheet of paper, drew a protein chain on it with the correct bond lengths and angles, and began folding the paper to see how the chain could twist so that its parts held onto each other with hydrogen bonds. He found a spiral, like a spring or a spiral staircase. It took another three years of checking with his colleagues before they published it in 1951, as the **alpha helix**. It turned out to be right: it is one of the most common shapes in proteins in your body.

In 1954 he won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for his work on the chemical bond.

#### The second prize

Linus and Ava Helen were both deeply worried about nuclear weapons. In the 1950s, countries were testing atomic bombs in the open air, and radioactive dust was spreading round the world. Pauling gathered the signatures of thousands of scientists on a petition calling for the tests to end, gave talks everywhere, and was attacked for it: for a while the American government would not let him have a passport. In 1963 a treaty banning nuclear tests in the air came into force, and Pauling was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize for 1962. He is still the only person to have won two Nobel Prizes on his own, without sharing either.

#### Getting it wrong

Pauling was brilliant, but he was not always right, and the honest story includes his mistakes.

In early 1953 he was racing to work out the structure of DNA. He published a model with three twisted strands, a triple helix. It was wrong: it even had a basic chemical error, putting acid groups in a place where they could not have held together. A few weeks later, James Watson and Francis Crick in Cambridge published the correct double helix, built partly on Rosalind Franklin's X-ray photographs, which Pauling had never seen.

Later in life, Pauling became convinced that very large doses of vitamin C could prevent colds and help treat cancer. He wrote best-selling books about it. Careful medical trials since then have *not* supported his biggest claims. Being a great scientist in one area did not make him right in another, and doctors who tested his ideas properly found that the evidence was not there.

That is worth remembering. In science, it does not matter how famous you are. What matters is what the evidence shows.

:::home Fold a helix
Take a long strip of paper, about 3 cm wide (cut along the long side of a sheet). Draw a zigzag line along it, like a chain of bonds. Now wind the strip loosely round a pencil or a marker, at a slant, so it makes a spiral, then slide the pencil out. Notice how, in a helix, each turn sits just above the one before. In a real alpha helix, each turn is held to the next by hydrogen bonds, which is what Pauling was looking for as he folded his paper in bed.
:::

When you read about why water's molecules stick together, or why carbon's bonds point the way they do, you are reading Linus Pauling's chemistry.
