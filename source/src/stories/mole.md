:::scene A market street in Mumbai, any morning

Walk through any market and you will see three different ways of counting. The egg seller counts eggs, but in dozens: nobody asks for "fourteen eggs and a bit". The stationery shop sells paper by the ream, 500 sheets at a time, and nobody counts the sheets. And the rice seller does not count at all. You ask for a kilo, the grains go on the scale, and in a few seconds you have about fifty thousand grains of rice without anyone counting a single one.

That last trick, counting by weighing, is the whole secret of the mole. Chemists had to learn it, because the things they needed to count were far smaller than rice and far, far more numerous. This is the story of how they did it, one step at a time.

#### Step 1: the problem

By the early 1800s chemists had discovered something wonderful: substances combine in fixed proportions, because atoms combine in small whole numbers. Two hydrogen atoms join one oxygen atom to make water. One carbon atom joins two oxygen atoms to make carbon dioxide. Chemistry happens atom by atom.

But nobody could pick up an atom. In the laboratory you can only *weigh* things. So the question became: if a recipe says "two hydrogens for every oxygen", how many grams of hydrogen and how many grams of oxygen do you need? To answer that, you have to know how heavy each kind of atom is, at least compared with the others.

#### Step 2: weighing atoms against each other

In 1808 an English schoolteacher, John Dalton, published one of the first tables of relative atomic weights. He could not weigh a single atom, but he could compare. If 1 gram of hydrogen combines with 8 grams of oxygen to make water, and each water molecule has two hydrogens for every oxygen, then one oxygen atom must weigh about 16 times as much as one hydrogen atom. (Dalton himself got water's formula wrong and put oxygen at 7, but his *method* was right, and later chemists fixed the numbers.)

Repeat that kind of reasoning for every element and you get a list of ==relative atomic masses==: how heavy each atom is compared with the others. Roughly: hydrogen 1, carbon 12, oxygen 16, sodium 23, chlorine 35.5. These numbers have no units. They are comparisons, like saying an elephant is about 70 times as heavy as a person.

#### Step 3: the trick that makes counting possible

Here is the clever part. Imagine a jar of big glass marbles, each weighing 16 grams, and a jar of small beads, each weighing 1 gram. You want the same *number* of marbles as beads, but you only have a kitchen scale.

:::work
Weigh out 16 g of marbles: that is 1 marble. Weigh out 1 g of beads: that is 1 bead.
Weigh out 1,600 g of marbles: 100 marbles. Weigh out 100 g of beads: 100 beads.
Whatever you choose, if the masses are in the ratio 16 : 1, the numbers are equal.
:::

Atoms work exactly the same way. An oxygen atom is 16 times heavier than a hydrogen atom. So 16 grams of oxygen and 1 gram of hydrogen contain *the same number of atoms*. So do 12 grams of carbon, 23 grams of sodium and 35.5 grams of chlorine. Take any element, weigh out its relative atomic mass in grams, and you always get the same number of atoms. You do not even need to know what that number is.

That fixed number of atoms is so useful that it needed a name. In the 1890s the German chemist Wilhelm Ostwald gave it one: the ==Mol==, from *Molekül*, molecule. In English it became the **mole**. A mole is simply that number of things, just as a dozen is twelve things.

:::fig c6_mole_piles() | One mole each of three substances: the same number of particles in every pile, but different masses, because the particles themselves have different masses.

#### Step 4: but how many is it?

For a long time, nobody knew. Chemists used moles happily for decades while having only a rough idea of how many atoms were in one. Think about how odd that is: it is like buying eggs by the dozen without knowing that a dozen is twelve. It worked because, for chemistry, what matters is that the number is *the same* for every substance.

The first estimate came in 1865 from an Austrian schoolteacher-turned-physicist, Josef Loschmidt, who worked out roughly how big air molecules must be from how gases behave. His answer was in the right range, astonishingly, but rough. Then, around 1909, Jean Perrin in Paris measured the number several independent ways, including by watching tiny grains jiggle in water, and every method gave nearly the same answer. Perrin proposed naming it after Amedeo Avogadro, whose ideas about gases had made all of this possible. Today it is called ==Avogadro's constant==, and since 2019 it is not measured at all but fixed by definition:

:::eq
**1 mole = 6.022 140 76 × 10²³ things**
:::

#### Step 5: feeling how big that is

Written out in full, that is 602,214,076,000,000,000,000,000. Numbers this big slide straight past the imagination, so here are two ways to get a grip on it.

If you counted one thing every second, day and night, without stopping, counting to one mole would take about 19 thousand million million years: more than a million times the age of the universe.

A grain of rice has a mass of about 0.02 grams. A mole of rice grains would weigh about 12 thousand million million tonnes (1.2 × 10¹⁶ tonnes). The whole world grows roughly 500 million tonnes of rice a year, so growing one mole of rice would take every rice farmer on Earth tens of millions of years.

And yet a mole of water molecules is only 18 grams: a single gulp. That is how small molecules are.

#### Step 6: using moles, with your hand held

The mass of one mole of a substance is its ==molar mass==, in grams per mole. For an element it is the relative atomic mass in grams; for a molecule you add up its atoms. Water, H₂O, is 1 + 1 + 16 = 18 grams per mole.

:::work
**How many moles in 9 grams of water?**
Each mole is 18 g. 9 g is half of 18 g. So 9 g is 0.5 moles.
As a rule: moles = grams ÷ molar mass = 9 ÷ 18 = 0.5 mol.
:::

:::work
**How many sugar molecules in a 4-gram teaspoon of sugar?**
Sugar (sucrose, C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁) has a molar mass of about 342 g per mole.
Moles = 4 ÷ 342 ≈ 0.0117 mol.
Molecules = 0.0117 × 6.022 × 10²³ ≈ 7 × 10²¹: about seven thousand million million million molecules in one teaspoon.
:::

:::careful
> "A mole is an amount of mass, like a kilogram."

It is not. A mole is a *number* of things, like a dozen. A dozen eggs and a dozen elephants are both twelve, but they do not weigh the same. A mole of hydrogen atoms has a mass of 1 gram; a mole of gold atoms has a mass of 197 grams. Same number, very different weights.
:::

:::careful
> "A mole of oxygen is 16 grams."

It depends what you are counting. A mole of oxygen *atoms* is 16 g. But oxygen gas is made of O₂ molecules, and a mole of O₂ *molecules* is 32 g, because each molecule contains two atoms. Chemists always say what they are counting, and so should you.
:::

:::home Count rice by weighing it
You need a kitchen scale and some uncooked rice. Count out exactly 100 grains and weigh them (a sensitive scale helps; if yours only reads to the nearest gram, weigh 200 or 500 grains). Now weigh a cupful of rice. Divide the cup's mass by the mass of one grain to find how many grains are in the cup, without counting them. Then count a small part of the cup to check. You have just done what chemists do with moles.
:::

So the next time you see "1 mole" in this book, translate it in your head: *six hundred thousand million million million of something, which conveniently weighs its formula's mass in grams*. It is the chemist's dozen, and it is the bridge between the invisible world of atoms and the kitchen scale on the bench.
