:::scene Cambridge, Massachusetts, 1925

Cecilia Payne was twenty-five, an English student who had crossed the Atlantic because women in Cambridge, England, could study science but could not be awarded degrees. At Harvard College Observatory she was given thousands of glass photographs of starlight, each one spread into a spectrum crossed by dark lines: the fingerprints of elements, the same kind Bunsen and Kirchhoff had found in flames (the spectra story tells that part).

Using the new physics of the atom, Payne worked out not just *which* elements were in the stars, but *how much* of each. Her answer was astonishing. The stars were not made of roughly the same stuff as the Earth, as everyone assumed. They were overwhelmingly hydrogen and helium, with everything else a light sprinkling. The most senior astronomer in America, Henry Norris Russell, told her the result was "clearly impossible", and in her thesis she wrote that the huge amount of hydrogen was "almost certainly not real". A few years later Russell himself reached the same conclusion by another route. Her thesis has since been called one of the most brilliant ever written in astronomy.

She was right. By mass, the ordinary matter of the universe is about three-quarters hydrogen and about a quarter helium. Everything else, all the carbon and oxygen and iron and gold, adds up to around two percent. So where did *that* come from?

#### Chapter one of the story: the Big Bang

About 13.8 billion years ago the universe was unimaginably hot and dense, and it was expanding fast. In the first few minutes it was hot enough for protons and neutrons to fuse, but it cooled too quickly to get far. It left mostly hydrogen nuclei, formed helium nuclei and a tiny trace of lithium, and then the nuclear-fusion window closed. Complete neutral atoms formed much later, about 380,000 years after the Big Bang, when nuclei could capture electrons.

That means something remarkable about you. Most of the atoms in your body are hydrogen (you are mostly water, and each water molecule has two hydrogen atoms). Most of those hydrogen nuclei trace back to the early universe. The nuclei in a glass of water carry that ancient history; a complete atom, with its electrons, is a different level of description.

#### Chapter two: the kitchens inside stars

For everything heavier, the universe needed stars. A star is a vast ball of gas squeezing itself under its own weight until the centre is hot enough for nuclei to fuse. Our Sun turns hydrogen into helium and will keep doing so for billions of years.

Bigger stars go further. When their hydrogen runs low, their cores squeeze harder and heat up more, and they fuse helium into carbon and oxygen, then those into neon, magnesium, silicon and sulfur, and finally into iron. Each step releases energy, which keeps the star shining. But iron is the end of the line. Fusing iron into anything heavier *costs* energy instead of releasing it, so a star cannot live on it.

:::scene Pasadena, 1957

How all this works was set out in a single huge paper in 1957, "Synthesis of the Elements in Stars", by Margaret Burbidge, Geoffrey Burbidge, William Fowler and Fred Hoyle. It is still known by their initials, B²FH. Margaret Burbidge had to fight for her place in astronomy: she once had to pretend to be her husband's assistant to be allowed to use a famous telescope. The paper explained, step by step, how stars could build almost every element, and Fowler later shared the 1983 Nobel Prize in Physics for this kind of work.

#### Chapter three: explosions

When a very big star's iron core can no longer hold itself up, it collapses in a fraction of a second and the star blows itself apart in a **supernova**, briefly outshining a whole galaxy. The explosion flings the carbon, oxygen, silicon and iron the star has made out into space, and in the violent blast it makes some heavier elements too. Another kind of explosion, when a small dead star called a white dwarf is pushed over the edge, makes a great deal of the iron in the universe.

The flung-out gas mixes with the clouds between the stars. New stars, and the planets around them, form from that enriched gas. Our Sun and the Earth formed about 4.6 billion years ago from a cloud that had already been seeded by generations of earlier stars. The calcium in your bones and the iron in your blood were made inside stars that died before the Sun was born.

:::scene Hanford and Livingston, 17 August 2017

The heaviest elements, like gold, platinum and uranium, need a flood of neutrons to build, far more than ordinary stars can supply. For a long time nobody was sure where that flood happened. Then, on 17 August 2017, the gravitational-wave detectors of LIGO in the United States and Virgo in Italy felt a faint ripple in space itself. It came from two **neutron stars**, the collapsed cores of dead stars, so dense that a teaspoon would weigh as much as a mountain, spiralling into each other about 130 million light years away. Telescopes around the world turned to the spot and watched a new kind of glow, called a **kilonova**. Its light carried the signatures of freshly made heavy elements. LIGO's own summary estimates the collision threw out somewhere between a thousandth and a hundredth of the mass of our Sun.

So the universe has its own gold factory: colliding dead stars. Remember the transmutation story, where people spent fortunes making a few atoms of gold? Nature makes it in quantities the size of planets, in explosions we can now hear across space. Astronomers still debate how much of the universe's gold comes from mergers like this and how much from rare kinds of supernova; the honest answer is that both seem to contribute, and nobody yet knows the exact share.

#### Chapter four: cosmic rays and laboratories

Three light elements, lithium, beryllium and boron, are made largely a stranger way (lithium also comes from the Big Bang and from some dying stars): fast particles called cosmic rays smash into heavier nuclei drifting in space and chip pieces off them. And the newest elements of all were made by people. Almost everything heavier than uranium is made in laboratories, a few atoms at a time, by smashing nuclei together; only tiny natural traces of neptunium and plutonium exist.

The astronomer Jennifer Johnson, at Ohio State University, drew a periodic table in which every box is coloured by where that element comes from: the Big Bang, dying small stars, exploding big stars, exploding white dwarfs, colliding neutron stars, cosmic rays. It is one of the most beautiful versions of the table ever made, and worth looking up.

:::careful
> "Elements are made in factories and chemistry labs."

Factories and labs only *rearrange* atoms into new substances. They do not make new atoms of an element; a chemical reaction never changes a nucleus. Almost every atom on Earth, apart from a few made in nuclear reactors and particle accelerators, was made in the Big Bang or in stars long before the Earth existed, and has been recycled ever since.
:::

When you look at the periodic table now, you can see a history in it. The first two boxes are the Big Bang. Most of the rest are the work of stars. The heavy metals at the bottom are the ashes of the most violent events in the universe. And the very last boxes were made by people, in laboratories, a few atoms at a time.
