:::scene Tobolsk, Siberia, the 1840s

Dmitri Mendeleev was born in 1834 in Tobolsk, a town in western Siberia. He was the youngest child of a very big family: sources disagree on how many children there were (numbers from fourteen to seventeen are given), but everyone agrees he was the last.

His father, Ivan, was the headmaster of the local school, until he went blind and had to stop working. The family was suddenly poor. His mother, Maria, did something remarkable: she took over a glass factory that belonged to her family, and ran it to keep the children fed. Dmitri grew up around furnaces, sand and molten glass. When he was about thirteen his father died, and not long afterwards the glass factory burned down.

#### A mother's journey

Maria was sure her youngest son was gifted. In 1849, when he was fifteen, she set off with him across Russia to find him a university place. They travelled thousands of kilometres, mostly by horse-drawn transport. Moscow University would not take him, because he came from Siberia. So they went on to St Petersburg, where in 1850 he got into a college for training teachers, partly because an old friend of his father worked there.

A few months later his mother died. Dmitri was sixteen, alone and often ill (for a while doctors believed he had a lung disease and not long to live). He kept working. Many years later he dedicated a great book to her, and wrote that she had taught him to seek truth patiently, through work rather than words.

#### Sixty-three elements and a problem

:::scene St Petersburg, February 1869

By the 1860s Mendeleev was a chemistry professor in St Petersburg. He was writing a textbook for his students, and he had a problem: how to organise the chapters. About sixty-three elements were known. In what order should he teach them?

Other chemists had noticed patterns. Some elements came in families, such as lithium, sodium and potassium, which all behave alike. But nobody had found one arrangement that included everything.

Mendeleev wrote each element on a card, with its atomic weight and its main properties, and laid the cards out on a table, moving them about to find the pattern. Lining them up in order of weight, he saw that similar properties came round again and again, like the days of the week. He arranged the cards so that families sat together in columns. This was his periodic table, first published in 1869. (A German chemist, Lothar Meyer, found a very similar arrangement at about the same time.)

:::careful
> "Mendeleev saw the periodic table in a dream."

This is a famous story, but it is a legend. It comes from an account written decades later by someone who said Mendeleev had told him so. There is no evidence from 1869 itself. What we do know is that he worked hard on it, with cards, notes and many rough drafts.
:::

#### The daring part: gaps

Here is what made Mendeleev different. Sometimes the next element by weight did not fit the family pattern. Instead of forcing it in, he left a **gap**, and said: *an element belongs here that nobody has discovered yet.*

He went further. From the neighbours of each gap he predicted what the missing element would be like: its atomic weight, how dense it would be, what compounds it would make. He gave them temporary names, such as *eka-aluminium* ("one below aluminium").

:::scene Paris, 1875

In 1875 a French chemist, Paul-Émile Lecoq de Boisbaudran, found a new metal using a spectroscope and called it **gallium**. Mendeleev read the report and said: that is my eka-aluminium. But Lecoq's measured density, about 4.7 grams per cubic centimetre, did not match Mendeleev's prediction of about 5.9. Mendeleev wrote to say the measurement must be wrong. Lecoq purified his sample and measured again, and got about 5.9. The man who had never seen gallium knew it better than the man holding it.

Then came scandium (1879) and germanium (1886), both matching his predictions closely. After that, nobody laughed at the periodic table.

:::home Build a table with gaps
Take an ordinary pack of cards. Secretly remove two or three cards, say the 7 of hearts and the queen of clubs, and give the rest to a friend. Ask them to lay out the cards in a grid: one row for each suit, numbers in order. Gaps will appear exactly where the missing cards belong, and your friend can tell you what the missing cards must be without ever seeing them. That is exactly what Mendeleev did with elements.
:::

#### A fiery man

Mendeleev was famous for his temper, his wild hair and beard (he is said to have had it cut only once a year), and his energy. He worked on oil, on agriculture, on weights and measures (he later ran Russia's office of weights and measures), and much else.

In 1887 he went up alone in a balloon filled with hydrogen to watch a solar eclipse from above the clouds, although he had never flown one by himself before. He landed safely.

He never won a Nobel Prize, though he was nominated and came very close in 1906. Historians think he had made enemies, including a powerful Swedish chemist, Svante Arrhenius, whose ideas Mendeleev had criticised. Nobody can be completely sure. He died in 1907. In 1955 a newly made element, number 101, was named **mendelevium** in his honour.

When you open the periodic table in Chapters 1 and 9, look for the places where he left gaps, and think of the boy from Siberia whose mother would not give up on him.
