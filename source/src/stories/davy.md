:::scene Penzance, Cornwall, the 1780s

At the very bottom tip of England, where the land runs out into the Atlantic, there is a small fishing town called Penzance. Humphry Davy was born there in 1778. His father was a woodcarver who never made much money, and when he died the family was left poor. Humphry was the eldest of five children, and at sixteen he had to earn a living.

He was apprenticed to a local surgeon-apothecary: a kind of village doctor who also mixed medicines. It was not a grand job. But the shop had bottles, scales and books, and Humphry read everything. He taught himself chemistry from Lavoisier's new book, in French. He also wrote poetry, fished, and wandered the cliffs. All his life he would be two things at once: a poet and a scientist.

#### The gas that made people laugh

:::scene Bristol, 1799

At nineteen Davy got a job at a strange new place in Bristol: the Pneumatic Institution, run by a doctor called Thomas Beddoes. "Pneumatic" means to do with air. Beddoes hoped that breathing newly discovered gases might cure diseases.

Davy's job was to make the gases and test them. Often he tested them on himself, which was brave and also quite reckless. One of them was nitrous oxide. When Davy breathed it, he felt light-headed and giggly, and the world seemed wonderful. Soon his friends were trying it too, including poets. It got the nickname *laughing gas*.

Davy also noticed something important: it seemed to take away pain. He wrote in 1800 that it might be used during surgery. Almost nobody followed this up for more than forty years, and in the meantime surgery went on without anything to stop the pain. Today nitrous oxide is still used by dentists and in hospitals.

:::careful
> "Davy was being sensible about safety."

He was not, by today's standards. He breathed several gases that could have killed him, and once nearly did. Modern chemists never test unknown gases on themselves. Davy was lucky, and his luck is not a method.
:::

#### The most famous lecturer in London

:::scene The Royal Institution, London, 1801

In 1801 Davy moved to the Royal Institution in London, a new place built to bring science to the public. He was twenty-two, good-looking and a brilliant speaker, and his lectures became the place to be seen. Carriages blocked the street outside. People who knew no chemistry at all came to watch him make things flash, bang, fizz and change colour, while he explained, in beautiful sentences, what was happening.

#### Tearing metals out of rocks

:::scene The Royal Institution basement, October 1807

In 1800, Alessandro Volta had invented the battery (you meet it in Chapter 8). Davy saw at once what it could do. Electricity could pull compounds apart. If you pass a current through water, it splits into hydrogen and oxygen. What else could it split?

For years, chemists had suspected that potash (a substance made from wood ashes) and soda contained metals, but nothing could break them apart. Davy built enormous batteries in the basement of the Royal Institution, melted some potash, and passed a powerful current through it.

At one wire, tiny shining globules appeared, like quicksilver. Some of them burst into flame the moment they appeared. Davy had discovered **potassium**, a metal so reactive that it catches fire in water. His cousin Edmund, who was helping him, later wrote that Humphry danced about the room with delight. A few days later he made **sodium** from soda in the same way.

The next year he used the same trick to find calcium, magnesium, barium and strontium. In about two years, one man had added six new elements to the list.

Why did potash resist every chemist before him? Because potassium gives its electrons away more readily than almost any other metal. Ordinary chemicals cannot force those electrons back onto it, so the metal stays locked up in its compounds. A strong enough battery can push the electrons back by brute force. That is why the most reactive metals were the last to be found, and why they were all found with electricity.

#### A lamp that saved lives

:::scene The coal mines of northern England, 1815

Coal mines were filled with a dangerous gas called *firedamp* (mostly methane). Miners needed light to work, but a candle flame could set the gas off in a terrible explosion. In 1815 the mine owners asked Davy for help.

He worked out that a flame cannot pass through a fine metal gauze, because the metal draws away heat so fast that the gas on the other side never gets hot enough to catch fire. So he wrapped the flame of a miner's lamp in a cylinder of wire gauze. The gas could get in and burn harmlessly inside, but the flame could not get out.

Davy refused to patent the lamp. He wanted it to be free for every mine. (An engineer called George Stephenson designed a similar lamp at almost the same time, and there was a bitter argument about who came first.)

#### His greatest discovery

Davy once hired a young bookbinder as an assistant, a young man called Michael Faraday. It is often said, though nobody knows if Davy himself said it, that "Davy's greatest discovery was Michael Faraday". Faraday became an even greater scientist than his teacher.

That was not always easy for Davy. As he grew older and more famous (he was made a knight, then president of the Royal Society), he became prouder and touchier. When Faraday was proposed for the Royal Society in 1823, Davy, as president, is reported to have opposed it. Great people can be generous and jealous at once.

Davy's health failed in his late forties, perhaps in part because of all the chemicals he had breathed and handled, though nobody can be sure. He died in Geneva in 1829, aged fifty.
