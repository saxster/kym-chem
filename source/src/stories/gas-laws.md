:::scene Florence, 1643

The well-diggers and fountain-builders of Italy had a problem that nobody could explain. A suction pump, the kind you work with a handle, could lift water up a pipe, but only so far. Past about ten metres the water simply stopped rising, however hard the pump worked. The great Galileo had puzzled over it before his death in 1642. His young pupil, Evangelista Torricelli, had an idea that sounded almost silly: perhaps the pump was not *pulling* the water up at all. Perhaps the air outside, pressing down on the water in the well, was *pushing* it up, and ten metres of water was simply as much as the weight of the air could hold.

If that was right, a heavier liquid should rise less. Mercury is about 13.6 times as heavy as water. So Torricelli filled a glass tube with mercury, put his thumb over the end, turned it upside down into a dish of mercury and let go. The mercury dropped, and then stopped, leaving a column about 76 centimetres tall and an empty space at the top of the tube. About ten and a third metres divided by 13.6 is about 76 centimetres. The air really did have weight, and it was holding the mercury up.

:::fig s_torricelli() | Torricelli's tube, the first barometer. The air pressing on the open dish holds up a column of mercury about 76 cm tall. The same push would hold up about 10.3 metres of water, which is why suction pumps could never lift water higher than that.

Torricelli wrote, beautifully, that "we live submerged at the bottom of an ocean of air". That ocean presses on you all the time. At sea level it pushes on every square centimetre of your skin with the weight of about one kilogram, and on every square metre with the weight of about ten tonnes. You do not feel it because it pushes equally from every side, inside and out. That push is called ==pressure==.

:::scene Oxford, 1662

Twenty years later, in England, Robert Boyle and his brilliant assistant Robert Hooke built an air pump that could suck most of the air out of a glass globe. They watched a ringing bell go quiet inside it, and a candle go out. Then Boyle tried something simple and careful. He took a long glass tube bent into a J, sealed at the short end, and poured mercury into the long end. A pocket of air was trapped in the short arm. The more mercury he poured in, the harder it pressed on the trapped air, and the smaller the air pocket became.

When he doubled the pressure, the air squeezed into half the space. When he tripled it, a third. Pressure times volume stayed the same. This is ==Boyle's law==, and it was one of the first times anyone had found a precise mathematical rule hiding inside the behaviour of an ordinary substance.

#### Step 1: why squeezing raises the pressure

Today we can explain what Boyle could only measure. A gas is a crowd of tiny particles flying about and bouncing off the walls. Every bounce is a tiny push. Pressure is all those pushes added up.

Squeeze the same particles into half the space and they are packed twice as closely. Each bit of wall gets hit twice as often. Twice the hits means twice the pressure.

:::scene Paris, 1783

Then came the balloons. In the summer and autumn of 1783 France went balloon-mad. The Montgolfier brothers, paper-makers, discovered that a big bag of hot air would float, and on 21 November 1783 two men rose over Paris under a Montgolfier balloon, the first people ever to fly. Just ten days later the scientist Jacques Charles went up in a different kind of balloon, filled with hydrogen, the lightest gas of all. A huge crowd watched it rise from the Tuileries gardens.

Charles was interested in more than flying. He noticed that gases expand when they are warmed, and by about 1787 he had found that different gases all expand by the same fraction for each degree of warming. He never published it. In 1802 Joseph Gay-Lussac measured it carefully and published it, generously giving Charles the credit. Two years later Gay-Lussac went up in a balloon himself, to a height of about 7,000 metres, higher than anyone had been, to collect samples of thin, freezing air. (They turned out to contain the same mixture of gases as the air at the ground.)

#### Step 2: why warming raises the pressure

Warm a gas and its particles move faster. Faster particles hit the walls harder *and* more often. If the gas is in a sealed, rigid box, the pressure goes up. If the gas is in a stretchy balloon, the balloon swells until the pressure inside matches the air outside again.

:::fig c3_gas_temperature() | The same gas warmed from 200 K to 400 K in a sealed box: the particles move faster and the pressure doubles.
:::live gasbox {"mode":"temperature"}

The rule only works neatly if you measure temperature from absolute zero, in kelvin, not in degrees Celsius. (The story of how anyone found absolute zero is told in *How cold can cold get?*)

#### Step 3: putting it all together

Over the next century these separate rules were joined into one: the ==ideal gas== law. For a fixed amount of gas, pressure goes up when you squeeze it smaller, goes up when you warm it, and goes up when you add more gas. In symbols chemists write it as *PV* = *nRT*, but in words it is just those three ideas at once.

:::work
**A syringe with your thumb over the end.**
Pull the plunger out to 20 mL of trapped air, then push it in to 10 mL. Half the space: the pressure inside doubles.
Push it further, to 5 mL: half again, so the pressure is now four times what it was.
That is why it gets harder and harder to push.
:::

It is called "ideal" because it pretends the particles are tiny points that never attract each other. Real gases follow it very closely in everyday conditions, and stop following it when you squeeze them very hard or cool them until they are about to turn into liquid.

:::careful
> "When you squeeze a gas, the particles get smaller."

The particles stay exactly the same size. Only the empty space between them shrinks. That is why gases are so easy to squeeze and liquids, whose particles are already touching, are not.
:::

:::home The upside-down glass
Over a sink, fill a glass right to the brim with water. Slide a piece of stiff card over the top so there is no air bubble, hold the card in place, and turn the glass upside down. Take your hand away from the card. The water stays in. The air pushing up on the card is far stronger than the weight of the water pushing down: remember, it could hold up ten metres of water.
:::

:::home A balloon in the freezer
Blow up a balloon, tie it, and measure round its middle with a piece of string. Put it in the freezer for half an hour and measure again. Then let it warm up. The particles inside slow down in the cold and push less hard, so the balloon shrinks; as they warm, it grows back. Nothing got in or out.
:::

Every time this book talks about gases, from boiling water to car tyres to the air in a diver's tank, it is standing on the shoulders of a pump that would not pump, a tube of mercury, and a sky full of balloons.
