:::scene Harvard University, 1902

Chemists in 1900 drew molecules with little lines between the atoms, H–O–H for water, and they were very good at it. They knew which atoms joined to which, and how many lines each kind of atom liked to have: one for hydrogen, two for oxygen, four for carbon. But nobody could say what a line actually *was*. A bond was a mystery written as a dash.

The electron had been discovered only five years earlier. A young American chemist, Gilbert Newton Lewis, began wondering whether electrons might be the answer. In 1902, while preparing a lecture, he sketched atoms as little cubes, with electrons sitting at the corners. A cube has eight corners. Many atoms seemed to end up, when they combined, with eight outer electrons around them. Could a bond be two atoms sharing corners?

He put the sketches in a drawer, and did not publish them for fourteen years.

#### The shared pair

:::scene Berkeley, California, 1916

By 1916 Lewis was a famous professor at Berkeley, and he finally published his idea, improved. A chemical bond, he proposed, is a pair of electrons *shared* between two atoms. Each line in H–O–H stands for two electrons that belong to both atoms at once.

He invented a way of drawing it that you will still see in school books today: the element symbol, with dots around it for the outer electrons. Hydrogen has one outer electron; oxygen has six. Draw water and you see two shared pairs, one between oxygen and each hydrogen, plus two pairs that belong to oxygen alone.

:::fig s_lewis_water() | Lewis's way of drawing water. Each dot is an outer electron. The pairs between atoms are shared: those are the bonds. Oxygen also keeps two pairs of its own, called lone pairs.

A few years later another American, Irving Langmuir, spread Lewis's ideas widely and gave a name to sharing bonds: **covalent**, meaning "sharing strength". He also popularised the "octet", the eight outer electrons that Lewis had first drawn on his cubes.

#### Why does sharing hold atoms together?

Here is the part that matters most, because it is where people start saying atoms "want" to share. They do not want anything. The real reason is electric attraction.

1. **Every nucleus is positive; every electron is negative.** Opposite charges pull on each other.
2. **A pair of electrons sitting between two nuclei is pulled by both of them at once**, and pulls back on both. It acts like glue between them, held in place by two attractions instead of one.
3. **The nuclei also repel each other**, and so do the electrons. If the atoms come too close, the repulsion wins.
4. **The bond length is the balance point**, where attraction and repulsion together give the lowest energy. For two hydrogen atoms it is 74 picometres apart.
5. **Pulling the atoms apart takes energy**, because you have to work against that attraction. For a mole of hydrogen molecules it takes about 436 kilojoules. That is why breaking bonds always costs energy, an idea that comes back in Chapter 5.

:::careful
> "Atoms share electrons because they want a full outer shell of eight."

The "eight" is a useful pattern for many atoms of the lighter elements, and Lewis's drawings work beautifully for them. But it is a pattern, not a reason. The reason is that shared electrons between two nuclei lower the energy of the whole system, through electric attraction. And plenty of stable molecules break the eight rule.
:::

#### Pauling makes it scientific

:::scene Pasadena, California, 1930s

Lewis's picture was a sketch. To turn it into science that could calculate, chemists needed the new quantum physics of the 1920s. The person who did more than anyone to bring the two together was Linus Pauling, a young chemist at Caltech who as a boy had collected insects and minerals in Oregon.

Pauling explained why carbon makes four bonds pointing to the corners of a tetrahedron, why some bonds are shared unequally (his scale of electronegativity, in another story), and how to predict bond lengths and energies. In 1939 he collected it all in a book, *The Nature of the Chemical Bond*, which became one of the most influential science books of the century. He won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1954, and later a second Nobel, for Peace, for his campaign against nuclear weapons testing. He is still the only person to have won two Nobel Prizes on his own, not shared with anyone else.

#### Lines, dots and real molecules

So when you see a line between two atoms in this book, read it as Lewis taught: *two shared electrons, held between two nuclei by electric attraction.* A double line is two shared pairs; a triple line, three. More shared pairs pull the atoms closer and hold them tighter: the C=C double bond is shorter and stronger than C–C, and the N≡N triple bond in nitrogen gas is one of the strongest bonds there is, which is why nitrogen in the air is so hard to make react.
