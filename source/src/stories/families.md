:::scene Crawford Market, Mumbai, any afternoon

Walk through a big Mumbai market and the periodic table is all around you, though nobody has labelled it. The salt in the spice stalls is sodium and chlorine. The steel tiffin boxes are iron with chromium and nickel. The copper handi in the pot shop, the aluminium pressure cooker, the neon-red shop sign, the paan seller's tin of chuna, the phone in your pocket with its lithium battery and its tiny speaker magnet: every one of them is a member of a family.

Chemists first noticed the families two hundred years ago. In 1817 Johann Döbereiner, in Jena, saw that some elements came in threes with matching habits. You can read that story in the periodic-table story. Here is the cast list as we know it now, column by column, with where to meet each family yourself.

#### The alkali metals (column 1)

Lithium, sodium, potassium, rubidium, caesium, francium. Every one has a single electron alone in its outer shell, and it is held loosely, so these metals hand it over at the slightest chance. They are soft enough to cut with a knife, so reactive that they are stored under oil, and they react with water more fiercely the further down you go: lithium fizzes, sodium skates about, potassium bursts into lilac flame. You will never see the metals themselves at home, but their ions are everywhere: sodium in salt, potassium in bananas and coconut water, lithium in the battery of every phone.

#### The alkaline earth metals (column 2)

Beryllium, magnesium, calcium, strontium, barium, radium. Two outer electrons, a little more firmly held, so they are tougher and less wild than their neighbours. Calcium is in your bones and teeth, in milk, in the marble of old buildings and in chuna, the slaked lime the paan seller spreads on a leaf (it is strongly alkaline, which is why it must never go near eyes). Magnesium sits at the heart of every chlorophyll molecule, so every green leaf of palak contains it. Strontium makes the crimson of Diwali rockets, and barium the green.

#### The transition metals (the sunken middle)

Iron, copper, zinc, chromium, nickel, silver, gold, platinum and their neighbours. These are the hard, shiny, useful metals of everyday life. Their newest electrons go into an inner d room, so their outer surfaces look much alike and they mix happily into alloys. Most steel kitchen vessels in India are stainless steel, iron mixed with chromium and usually some nickel (the best grade, often stamped 18/8, has about 18 percent chromium and 8 percent nickel), the chromium forming an invisible skin that stops rust. Copper carries electricity through the wires in your walls. Zinc coats nails and buckets to protect them. Many transition metals make coloured compounds: the blue of copper sulfate, the green of a copper statue, the reds and yellows of iron in the soil.

#### The boron group and the carbon group (columns 13 and 14)

Here the family characters start to change as you go down a column, from non-metal at the top to metal at the bottom. In column 13, aluminium is the metal of foil and pressure cookers; indium, below it, is in the clear electrical coating on your phone's touchscreen. Column 14 begins with carbon, the backbone of every living thing (the whole of Chapter 10), then silicon, which as silicon dioxide makes up the quartz in most sand and glass, and which is the heart of the chips in every computer, then tin and lead, which are plainly metals.

#### The nitrogen and oxygen families (columns 15 and 16)

Nitrogen makes up 78 percent of the air you breathe; phosphorus, below it, is on the striking strip of every matchbox and in the backbone of your DNA. Oxygen is the most abundant element in your body by mass; sulfur, just below, is behind the sharp smell of cut onions and of hing in the tadka. Same outer electrons, very different looks: a reminder that families share chemistry, not appearance.

#### The halogens (column 17)

Fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine, astatine. Each is one electron short of the familiar closed-shell count. An isolated halogen atom releases energy when it captures a free electron, but removing that electron from another atom costs energy: the whole transfer needs a complete energy account. The familiar halogens are reactive and can be hazardous; they also have striking colours: pale yellow fluorine, yellow-green chlorine, red-brown liquid bromine, nearly black iodine with a purple vapour. Tamed as ions, they are everywhere. Chlorine disinfects city tap water and is the "chlor" in bleach. Fluoride in toothpaste hardens teeth. Most salt sold in India has a little iodine added, because a lack of iodine harms growing children.

#### The noble gases (column 18)

Helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon, radon. Full outer shells, so they almost never react: for a century chemists barely noticed them, until William Ramsay's work in the 1890s (his life story tells it). Neon glows orange-red in signs; argon fills welding torches and old-style light bulbs; helium lifts party balloons.

#### The lanthanides and actinides (the floating strip)

The lanthanides, once called the rare earths, are fifteen near-twins that chemists took a century to separate. They are hidden inside modern life: neodymium makes the tiny powerful magnets in phone speakers and earphones; a coating containing cerium helps turn the blue light of a white LED bulb into white. The actinides are all radioactive. Uranium fuels nuclear power stations, including India's first, at Tarapur, about a hundred kilometres north of Mumbai, which started up in 1969. Thorium matters especially to India, which has large reserves of it in the monazite sands of Kerala; Homi Bhabha planned India's nuclear programme around using it one day.

:::careful
> "Elements in the same family look the same."

They share outer electrons, so they share *chemical habits*: what they combine with and in what ratios. They can look completely different. Fluorine is a gas and iodine is a solid; carbon is a black powder or a diamond while lead is a soft grey metal. Look for the family resemblance in how they react, not in how they look.
:::

:::home A family hunt in the kitchen
With an adult, go through the kitchen and the bathroom and read labels. Make a chart with a column for each family and add every element you can find a sign of: sodium and chlorine in salt, iodine in iodised salt, sodium in baking soda (sodium hydrogencarbonate), calcium in chuna or calcium tablets, aluminium in foil, iron, chromium and nickel in steel vessels, copper in a copper vessel or wire, fluorine in toothpaste, carbon in sugar. Read and look only: do not open or taste anything that is not ordinary food, and leave chuna and cleaning products closed. Which family turns up most? Which can you not find at all?
:::

When you meet a new element, ask first: which family is it in? That one question tells you more about how it will behave than any other fact you could learn about it.
