:::scene Heidelberg, Germany, 1859

Think of Diwali night. A rocket bursts into deep red, then a shower of green, then a sparkle of brilliant gold. Those colours are not paint. Each one is made by a different element, glowing with its own particular light: strontium for red, barium for green, copper compounds for blue, sodium for that bright yellow-orange. The people who make fireworks learned this by trial and error. The reason it works was discovered by two friends in a German university town.

Robert Bunsen was a chemist who had helped design a new gas burner with a clean, almost invisible blue flame (you may meet a Bunsen burner in a school laboratory). Bunsen liked to hold a bit of a substance in the flame on a wire and watch its colour. Lithium gives crimson, potassium a soft lilac, sodium a strong yellow. This is called a **flame test**. But colours can be confusing to the eye: a little sodium drowns out almost anything else.

His friend Gustav Kirchhoff, a physicist, suggested a better way to look. Pass the coloured light through a prism, which spreads light out into its separate colours. Together they built an instrument for it, the **spectroscope**.

#### Step 1: light in lines

What they saw changed science. A hot glowing solid, like the filament in an old bulb, gives a smooth rainbow with every colour. But a glowing *gas* or *vapour* of an element gives only a few sharp, bright lines of particular colours, always in the same places for the same element. Sodium gives a close pair of yellow lines. Lithium gives a red line. Every element has its own pattern, as unique as a fingerprint.

:::fig s_spectra() | Each element's own pattern of bright lines, spread out by a prism. Hydrogen, sodium and helium are shown; the numbers are wavelengths of light in nanometres.

(Why the lines? Nobody knew for another fifty years. The answer, from Niels Bohr in 1913, is that electrons in each kind of atom can only jump between certain energy steps, and each jump gives out light of one exact colour.)

#### Step 2: finding elements nobody had seen

Bunsen and Kirchhoff realised they now had a detective's tool. If a sample glowed with a line that matched no known element, it must contain a new one.

In 1860 they boiled down an enormous amount of mineral water from a spa town, tens of thousands of litres of it, and looked at the glow of what was left. There were two bright blue lines that belonged to no known element. They named the new element **caesium**, from the Latin for sky blue. The next year, in a mineral, they found two deep red lines and named that element **rubidium**, from the Latin for dark red. Two new elements, found by their colours before anyone had held a pure piece of either.

#### Step 3: reading the Sun

Kirchhoff went further. Decades earlier, the glass-maker Joseph von Fraunhofer had noticed that sunlight, spread into a rainbow, is crossed by hundreds of thin *dark* lines. Kirchhoff showed that a cooler gas absorbs exactly the same colours that it gives off when hot. So the dark lines in sunlight are fingerprints of elements in the Sun's outer layers, absorbing light from the hotter Sun beneath. Sodium's dark pair sat exactly where sodium's bright yellow pair would be. For the first time, people could tell what a star is made of, without ever going there.

:::scene Guntur, India, 18 August 1868

On that day, a total eclipse of the Sun crossed India. The French astronomer Jules Janssen set up his spectroscope at Guntur, in what is now Andhra Pradesh, and studied the glowing gas leaping off the edge of the darkened Sun. He saw a bright yellow line close to sodium's, but not quite in the same place. The English astronomer Norman Lockyer saw the same line from England two months later, without needing an eclipse.

No known element on Earth made that line. Lockyer concluded it came from a new element, found so far only in the Sun, and named it after the Greek word for the Sun, *helios*: **helium**. Many chemists were doubtful. An element nobody could put in a bottle?

They had to wait twenty-seven years. In 1895 William Ramsay in London dissolved a uranium mineral in acid, collected the gas that came off, and looked at its glow. There was the yellow line. Helium had been found on Earth, after first being discovered 150 million kilometres away.

#### Step 4: why this matters

1. Every element gives out and absorbs light of its own particular colours.
2. So light carries information about the atoms that made it.
3. That lets us identify elements in tiny amounts, in the lab, in fireworks, in street lamps, and in stars millions of millions of kilometres away.

:::home Spot it in the kitchen
Next time an adult is cooking on a gas stove and a pan of salted water boils over, watch the flame where the water lands: the blue flame flashes bright yellow-orange. That is sodium from the salt, giving off its fingerprint colour. (Just watch: don't put anything into a flame yourself.) You can also see the same yellow in the old orange street lamps, which are filled with sodium vapour.
:::

The next time you watch fireworks, you are watching a spectroscope experiment in the sky. Every colour is an element announcing itself.
