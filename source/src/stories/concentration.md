:::scene A refugee camp at Bangaon, West Bengal, 1971

In 1971, during the war that created Bangladesh, millions of people fled across the border into India. In the crowded camps near the border, cholera broke out. Cholera kills by draining water and salt out of the body through terrible diarrhoea. The usual treatment was a drip of salt solution straight into a vein, but there were far too few drips, needles and nurses for so many sick people.

A young Indian doctor, Dilip Mahalanabis, decided to try something that had only just been tested in hospitals: a drink. Not any drink, but water with salt and sugar dissolved in it in exactly the right amounts, so that the gut could soak up the water. Families could give it to their own children with a cup. In his camp, the share of cholera patients who died is reported to have fallen from around 30 in every 100, as in camps without enough treatment, to fewer than 4 in every 100. That drink, oral rehydration solution or ORS, has since saved tens of millions of lives around the world. And the secret of it is one word: *concentration*.

#### Step 1: strong and weak

You already know what concentration means; you just may not have the word yet. A glass of sharbat made with lots of syrup is "strong". With a splash of syrup and a lot of water, it is "weak". The syrup is the ==solute== (the thing dissolved) and the water is the ==solvent== (the thing it dissolves in).

But "strong" and "weak" are not good enough for a doctor. Too little salt in ORS and it does not work; too much, and it can pull water the wrong way and make things worse. You need a number.

#### Step 2: how much in each litre

The trick is to ask how much solute there is *in each litre* of the finished drink. That number does not depend on how big the jug is.

:::work
2 spoons of syrup in 1 litre: 2 spoons per litre.
4 spoons of syrup in 2 litres: still 2 spoons per litre. Same strength, more drink.
2 spoons of syrup in 2 litres: 1 spoon per litre. Half the strength.
:::

Chemists do exactly the same, but instead of spoons they count [moles](#story-mole) of particles. Concentration is written in **moles per litre**, mol/L:

:::eq
**concentration = amount of solute (in moles) ÷ volume of solution (in litres)**
:::

#### Step 3: a real recipe

The World Health Organization's ORS recipe for one litre of water includes 2.6 grams of table salt (sodium chloride) and 13.5 grams of glucose. Let us turn those into concentrations.

:::work
Salt, NaCl, has a molar mass of about 58.4 g per mole.
2.6 g ÷ 58.4 g/mol ≈ 0.045 mol in 1 litre, so 0.045 mol/L of salt.
Glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆, has a molar mass of about 180 g per mole.
13.5 g ÷ 180 g/mol ≈ 0.075 mol in 1 litre, so 0.075 mol/L of glucose.
:::

(The full recipe also contains a little potassium chloride and sodium citrate, which bring the total sodium up to about 0.075 mol/L, matched to the glucose. The match is the clever part: the gut takes up sodium and glucose together, and water follows them in.)

Please do not mix medicines at home: ORS comes in ready-made packets for a reason. But now you can read the packet like a chemist.

#### Step 4: watering down, without losing anything

What happens when you add more water? The number of solute particles does not change. They just spread through more liquid. This is ==dilution==.

:::work
Start: 0.5 mol of salt in 1 litre: 0.5 mol/L.
Add water until the total is 2 litres. Still 0.5 mol of salt.
New concentration = 0.5 ÷ 2 = 0.25 mol/L. Double the volume, half the concentration.
:::

Notice the word "total". The volume in the rule is the volume of the *finished solution*, not the volume of water you poured in. If you dissolve a lot of sugar, the solution takes up more room than the water did.

:::careful
> "If I pour half the sharbat into another glass, each glass is half as strong."

No: each glass is exactly as strong as the jug. Pouring some away removes solute *and* solvent in the same proportion, so the amount per litre stays the same. What changes is the total amount of syrup in each glass. Concentration is about "how crowded", not "how much altogether".
:::

#### Step 5: some concentrations from real life

:::work
Seawater: about 0.46 mol of salt per litre (around 27 grams of sodium chloride in each litre).
Your blood: about 0.14 mol of sodium ions per litre.
Two teaspoons of sugar (about 8 g) in a 250 mL cup of chai: about 0.09 mol/L of sugar.
:::

That last one explains why seawater is no use for quenching thirst: it is more than three times as salty as your blood, so drinking it makes your body lose water to get rid of the extra salt.

:::home A dilution staircase
You need five clear glasses, water and food colouring (or strong tea). Put a few drops of colouring in the first glass with 100 mL of water. Pour half of it (50 mL) into the second glass and add 50 mL of water. Pour half of *that* into the third glass and add 50 mL of water, and so on. Each glass is half as concentrated as the one before. Line them up against a white sheet of paper. Can you still see colour in the fifth glass? It is 16 times more dilute than the first.
:::

So when this book talks about 0.10 mol/L of an acid, picture a jug with a recipe on it: so many moles of particles, crowded into each litre. That one number told a doctor in 1971 exactly how much salt and sugar a drink needed to save a life.
