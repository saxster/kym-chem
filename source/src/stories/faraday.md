:::scene A bookbinder's shop, Blandford Street, London, 1805

Michael Faraday was born in 1791 in a poor part of London. His father was a blacksmith who was often too ill to work, and there were days when Michael's share of food for a whole week was a single loaf of bread. He went to a simple day school long enough to learn reading, writing and arithmetic, and that was the end of his schooling.

At about fourteen he was apprenticed to a bookbinder, a kind man called George Riebau. A bookbinder sews and glues the pages of books into their covers. Faraday did something most apprentices did not: he *read* the books he was binding. One was an encyclopaedia with a long article on electricity. Another was a chemistry book written for beginners by Jane Marcet, *Conversations on Chemistry*. He tried the experiments he read about, with whatever bits of glass and wire he could afford.

#### Four tickets that changed a life

:::scene The Royal Institution, London, 1812

In 1812 a customer of the shop gave Faraday tickets to four lectures by the most famous chemist in London, Humphry Davy. Faraday sat high up in the gallery and wrote down everything. Afterwards he copied his notes out neatly, added drawings, and bound them into a book.

His apprenticeship was ending and he dreaded spending his life in trade. So he did something bold: he sent the bound notes to Davy and asked for any job at all in science. Davy was impressed, but had nothing to offer. Then, in early 1813, Davy's laboratory assistant was dismissed after a fight, and Faraday got the job. He was twenty-one. His work included washing bottles and cleaning the apparatus.

#### A hard journey

:::scene Paris, Florence, Rome, 1813 to 1815

Later in 1813 Davy set off on a long tour of Europe, even though Britain and France were at war (Napoleon gave him special permission). He took Faraday as his assistant. Faraday met famous scientists, saw a volcano, and watched Davy use a great burning lens in Florence to burn a diamond and prove it was made of carbon.

But there was a bitter side. Davy's valet refused to come, so Faraday was asked to act as a servant too. Davy's wife, Lady Davy, treated him as one. In letters home he wrote how unhappy this made him. He nearly gave up science and went home. He did not, and the tour gave him an education no university could have.

#### Naming the words you use

Back in London, Faraday became a brilliant chemist in his own right. In 1825 he discovered benzene, a liquid that turned out to be one of the most important compounds in all of carbon chemistry (Chapter 10).

He was even more famous for his work on electricity. He built the first electric motor (1821) and found how to make electricity from magnets (1831), the idea behind every power station today. And he worked out the rules of what happens when electricity passes through a solution and splits it, which he called **electrolysis**.

He needed words for the new ideas, and he asked a scholar friend, William Whewell, to help him make them up from Greek. Together, in 1834, they gave us:

* **electrode**: the metal or carbon rod where the current goes in or out (from Greek for "electricity" and "road");
* **anode** and **cathode**: the two electrodes ("the way up" and "the way down");
* **ion**: the charged particle that travels through the liquid (from Greek for "going", because ions move).

Every time you read *ion* in this book, you are using a word Faraday and Whewell invented.

#### The Christmas Lectures

:::scene The Royal Institution lecture theatre, Christmas, 1860

Faraday never forgot the boy in the gallery. In 1825 he started lectures at the Royal Institution for young people at Christmas. They still happen every year, and are shown on television in Britain.

His most famous set, given in 1860, was called *The Chemical History of a Candle*. For six lectures he talked about nothing but a candle, and through it explained almost all of chemistry: melting, burning, the gases in air, the water and carbon dioxide made by a flame, even breathing. He said, roughly, that there was no better door into science than studying a candle.

:::home Watch a candle like Faraday
**An adult must light the candle and stay with you; this is for looking only.** Put a plain candle in a holder on a plate, away from anything that can burn. Look closely: the little cup of melted wax at the top, the wick drawing liquid wax up, the dark zone near the wick and the bright yellow part above it. Hold a cold metal spoon briefly above the flame (the adult can do this): it gets a black coating of soot, which is unburnt carbon. Then hold a cold dry glass upside down well above the flame for a few seconds: mist forms inside. That is water, made by burning. A candle is a small chemistry factory.
:::

#### Humble to the end

Faraday became world-famous, but stayed simple and modest. He belonged to a small, strict Christian church and lived quietly with his wife, Sarah, above the Royal Institution. It is often said that he turned down a knighthood and, later, the presidency of the Royal Society, wanting to remain "plain Michael Faraday" to the end. The story about the presidency is well recorded: he was offered it twice. The knighthood story is less certain: he said more than once that he would never accept one, but no record has been found that he was ever offered it.

He worked on almost every great question of his day, but his own teachers had never been professors: they were the books he bound and the man whose lectures he copied. He died in 1867.

When you meet ions and electrolysis in Chapters 2 and 8, remember the apprentice who read the books he was meant to be sewing, and who named the very words you are reading.
