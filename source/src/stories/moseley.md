:::scene Weymouth, England, 1887

Henry Gwyn Jeffreys Moseley was born on 23 November 1887, into a family of scientists. His father was a naturalist who had sailed round the world on a famous survey ship, HMS *Challenger*, collecting sea creatures; his grandfather on his mother's side was an expert on shells. His father died before Henry was four, and he grew up with his mother and sisters, collecting birds' eggs, studying animals, and doing very well at school. He won a scholarship to Eton, and then went to Trinity College, Oxford, to study physics.

He was quiet, intense, and extremely hard-working. People who worked with him remembered that he would carry on an experiment through the night, and that he was impatient with anyone who wasted his time.

#### A puzzle in the periodic table

:::scene Manchester, 1913

In 1910 Moseley went to Manchester to work with Ernest Rutherford, who had just discovered the atomic nucleus. By then, the periodic table had been in use for forty years. Mendeleev had arranged the elements roughly in order of their atomic weight, and it worked beautifully. But there were a few awkward places where it did not. Tellurium is *heavier* than iodine, yet iodine plainly belongs in the column after tellurium, alongside chlorine and bromine. Mendeleev had swapped them, trusting their chemistry over their weight. Nobody could explain why that was allowed.

Some scientists had begun to suspect that the true order was not about weight at all, but about the **charge on the nucleus**: how many units of positive charge sat at the centre of the atom. But nobody could measure that charge.

Moseley found a way. He knew that when you fire fast electrons at a metal, it gives off X-rays, which are light of extremely high energy, far beyond violet. Each metal gives off X-rays of its own particular energies. Using a crystal to split the X-rays (the trick the Braggs had just invented, see the crystals story), Moseley measured them for one element after another: calcium, titanium, vanadium, chromium, manganese, iron, cobalt, nickel, copper, zinc.

#### The staircase

When he plotted his results in the right way (using the square root of the X-ray frequency), the elements lined up like a nearly even staircase within each X-ray spectral series. The square-root frequency rose by approximately the same amount from one element to the next; small departures did not erase the pattern.

It meant that each element has exactly *one more* unit of charge on its nucleus than the one before. Hydrogen has 1, helium 2, lithium 3, and so on, all the way up. That number is the **atomic number**, and Moseley had found a way to measure it directly. It is the true order of the periodic table. It explained the tellurium and iodine puzzle at once: tellurium is number 52 and iodine is number 53, whatever their weights say. (The weights are muddled because of neutrons, which add weight but no charge. That was discovered later.)

#### Gaps you can count

There was an even more exciting consequence. If every step on the staircase is one element, then you can see exactly where steps are *missing*. Moseley, and others using his method, could say: between molybdenum (42) and ruthenium (44) there should be an element number 43, and nobody has found it. The same for 61, 72 and 75. No more guessing about how many elements might be hiding. You could count the gaps.

All of them were later filled. Element 72, hafnium, was found in 1923 by scientists in Bohr's institute in Copenhagen, who searched for it in zirconium ores *because* the staircase told them where to look. Element 75, rhenium, followed in 1925. Element 43, technetium, turned out not to exist naturally on Earth in any useful amount; it was made artificially in 1937. Element 61, promethium, was finally identified in the 1940s.

#### The war

Moseley did all of this in about two years. He moved back to Oxford in 1914 to continue, and many people expected that he would one day win a Nobel Prize.

In the summer of 1914 he travelled to Australia for a scientific meeting. While he was there, the First World War began. He came home and, against the advice of people who knew how valuable his work was, joined the army as a signals officer, setting up telephone and telegraph lines. In 1915 he was sent to Gallipoli, in what is now Turkey. On 10 August 1915, during the fighting there, he was killed. He was 27 years old.

Many scientists felt his loss very deeply, Rutherford among them. It is often said that his death made the British government more careful about sending its scientists into battle in later wars, though it is hard to know how much difference it really made.

:::home Find the missing step
Here is Moseley's idea with coins. Ask someone to make a row of stacks of coins: a stack of 1, then 2, then 3, and so on up to 12, but to secretly leave out one stack and push the others together so there is no gap. Now look at the row. You cannot easily see which is missing just by glancing at the heights. But count the coins in each stack in order (1, 2, 3, 4, 6 ...) and the missing number jumps out. Moseley found the "coin count" of every element, and the missing elements jumped out in the same way.
:::

Every time you see an element's atomic number in this book, the small whole number in the corner of its box in the periodic table, you are using what Henry Moseley found in two years in Manchester.
