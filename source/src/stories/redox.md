:::scene The Royal Institution, London, October 1807

Humphry Davy had a new toy: one of the most powerful batteries in Britain, hundreds of metal plates stacked in troughs of acid. He was using it to tear substances apart with electricity. On 6 October 1807 he pointed it at a lump of potash, a substance made from wood ash that chemists had long suspected contained an unknown metal but had never managed to split.

He took a small piece of potash, just damp enough from the air to carry a current, and passed the current through it until it began to melt. At one wire, tiny bright globules of metal appeared, "having a high metallic lustre", as he wrote. Some of them burst into flame at once. Davy's cousin Edmund, who was assisting him, later remembered that Humphry was so delighted he danced about the room. He had discovered potassium. A few days later he did the same with soda and found sodium.

Why was it so hard to get these metals out, and why did they burst into flame the moment they appeared? The answer is one of the most important ideas in chemistry, and it began with rust.

#### Step 1: the old meaning, adding oxygen

When Lavoisier showed in the 1770s that burning is a reaction with oxygen, chemists started calling any reaction that adds oxygen to a substance ==oxidation==. Iron slowly combining with oxygen and water to make flaky red ==rust== is oxidation. So is wood burning, and a cut apple turning brown. The reverse, taking oxygen away from a compound to get the metal back, they called ==reduction==, from a Latin word meaning "to lead back": the metal was led back out of its ore. People had been doing reduction for thousands of years without the word, every time they heated iron ore with charcoal in a furnace to make iron.

#### Step 2: the new meaning, moving electrons

After the electron was discovered in 1897, chemists looked again at these reactions and saw something deeper. When iron combines with oxygen, the iron atoms lose electrons and the oxygen atoms gain them. And exactly the same kind of electron loss happens in many reactions that involve no oxygen at all, like zinc reacting with copper ions in Chapter 8. So the words were widened:

:::work
**Oxidation** is losing electrons.
**Reduction** is gaining electrons.
:::

A favourite way to remember it is **OIL RIG**: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain.

#### Step 3: always together

Electrons do not float around loose in a beaker; if one substance loses them, another must gain them. So oxidation and reduction always happen together, as a pair. Chemists call the pair a ==redox== reaction (from REDuction and OXidation). Here is one you can picture:

:::eq
Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu
:::

Each zinc atom loses two electrons (it is oxidised); each copper ion gains them (it is reduced). Count the charge on each side: +2 before, +2 after. Electrons were passed, not created.

#### Step 4: who gives electrons most easily?

Line up metals by how readily they lose electrons in reactions like this and you get the ==reactivity series==. Near the top are potassium and sodium, which give up electrons so readily that they react with water and air almost instantly; that is why Davy's globules burst into flame. Lower down come magnesium, zinc and iron; further down, copper, silver and gold, which hardly react at all. That is why gold coins dug up after two thousand years still shine, while an iron nail rusts in a single wet monsoon.

It also explains why Davy needed his giant battery. Metals at the top of the series are the hardest to get back from their compounds, because their atoms have lost electrons so readily. To push the electrons back onto them takes a big electrical shove.

:::careful
> "Oxidation always involves oxygen."

It used to mean that, and the name comes from oxygen. But today chemists use it for any loss of electrons. When magnesium burns in chlorine gas, there is no oxygen anywhere, yet the magnesium is oxidised, because it loses electrons to the chlorine.
:::

#### Redox all around you

A green statue: the Statue of Liberty in New York was a shiny copper colour when it was put up in 1886. Over the next few decades, rain and air slowly oxidised the copper surface into a crust of green copper compounds, and that crust now protects the metal underneath. Indian temple bells and old copper pots go green in the same way.

A tarnished spoon: silver turns black when it meets traces of sulfur compounds in air or food (eggs are famous for it), because the silver is oxidised to black silver sulfide.

Your own breath: inside your cells, the food you eat is slowly oxidised by the oxygen you breathe, passing electrons step by step along a chain of proteins. That controlled redox reaction is where your energy comes from.

And every battery, from Volta's pile to the one in your pocket, is a redox reaction arranged so that the electrons have to take the long way round through a wire.

:::home The browning apple test
You need an apple, a knife (ask an adult), a lemon and a plate. Cut two slices of apple. Squeeze lemon juice all over one slice and leave the other bare. Put both on the plate and look every 15 minutes for an hour. The bare slice turns brown as natural substances in the apple are oxidised by oxygen from the air, helped along by an enzyme. The lemon slice stays pale for much longer: the acid slows the enzyme down, and the vitamin C in lemon juice is oxidised more easily than the apple's own substances, so it takes the hit first. You can eat both slices afterwards.
:::

So rust on a gate, a flame in a grate and an apple on a plate are the same story, told with electrons. Chapter 8 follows those electrons one at a time.
