:::scene A study table at home, the night before a test

It is late, the chapter is open, and the periodic table stares back: 118 boxes, every one with a symbol, a name and a number. The temptation is to read it over and over until it sticks. That is the method most people use, and research on learning suggests it is one of the least effective there is. This story is about better ways, and about something even more useful: how to *understand* the table so well that you hardly need to memorise it at all.

#### Step 1: understand before you memorise

A chemist who has forgotten where molybdenum goes can still draw the periodic table, because the shape is not arbitrary. It is the 2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32, 32 pattern of electron rooms (the table-shape story explains why). If you can draw the skeleton from memory, you already know where every block is and roughly where every element must sit.

So learn the skeleton first, as a drawing, not as a list:

:::work
Row 1: one box on the far left, one on the far right (hydrogen and helium).
Rows 2 and 3: two boxes on the left, six on the right, a gap between.
Rows 4 and 5: two on the left, a block of ten in the middle, six on the right: 18.
Rows 6 and 7: the same, plus a strip of fourteen floated underneath: 32.
:::

Then learn the families, because a family tells you more than a name ever will. Column 1 is the soft, violent alkali metals; column 17 the fierce halogens; column 18 the calm noble gases (the families story introduces them all). Once you know that sodium is in column 1, you know a dozen things about it without memorising any of them.

#### Step 2: memorise strategically

Not all 118 boxes deserve equal effort. Most of the chemistry in this book uses the first twenty elements, plus a handful of famous metals (iron, copper, zinc, silver, gold, mercury, lead) and the halogens bromine and iodine. Learn those well and you can read almost any chemistry you will meet for years. The rest you can always look up, and you will find you remember more of them each time you do.

The first twenty, in order, are hydrogen, helium, lithium, beryllium, boron, carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, fluorine, neon, sodium, magnesium, aluminium, silicon, phosphorus, sulfur, chlorine, argon, potassium and calcium.

#### Step 3: use methods that actually work

Psychologists have studied memory for more than a century, since Hermann Ebbinghaus in Germany in the 1880s memorised long lists of nonsense syllables and measured how fast he forgot them. Three findings matter most for you.

**Test yourself instead of rereading.** In a well-known 2006 experiment, Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke in the United States had students study a passage, then either study it again or take a practice test. A week later, the students who had *tested* themselves remembered much more. The struggle to pull something out of your memory is what makes it stick. Reading the table again feels productive; covering it and trying to write it down actually is.

**Space it out.** A large review of hundreds of experiments, published in 2006 by Nicholas Cepeda and colleagues, found that the same amount of practice spread over several days beats cramming it into one session, often by a lot. Ten minutes a day for a week works better than seventy minutes the night before. Each time you are about to forget something and you pull it back, the memory becomes stronger. This idea is called **spaced repetition**.

**Chunk it.** In 1956 the psychologist George Miller pointed out that we hold only a few separate items in mind at once, but each item can be a big "chunk". Do not learn twenty separate elements; learn four rows of the table, or the families, as chunks.

#### Step 4: tricks that help the hard parts

For lists that have no logic, like the order of the first twenty, memory tricks really do help.

A **mnemonic** is a sentence whose words start with the same letters as the things you need to remember. The best ones are the ones you make yourself, because making one is itself a kind of practice, and silly or vivid ones stick best. Here is one we made up for this book, with a Mumbai flavour:

:::eq
**H**ungry **He**ns **Li**ke **Be**l **B**ut **C**an't **N**ibble **O**ur **F**ried **Ne**ems; **Na**ughty **M**onkeys **Al**ways **Si**lently **P**inch **S**amosas, **Cl**ever **Ar**jun **K**eeps **Ca**lm.
:::

(Bel is the wood-apple fruit; neem leaves are bitter, so a fried neem would indeed be hard to nibble.) Now make a better one of your own.

A **memory palace** is an older trick. According to a story told by the Roman writer Cicero, the Greek poet Simonides survived the collapse of a banquet hall and could name the dead by remembering where each guest had been sitting. Whether or not that happened, the method works: imagine walking through a place you know well, such as your home, and leave each element at a spot along the way. Hydrogen in a balloon at the front door, helium floating beside it, lithium in the phone on the shoe rack, and so on. To recall them, walk the route again in your mind.

There are also songs. The most famous is "The Elements" by the American musician Tom Lehrer, from 1959, which rattles through the names to a tune by Gilbert and Sullivan. If a tune helps you, make your own: a familiar melody with element names fitted to it can be remarkably sticky.

:::careful
> "Learning the periodic table means memorising all 118 boxes."

Chemists do not carry 118 boxes in their heads. They carry the shape, the families and the trends, plus the elements they use most, and they look up the rest. Understanding the pattern is worth far more than reciting it, because the pattern lets you predict, and reciting does not.
:::

#### Practise here

On the book's page "[The periodic table, inside out](#explore-table)" you will find a Practise tab, a memory trainer that quizzes you on symbols, names and positions and brings back the ones you got wrong more often: spaced repetition, done for you. Use it a little every day rather than a lot at once.

:::home A week with the skeleton
Every day for seven days, before you look at anything, take a blank sheet and draw the skeleton of the periodic table from memory: the towers, the sunken middle, the right-hand block, the floating strip. Then fill in as many of the first twenty elements as you can. Only then check against the real table and mark what you got wrong. Keep all seven sheets. On day seven, lay them side by side: you will see your memory grow, and you will have used testing, spacing and chunking all at once.
:::

The aim was never to become a walking list. It is to know the table the way you know your own neighbourhood: not every house number, but where everything is, and how to find the rest.
