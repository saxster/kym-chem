:::scene Padua, around 1593

Hot and cold seem like the simplest things in the world. You can feel them. But try this: put one hand in cold water and the other in hot water for a minute, then put both into the same bowl of lukewarm water. One hand says "warm", the other says "cold". Your skin is a terrible thermometer. For most of history nobody could measure how hot something was, only how hot it *felt*.

Around 1593 Galileo built a gadget called a thermoscope: a glass bulb of air on top of a thin tube dipped in water. When the air in the bulb warmed it expanded and pushed the water down; when it cooled the water rose. It showed *changes* in hotness, but it had no numbers, and it also wobbled with the weather, because the open water was being pushed by the air outside.

#### Step 1: putting numbers on hotness

The trick that turned a thermoscope into a thermometer was to choose fixed points: things that always happen at the same hotness, so that everyone's thermometer could agree. In 1724 the instrument-maker Daniel Fahrenheit, working in Amsterdam, published his scale, using sealed glass tubes of mercury that were far more reliable than anything before. In 1742 the Swedish astronomer Anders Celsius proposed a scale with 100 steps between the freezing and boiling points of water. Oddly, he put the boiling point at 0 and freezing at 100; within a few years others turned it the right way up, and that is the Celsius scale used in India today.

:::careful
> "40 °C is twice as hot as 20 °C."

It is not, and the reason is the heart of this story. The zero of the Celsius scale is just where water happens to freeze. It is not "no heat at all". Doubling a Celsius number does not double anything real, in the same way that doubling the floor number of a building does not double its height above the basement.
:::

#### Step 2: a gas points to the coldest possible cold

Around 1702 the French scientist Guillaume Amontons noticed something strange about air trapped in a sealed container. As he cooled it, its pressure dropped steadily, by the same amount for every degree. He wondered: if you kept cooling, the pressure would keep falling, and at some temperature it would reach zero. A gas cannot push less than not at all. So that temperature must be the coldest there can possibly be.

Over the next century, more careful measurements by Charles, Gay-Lussac and others showed the same thing for every gas they tried. Draw the straight lines of pressure against temperature, extend them backwards into the cold, and they all meet at zero pressure at the same place: about −273 °C. Today we know the exact value: −273.15 °C. It is called ==absolute zero==.

What happens there? Temperature, it turns out, is a measure of how vigorously particles are moving. At absolute zero they would have the very least motion that the laws of physics allow. You cannot go colder than that, for the same reason you cannot be shorter than zero centimetres.

:::scene Glasgow, 1848

In 1848 a 24-year-old professor at Glasgow University, William Thomson, proposed a temperature scale that starts at absolute zero itself. Thomson was a prodigy: he had started at university aged ten. Much later, Queen Victoria made him a lord, and he took his title from the River Kelvin that flows past the university, becoming Lord Kelvin. His scale is now called the ==kelvin== scale, and its unit is the kelvin, written K (with no degree sign).

#### Step 3: converting between Celsius and kelvin

The steps on the kelvin scale are exactly the same size as Celsius degrees. Only the starting point moves. So converting is just adding:

:::work
**kelvin = degrees Celsius + 273.15**
Water freezes: 0 °C = 273.15 K.
A warm Mumbai afternoon: 30 °C = 303.15 K.
Water boils: 100 °C = 373.15 K.
Absolute zero: −273.15 °C = 0 K.
:::

Now try the "twice as hot" question again, in kelvin. 20 °C is 293 K and 40 °C is 313 K. So 40 °C has only about 7% more of the jiggling we call temperature than 20 °C, not twice as much. To be truly twice as hot as a 20 °C room, in the physicist's sense, something would have to be at 586 K: about 313 °C, hotter than a kitchen oven.

This is why every gas law in this book uses kelvin. When a gas's kelvin temperature doubles, its particles have twice as much energy of motion, and if it is sealed in a rigid box, its pressure doubles too. That simple doubling only works if you count from the true zero.

#### Step 4: the race towards zero

Can anyone actually get to absolute zero? Scientists have been racing towards it for over a century. In 1908 the Dutch physicist Heike Kamerlingh Onnes turned helium into a liquid at about 4 K, and a few years later discovered that some metals lose all electrical resistance when that cold. Today laboratories can cool clouds of atoms to less than a billionth of a kelvin above absolute zero. But the laws of physics say you can never quite reach it: each step closer gets harder than the last. Even deep space is not that cold: the faint glow left over from the Big Bang keeps it at about 2.7 K.

:::home A thermometer's fixed point
If you have a kitchen thermometer, fill a glass with plenty of crushed ice and a little cold water, stir, and wait a few minutes. It should read very close to 0 °C. Add more ice, wait, and read it again: still 0 °C. While ice and water are both present, the temperature stays put, which is exactly why melting ice was chosen as a fixed point. (If your thermometer reads 1 or 2 degrees off, you have just calibrated it.)
:::

So whenever you see a temperature in kelvin in this book, remember what the number means: how far you are above the stillest possible state of matter, counted in steps the size of a Celsius degree.
