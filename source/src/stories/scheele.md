:::scene Stralsund, on the Baltic coast, 1742

Carl Wilhelm Scheele was the seventh of eleven children of a merchant in Stralsund, a town that then belonged to Sweden. At fourteen he was apprenticed to a pharmacist in Gothenburg. A pharmacy, or apothecary shop, was where medicines were made by hand from plants, minerals, acids and salts. To a boy who loved chemistry, it was a treasure house.

Scheele read every chemistry book in the shop, and at night, after work, he repeated the experiments in them. He never went to university. All his life he worked in pharmacies, and in the back rooms, with simple equipment and very little money, he became one of the greatest discoverers in the history of chemistry.

#### A list of discoveries

Scheele's list is astonishing. He was the first to identify or prepare many substances, including:

* the gas we now call **chlorine**, a greenish-yellow gas (he did not realise it was an element; Humphry Davy showed that later);
* acids from fruit and from milk, among them the acid of lemons (citric acid), of sour milk (lactic acid) and of apples (malic acid);
* glycerol, which is in soap and many foods;
* and important early work on manganese, barium, molybdenum and tungsten.

He also found that silver salts darken in light, especially violet light, a discovery that photography would later depend on. And a bright green pigment he invented, "Scheele's green", became fashionable for wallpaper and paint. Sadly, it contained arsenic, and in damp rooms it could make people ill. Scheele did not know that would happen.

#### Fire air

:::scene Uppsala, around 1771 to 1772

Scheele wanted to know what air is made of. He heated several different substances, among them a red powder made from mercury and a black mineral of manganese, and collected the gas that came off. A candle burned in it with dazzling brightness. He called it "fire air", because fire loved it. He also found that ordinary air contains some of this fire air and some "foul air" in which nothing will burn: oxygen and nitrogen, in today's words.

His notebooks suggest he did these experiments around 1771 or 1772, two or three years before Joseph Priestley found the same gas in England in 1774. But Scheele's book, *Chemical Observations and Experiments on Air and Fire*, was held up at the printer, and did not appear until 1777. By then Priestley had published, and Lavoisier had named the gas oxygen and explained burning.

So who discovered oxygen? Historians usually say Scheele found it first, Priestley published first, and Lavoisier understood it first. Science is often like that: a discovery is made by several people, each adding a piece.

#### A dangerous habit

Scheele had one habit that every chemist since has learned not to copy. In his day, chemists described substances by their colour, their smell, and their taste, and Scheele recorded the taste of many of the chemicals he made, including some that we now know are poisonous. He also worked for years with arsenic, mercury and lead, and breathed fumes in a small, poorly ventilated room.

He died in 1786, at only 43. Nobody knows the exact cause. Many historians think years of exposure to toxic chemicals damaged his health; others think he also had an illness unrelated to his work. Either way, it is a lesson that every chemistry lab in the world now teaches on the first day: **you never taste a chemical in a laboratory, and you never sniff one directly.** In this book the only things you ever taste are ordinary food.

#### A quiet, happy life

Scheele was offered jobs as a professor in Berlin and in England, but he turned them down. He preferred his own pharmacy in the small Swedish town of Köping, where he could work in peace. He was elected to the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences while still a pharmacist's assistant, which was a very unusual honour. Just days before he died, he married the widow of the pharmacist whose shop he had taken over, so that she would inherit his business.

:::home The acids in your fruit bowl
Scheele was the first to pull citric acid out of lemons and malic acid out of apples. You can taste the difference between them, because these are ordinary foods. Taste a tiny piece of lemon, then a slice of sour green apple, then a sweet ripe apple. The sharpness you notice comes mostly from the acids Scheele discovered. Now notice that a ripe apple tastes less sharp: as fruit ripens, the amount of acid falls and the amount of sugar rises.
:::

When you read in Chapter 2 about how the elements were found one by one, remember the young apprentice reading by candlelight in a Swedish pharmacy, who found more of chemistry's secrets than almost anyone, and did it all from a shop.
