:::scene Brightwater, New Zealand, 1871

Ernest Rutherford was born on 30 August 1871 near Nelson, at the top of New Zealand's South Island, the fourth of twelve children. His father was a wheelwright and flax-miller; his mother was a schoolteacher. There was not much money, but there were a lot of books, and young Ernest was the kind of boy who took clocks apart to see how they worked and built model water wheels in the creek.

He won scholarships at every step: to Nelson College, then to Canterbury College in Christchurch, where he did his first real experiments in a cold, draughty basement, building detectors for radio waves only a few years after they had been discovered. In 1895 he won a scholarship to study at Cambridge, on the other side of the world.

There is a famous story about that moment. The telegram arrived while he was digging potatoes in his family's garden, and he threw down his spade and said, "That's the last potato I'll ever dig." It is a lovely story, and it is told everywhere, but nobody wrote it down at the time, so treat it as a family legend rather than a fact.

#### The colonial in Cambridge

At the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge, Rutherford worked under J. J. Thomson, the man who would discover the electron. Some of the English students looked down on the loud young man from the colonies. It did not last. Within a few years Rutherford was famous for one thing above all: he could design simple, clever experiments that went straight to the heart of a question.

Radioactivity had just been discovered, and Rutherford threw himself into it. He found that the rays coming out of uranium were of at least two kinds, and he gave them the names we still use: *alpha* and *beta*.

#### Montreal, and the shock of transmutation

:::scene Montreal, Canada, 1901

In 1898 Rutherford became a professor at McGill University in Montreal. There he teamed up with a young English chemist, Frederick Soddy. Together they studied thorium, a radioactive element, and noticed something that seemed impossible: as it gave off radiation, a small amount of thorium was slowly *becoming a different element*.

For more than two thousand years, alchemists had dreamed of changing one element into another. Serious scientists had decided it could not be done. Now here it was, happening on its own, inside a lump of rock. Soddy later remembered blurting out, "Rutherford, this is transmutation!" and Rutherford snapping back, "For Mike's sake, Soddy, don't call it *transmutation*. They'll have our heads off as alchemists." (This is Soddy's own memory, told many years later, so the exact words may have drifted.)

In 1908 Rutherford won the Nobel Prize. He was a physicist through and through, and he rather looked down on chemistry, so he was amused to find it was the Nobel Prize in *Chemistry*. He joked that he had seen many transformations in his work, but none as quick as his own, from physicist to chemist.

#### Manchester and the gold foil

:::scene Manchester, 1909

By then he had moved to Manchester. He asked his assistant Hans Geiger and a twenty-year-old student, Ernest Marsden, to fire alpha particles at a sheet of gold foil thinner than a leaf, and to count where they went by watching for tiny flashes of light in a darkened room. Almost all of them flew straight through. But about one in every eight thousand bounced back.

Rutherford said it was as if you fired a large shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you. In 1911 he worked out what it meant: almost all the mass of an atom is packed into a tiny, positively charged centre, the **nucleus**, with the electrons spread out around it, far away. An atom is mostly empty space. Chapter 1 tells this story in full, and you can fire the alpha particles yourself in its figure.

#### The first person to change an element on purpose

In 1919, just before he went back to Cambridge to run the Cavendish Laboratory, Rutherford did something no alchemist had ever managed. He fired alpha particles into nitrogen gas and found hydrogen nuclei (protons) flying out. A few of the nitrogen nuclei had been hit so hard that they changed. Later work by his colleague Patrick Blackett showed exactly what happened: the alpha particle sticks to the nitrogen nucleus, a proton is knocked out, and what is left is a nucleus of **oxygen**.

So the first deliberate transmutation in history did not turn lead into gold. It turned nitrogen into oxygen, a handful of atoms at a time. It was worth nothing in money and everything in understanding.

#### The Crocodile

Rutherford was a big man with a big voice. His students said he sang "Onward, Christian Soldiers" loudly and badly as he walked round the laboratory, and there are stories (perhaps exaggerated) that his booming voice disturbed the delicate instruments. The Russian physicist Pyotr Kapitsa, who worked for him in Cambridge, nicknamed him "the Crocodile". A crocodile was carved on the wall of the laboratory Kapitsa built there, and you can still see it.

He could be gruff, but he was generous with his students, and he had a gift for picking good people and good problems. A remarkable number of the people who trained or worked with him went on to win Nobel Prizes of their own, among them Niels Bohr, Frederick Soddy, James Chadwick (who found the neutron), and John Cockcroft and Ernest Walton (who split the atom with a machine in 1932).

#### What became of him

Rutherford became Lord Rutherford of Nelson, named after the town near where he grew up. He died in 1937 and was buried in Westminster Abbey in London, close to Isaac Newton. Element 104 is called **rutherfordium** in his honour, and his face is on New Zealand's hundred-dollar note.

:::home Find a hidden shape without looking
This is how Rutherford "saw" the nucleus: by throwing things at it and watching how they bounced. Ask someone to put a small object (a book, a lunchbox, a box of tea) under a large tray or a sheet of cardboard, so you cannot see it. Kneel at the edge and roll marbles under the tray from different directions. Watch where they come out. Some roll straight through; some bounce off at angles. After twenty or thirty rolls, try to sketch the hidden object's shape and size. Then lift the tray and check. You were doing a scattering experiment.
:::

Whenever you read about the nucleus, or about one element turning into another, you are standing on the potato field in Brightwater, with the boy who threw down his spade.
