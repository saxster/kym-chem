:::scene Alexandria, Egypt, about the year 300

Picture a workshop in the great city of Alexandria, on the coast of Egypt. Furnaces glow. Clay pots bubble. A man called Zosimos, from the town of Panopolis, is writing down everything he knows about how to change one metal into another. He describes stills for boiling liquids and catching their vapour, and he writes about a woman he calls Mary the Jewess, who seems to have invented some of this equipment before him. Very little is known about Mary for certain, but a cooking method is still named after her: the *bain-marie*, a bowl set over hot water.

This was the beginning of **alchemy**, and for about the next fourteen hundred years some of the cleverest people in the world tried to do the same thing: turn cheap metals such as lead into gold.

#### Why it seemed possible

The idea was not silly at the time. Metals look alike: shiny, heavy, able to melt and be hammered. Copper mixed with zinc turns a golden colour. Ores dug from the ground change completely in a furnace. If dull rock can become bright metal, why shouldn't one metal become another? Many alchemists believed metals slowly "ripened" underground, the way fruit ripens, and that gold was the ripest of all. Perhaps, with the right substance, you could speed the ripening up. They called that imagined substance the **philosopher's stone**.

#### The Arabic chemists

When the Roman world faded, the work moved to Baghdad and the other great cities of the Islamic world. A large collection of books is credited to **Jabir ibn Hayyan**, who may have lived in the 700s. Historians argue about who really wrote them: some of the books were probably written by many different people over a long time and put under his famous name. Whoever wrote them, they describe careful laboratory work: purifying substances, crystallising them, and sorting them into groups.

A little later, in the late 800s and early 900s, the Persian doctor **Abu Bakr al-Razi** sorted substances into families, such as minerals, salts and spirits, and described his apparatus so clearly that you could rebuild it. He used distillation, the trick of boiling a liquid and cooling its vapour to collect something purer.

You still speak a little of their language every day. *Alchemy* comes from the Arabic *al-kīmiyā*, and *chemistry* comes from that. *Alcohol* and *alkali* are Arabic words too.

#### Paracelsus and the medicine-makers

In the 1500s a loud, quarrelsome Swiss doctor who called himself Paracelsus told alchemists to stop chasing gold and make medicines instead. He was rude to almost everyone, and some of his ideas were badly wrong, but he pushed alchemy towards chemistry: making useful substances and testing what they did.

:::scene Hamburg, 1669

Now for one of the strangest discoveries ever made. Hennig Brand, a merchant in Hamburg, was sure the secret of gold was hidden in human urine. It is yellow, after all. So he collected a great deal of it (stories say dozens of buckets, though nobody knows the exact amount), left it to stand for days, and boiled it down.

He did not get gold. At the bottom of his apparatus he got a white, waxy substance that glowed in the dark with a ghostly green light. It caught fire by itself in air. Brand had discovered **phosphorus**, from the Greek for "light-bringer". It is usually called the first element whose discoverer we know by name. The elements known before it, such as gold, iron, copper and sulfur, had been in use since ancient times, and nobody knows who first found them.

#### The secret alchemists

Alchemy did not end when modern science began. Robert Boyle, one of the founders of modern chemistry, practised alchemy all his life and hoped to see a metal transformed. Isaac Newton, the greatest scientist of his age, wrote more than a million words about alchemy, much of it in secret notebooks. Both men are in this book, and both have their own stories.

#### Why it could never work

Here is the answer the alchemists could not have known. Gold is an **element**: every gold atom has exactly 79 protons in its nucleus, and that number is what makes it gold. Lead has 82, and mercury has 80.

Chemistry, everything you do with fire, acids, stills and furnaces, only moves the electrons on the outside of atoms. It rearranges atoms into new partners. It never touches the nucleus in the middle. So no chemical reaction, however clever, can turn lead into gold. To change the number of protons, you have to break into the nucleus itself, and that takes energies millions of times greater than any furnace.

:::careful
> "So the alchemists were just foolish."

They were not. They had no way of knowing atoms existed, let alone nuclei. And while chasing gold they invented the tools of the laboratory: stills, flasks, furnaces, water baths, and the habit of writing down exactly what they did. They discovered acids, purified salts and found new elements. Modern chemistry grew out of their workshops.
:::

:::home Watch distillation happen
Ask an adult to pour a mug of very hot tap water. Hold a cold plate (straight from the fridge is best) a few centimetres above the mug for half a minute, then look underneath. Water vapour rising from the mug has cooled on the plate and turned back into drops of liquid. That is the heart of distillation, the trick al-Razi and the alchemists used to purify liquids. Don't use boiling water from a kettle, and keep your hand away from the steam.
:::

The alchemists' dream did eventually come true, in a way none of them could have imagined, inside particle accelerators and nuclear reactors. That is the story of **transmutation**.
