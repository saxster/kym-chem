:::scene Munich, 1912

Look at grains of table salt under a magnifying glass and you will see tiny cubes. Sugar grains are little slanted blocks. Snowflakes have six arms. Crystals have flat faces and sharp angles, and for centuries people guessed that this outer shape came from some inner order: atoms stacked in regular rows, like oranges on a market stall. But atoms are far too small to see. How could anyone check?

In 1912 a German physicist, Max von Laue, had an idea. X-rays had been discovered in 1895, and some scientists suspected they were a kind of light with extremely short waves, perhaps about as short as the spacing between atoms. If so, the rows of atoms in a crystal might scatter X-rays the way the fine grooves on a CD split light into rainbow colours. Two of his colleagues shone X-rays through a crystal onto a photographic plate. When they developed it, they found a beautiful pattern of spots. The crystal really was a regular grid of atoms, and X-rays could read it.

#### A father and son

:::scene Leeds and Cambridge, 1913

Laue's spots were hard to interpret. The puzzle was solved in England by a father and son working together: William Henry Bragg, a professor in Leeds, and his son William Lawrence Bragg, a 22-year-old student at Cambridge.

The son had the key insight while walking along the river in Cambridge. Think of the layers of atoms in a crystal as a stack of very thin mirrors. X-rays bouncing off one layer and off the next layer down travel slightly different distances. At most angles the bounced waves cancel out. But at certain special angles, the extra distance is exactly a whole number of wavelengths, the waves line up, and you get a strong reflection. Measure those angles, and you can calculate how far apart the layers are.

His father built a new instrument, the X-ray spectrometer, to measure the angles precisely. Together they turned it on crystals of ordinary salt, sodium chloride.

#### Salt has no molecules

What they found surprised many chemists. There were no NaCl molecules in salt at all. Instead, sodium and chlorine took turns, in every direction, in a vast cubic grid: every sodium surrounded by six chlorines, every chlorine surrounded by six sodiums. No sodium belonged to any one chlorine. The formula NaCl did not describe a molecule. It described a *ratio*: one sodium for every chlorine in the whole crystal.

:::fig c2_nacl_lattice() | The sodium chloride lattice the Braggs found: sodium and chloride ions alternating in every direction, each one surrounded by six of the other kind.

Not everyone liked this. Years later, in 1927, a senior English chemist, Henry Armstrong, complained that the idea that salt had no molecules went against common sense. But the X-rays did not care about common sense. They showed the same grid every time.

In 1915 father and son shared the Nobel Prize in Physics. Lawrence Bragg was 25, and he is still the youngest person ever to win a Nobel Prize in Physics. He heard the news while serving as a soldier in France during the First World War.

#### How X-ray crystallography works, step by step

1. **Grow a crystal**: a solid where the atoms or molecules repeat in a regular pattern, over and over, millions of times in every direction.
2. **Shine X-rays at it.** Their waves are about the same size as the spacing between atoms, a fraction of a nanometre.
3. **The rows of atoms scatter the X-rays** into a pattern of spots. Where the spots fall depends on how the atoms are spaced; how bright they are depends on what kind of atoms they are.
4. **Work backwards** from the pattern to the arrangement that must have made it. For salt this was a puzzle of a few hours. For a protein with thousands of atoms it can take years.

#### Women who read the spots

X-ray crystallography became one of the great tools of the twentieth century, and some of its greatest masters were women at a time when science was hard for women to enter.

Kathleen Lonsdale, who had trained with the elder Bragg in London, showed in 1929 that the ring of six carbon atoms in benzene compounds is flat, settling an argument that had run for sixty years. Dorothy Hodgkin worked out the structures of penicillin, of vitamin B₁₂, and, after 35 years of effort, of insulin, the hormone that people with diabetes need. She won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1964. And Rosalind Franklin's X-ray photographs of DNA, taken in London in 1952, were crucial evidence for its famous double-helix shape.

:::home Grow your own salt crystals
Ask an adult for a cup of warm tap water. Stir in salt a teaspoon at a time until no more will dissolve. Pour a little of the clear liquid (not the undissolved salt at the bottom) into a saucer and leave it somewhere still and dry for a few days. As the water evaporates, salt crystals grow. Look at them through a magnifying glass. The cubes you see are the giant, visible version of the Braggs' grid: their shape comes from the way the ions stack.
:::
