:::scene Woolsthorpe Manor, Lincolnshire, Christmas Day 1642

The baby was so small that, as he later said himself, he could have fitted into a quart pot. Nobody expected him to live. His father, a farmer who could not write his own name, had died three months before he was born. When the boy was three, his mother married again, a much older clergyman, and moved to the next village, leaving her son behind with his grandmother. He did not forgive her for years.

That boy was Isaac Newton, and he grew into one of the strangest and most brilliant people who ever lived. Most people know him for gravity and the falling apple. Very few know that he spent more time on alchemy than on gravity.

#### A lonely, clever boy

Young Isaac was not a friendly child. He built things instead: a working model of a windmill turned by a mouse running on a wheel, sundials carved into the walls of the house, kites carrying paper lanterns that frightened the neighbours at night. At school in Grantham he lodged with an apothecary, a man who mixed medicines, and it was probably there that he first saw jars of salts and powders and the art of turning one substance into another.

His mother tried to make him a farmer. It was hopeless. Sent to watch the sheep, he read books, and the sheep wandered into the neighbours' corn. In 1661 he went to Trinity College, Cambridge, at first as a poor student who paid his way partly by working as a servant to richer ones.

:::scene Woolsthorpe again, 1665 and 1666

Then the plague came. Cambridge closed, and Newton went home to the farm for nearly two years. Alone, in his early twenties, he worked out the beginnings of calculus, showed that white light is a mixture of all the colours, and began to think that the force pulling an apple down might be the same force holding the Moon in its orbit. (Whether an apple actually fell near him is a story he told in old age. It may be true; it is certainly a good story.)

#### The secret laboratory

Back in Cambridge, Newton became a professor at 26. And from around 1669, he started something he told almost nobody about. He bought furnaces, glassware, chemicals and old books. He built a small laboratory, with a furnace, beside his rooms at Trinity, and for nearly thirty years he worked there, sometimes for weeks on end with the fire burning day and night.

He was doing alchemy. The alchemists believed metals could be transformed: lead or mercury, purified and "ripened" in the right way, might become gold. Above all they hunted for the philosopher's stone, a substance said to turn base metals into gold. Newton copied out old alchemical texts by hand, compared them, and wrote his own notes in a secret language of symbols. He even signed some papers with a hidden name: *Jeova sanctus unus*, "Jehovah the holy one", which is a Latin anagram of *Isaacus Neuutonus*.

How much did he write? Historians who have studied his papers estimate about a million words on alchemy: more than he wrote on gravity and light. Almost none of it was published.

Why would the cleverest scientist in Europe spend so long on something that now sounds like magic? Because in the 1600s, nobody yet knew it was impossible. Chemistry and alchemy were not yet separate subjects. Newton was not looking for easy riches. He believed that metals might "grow" and change inside the Earth, and that hidden forces worked inside matter, just as gravity worked between planets. He wanted to find them. In that sense he was asking the right question, *what are things made of, and what holds them together?*, with the wrong tools.

#### The dark year

In 1693, Newton became seriously unwell. He could not sleep, he wrote wild, angry letters to friends accusing them of plotting against him, and then he wrote again to apologise. Nobody knows for certain why. He was exhausted, he had worked brutally hard for years, and he had been badly upset by personal troubles.

There is another possibility. Newton heated mercury and lead for hours in his laboratory, and sometimes tasted the substances he made, as chemists of his time often did. In 1979, scientists analysed strands of hair said to be his and reported very high levels of mercury. Some historians think mercury poisoning may have played a part in his illness; others point out that his symptoms do not fit neatly, and that we cannot be completely sure the hair was his. It remains an open question. What is not in doubt is that his laboratory was a dangerous place.

#### Why the gold never came

Here is the part you can understand better than Newton could.

Gold is an **element**. Every gold atom has exactly 79 protons in its nucleus; that number is what makes it gold. Mercury has 80, and lead has 82. A chemical reaction, however clever, only rearranges atoms and the electrons on their outside. It never touches the nucleus. Heat, acids, distillation, years of patient furnace work: none of it can take a proton out of a mercury atom. So no chemical recipe could ever turn mercury into gold. Newton was searching for a door that chemistry does not have.

That door does exist. But it took nuclear physics, more than two hundred years later, to open it, and the gold it makes is fantastically expensive. That story is told in the transmutation story, and it reaches all the way to the news of this year.

#### The Master of the Mint

In 1696 Newton left Cambridge for London to become Warden of the Royal Mint, where England's coins were made, and later its Master. Many people expected him to treat it as an easy job. Instead he threw himself into it: he oversaw the recoining of the nation's money, and he chased down forgers with the same fierce persistence he brought to everything else. There is a pleasing twist here. The man who had tried for decades to make gold ended up in charge of the country's real gold and silver.

He became President of the Royal Society, was knighted, and died in 1727 at the age of 84. He was buried in Westminster Abbey.

:::scene Sotheby's auction room, London, 1936

For two centuries his alchemy papers stayed mostly hidden, an embarrassment to people who wanted Newton to be a pure, modern scientist. In 1936 a huge collection of his papers was sold at auction. The economist John Maynard Keynes bought many of the alchemy papers and read them. Afterwards he wrote that Newton "was not the first of the age of reason. He was the last of the magicians."

Both halves of Newton are real. He was the man who gave us the laws of motion. And he was the last great alchemist, standing at the exact moment when the old magic was turning into modern science.

:::home Split sunlight into a rainbow
Newton's most famous experiment at home used a glass prism. You can do a version with a glass of water. On a sunny day, fill a clear, straight-sided glass to the brim and stand it on a windowsill in full sun, with a sheet of white paper on the floor below. Move the paper about until you catch a small band of colours. Newton showed that white light is all of those colours mixed, and that the glass bends each colour by a slightly different amount. No alchemy needed.
:::

When you meet Newton in the opening chapters of this book, remember both of him: the scientist who saw the hidden order in the heavens, and the alchemist who could not see that gold was beyond his reach.
