:::scene Raruli, Bengal, 1861

Prafulla Chandra Ray was born on 2 August 1861 in the village of Raruli-Katipara in Bengal (today in Bangladesh). His father was a landowner who loved books and had a large library. Prafulla read history, science and the lives of great people, and he decided early that he wanted to be useful to his country.

He studied in Calcutta, and in 1882 he won a scholarship to the University of Edinburgh in Scotland. He studied chemistry there for six years and earned a doctorate in 1887. Even then he was thinking about India. As a student in Edinburgh he wrote an essay criticising British rule in India, which was a bold thing to do.

#### A teacher on less pay

:::scene Presidency College, Calcutta, 1889

When he came home, Ray found it hard to get a job. Eventually, in 1889, he became a lecturer in chemistry at Presidency College in Calcutta. He was paid much less than British teachers doing the same work, simply because he was Indian. He objected, but he did the job anyway, and he did it brilliantly.

His students loved him. He taught chemistry with stories, jokes and demonstrations, and he expected his students to think for themselves. He was called **Acharya**, "teacher", a title of great respect. Among the people he taught or trained were some of India's greatest scientists, including the physicists Meghnad Saha and Satyendra Nath Bose (the "boson" is named after Bose).

#### A new compound

In 1896, working with mercury and nitric acid in his laboratory, Ray made a yellow crystalline compound that nobody had described before: **mercurous nitrite**. Chemists had thought such compounds would be too unstable to make. He went on to study many other nitrites, publishing paper after paper, and became so well known for them that the English chemist Henry Armstrong is said to have called him "the Master of Nitrites".

#### A company in his rooms

Ray noticed something that troubled him. India had plenty of raw materials, but almost every medicine and laboratory chemical in the country had to be bought from Europe. Indians were trained as clerks and officials, not as makers. He wanted India to make things for itself.

So, in 1892, with very little money (a few hundred rupees; accounts differ on the exact sum), he started making medicines and chemicals in his own rented house in Calcutta, working in the evenings. It was hard going, and for a while it nearly failed. But it grew, and in 1901 it became a proper company: **Bengal Chemical and Pharmaceutical Works**, one of the first modern chemical companies in India. It made medicines, laboratory chemicals and household products, and it still exists today.

#### The history of chemistry in India

Ray was also fascinated by the past. He studied ancient Sanskrit texts about metals, medicines and alchemy, and in 1902 he published the first volume of *A History of Hindu Chemistry*. It showed that people in ancient and medieval India had known a great deal of practical chemistry: how to extract and purify metals, make alloys, prepare medicines from minerals, and distil liquids. Some of these texts, like European alchemy, chased the dream of turning base metals into gold. Others recorded careful, real chemistry.

#### A simple life

Ray never married. He lived in a single room at the university's science college, wore plain clothes, and spent very little on himself. It is widely said that he gave most of his salary away, to the university, to students who could not afford their fees, and to good causes, though exact amounts are hard to pin down. When floods and famines struck Bengal, as they did in 1921 and 1922, he organised relief work, collecting money and supplies and sending volunteers, many of them students.

He kept teaching into his late seventies, and died in Calcutta on 16 June 1944.

Ray believed that science was not just something to admire in other countries, but something every country could do for itself, with its own people, in its own laboratories. When this book talks about acids, and the chemistry that turns raw materials into useful things, remember the Acharya who started a chemical company in his own rooms.
