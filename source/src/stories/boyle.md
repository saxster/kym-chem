:::scene Lismore Castle, Ireland, 1627

Robert Boyle was born into enormous wealth. His father, Richard Boyle, the first Earl of Cork, was one of the richest men in the British Isles, and Robert was the fourteenth of his fifteen children. He grew up in a castle above a river in the south of Ireland, and at the age of eight he was sent to Eton, the famous English school.

You might expect a boy like that to grow up to do nothing much. Instead, Boyle became one of the founders of modern science, and the man who did more than anyone to turn chemistry from a secret craft into an open, experimental subject.

#### A storm and a promise

At twelve, Boyle was sent on a long tour of Europe with a tutor. In Florence, in 1642, he read about Galileo, who had just died there, and he was fascinated by the new science that tested ideas against the world. Earlier, in Geneva, according to his own later account, a violent thunderstorm in the night terrified him so much that he vowed to live a deeply religious life. He kept that promise. All his life he was devout, and he saw science as a way of understanding God's creation.

When he came home, his father had died and Ireland and England were at war. Boyle inherited land and money, which meant he never needed a job. He spent his life, and his fortune, on experiments.

#### Doubt everything, test everything

In the 1600s, most people still learned about matter from very old books. Some followed Aristotle's four elements: earth, water, air and fire. Others followed the alchemists' three "principles": salt, sulfur and mercury. Both groups mostly argued from authority: *the great teacher said so, so it must be true.*

Boyle thought this was the wrong way round. In 1661 he published a book with a wonderful title: *The Sceptical Chymist*. It is written as a conversation between friends, and in it Boyle asks awkward questions. How do you know fire breaks things into their "elements"? When you burn wood, what comes out depends on how you burn it. Shouldn't an element be something you can actually find by experiment, rather than something an ancient author declared?

He did not discover the modern elements. But he insisted that chemistry must rest on experiments, done carefully, described exactly, and repeatable by anyone. He wrote down his methods in such detail that other people could check them, which was unusual at the time. This is the habit at the very heart of science.

#### The air pump

:::scene Oxford, 1659

Boyle hired a brilliant young assistant called Robert Hooke, a clergyman's son from the Isle of Wight, who was a superb builder of instruments. Together they built an improved air pump: a glass globe from which most of the air could be pumped out.

With it, they could do things no one had seen before. A ringing bell inside the globe grew fainter and fainter as the air was removed, showing that sound needs air to travel. A candle went out. A feather and a coin fell together. And Boyle measured how the pressure of trapped air changes when you squeeze it, finding that if you halve its volume, its pressure doubles. That rule is now called Boyle's law, and you will meet it in Chapter 3. (Hooke deserves much of the credit for the apparatus; Boyle was always generous in saying so.)

Some of the experiments are hard for us to read about. Like many experimenters of his time, Boyle put small animals in the globe to see what happened when the air was taken away. It helped show that animals need air, but it is not something scientists would do today.

#### A Royal Society

Boyle belonged to a group of curious people who met to discuss experiments, sometimes called the "Invisible College". In 1660 it became the Royal Society of London, one of the oldest scientific societies in the world. Its motto, *Nullius in verba*, roughly means "take nobody's word for it". That is Boyle's whole philosophy in three words.

#### The other Boyle: still an alchemist

Here is the surprise. The sceptical chymist was also a believer in alchemy.

Boyle was certain, like his younger friend Isaac Newton, that metals could in principle be transformed. He spent years on alchemical experiments, traded secret recipes, and in 1676 published a paper about a special kind of mercury that he thought might "heat up" when mixed with gold. He seems to have believed, near the end of his life, that he had once witnessed lead being turned into gold.

England had an old law, from 1404 in the reign of Henry IV, which made "multiplying" gold and silver by alchemy a crime, because the king feared fake gold would wreck the currency. Historians generally credit Boyle with helping to get that law repealed in 1689. He did not want alchemists punished for trying.

Was this hypocrisy? Not really. Boyle was a man of his time, standing on the line between two worlds. He doubted ancient authorities, but he could not yet know that gold is an element whose atoms no chemical process can change. That knowledge was still more than two hundred years away.

#### What became of him

Boyle never married. He lived for many years in London with his beloved older sister Katherine, Lady Ranelagh, who was herself a keen thinker, and he built a laboratory in her house. He wrote dozens of books on science and religion and died in 1691, just a week after her.

:::home Squeeze the air
Boyle's law at your desk. Take a plastic syringe from a medicine kit (no needle). Pull the plunger out halfway, then put your finger firmly over the tip. Push the plunger in. Feel how the air pushes back harder the more you squeeze it. Let go, and the plunger springs back. You have just felt what Boyle and Hooke measured: squash a gas into less space and its pressure rises.
:::
