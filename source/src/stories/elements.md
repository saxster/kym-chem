:::scene Sicily, about 450 BCE

Watch a log burn. Smoke rises into the air, sap hisses out as water, flames dance, and grey ash is left behind on the ground. To the Greek thinker Empedocles, living in Sicily about 2,500 years ago, this looked like a clue to everything. Perhaps the log had been made of four basic ingredients all along, earth, air, fire and water, and burning simply let them go.

That idea was taken up by Aristotle and lasted, in one form or another, for about two thousand years. It was wrong, but it was not stupid. It was an honest attempt to explain the endless variety of the world from a few simple things. That hope, that a few ingredients make everything, turned out to be right. Only the ingredients were wrong.

#### The alchemists' long experiment

For centuries, alchemists in the Islamic world, in India, in China and in Europe heated, dissolved, distilled and mixed every substance they could find. Many were hoping to turn lead into gold, which cannot be done by chemistry. But along the way they invented much of the equipment chemists still use, and they discovered new substances: strong acids, alcohol, new salts. They piled up an enormous amount of practical knowledge, without a clear idea of what an element really was.

#### Boyle asks a better question

:::scene Oxford, 1661

In 1661 the Irish-born scientist Robert Boyle published a book with a wonderful title, *The Sceptical Chymist*. "Sceptical" means doubting, and Boyle doubted the four elements. Show me, he said in effect, that you can actually split gold into earth, air, fire and water. Nobody could.

Boyle suggested that an element should be something real you can find by experiment: a substance that cannot be broken down into anything simpler. It was a new kind of definition, based on what you can *do* in a laboratory rather than on philosophy.

#### Lavoisier's list

:::scene Paris, 1789

A little over a century later, Antoine Lavoisier put Boyle's idea to work. In his great textbook of 1789 he listed 33 "simple substances", things that no one had managed to break down. Many are on the modern list: oxygen, nitrogen, hydrogen, sulfur, phosphorus, carbon, and metals such as iron, copper, zinc, gold and mercury.

But look closely and you find some surprises. Lavoisier's list included *light* and *caloric*, which he thought was a weightless fluid that carried heat. And it included lime, magnesia and silica, which are not elements at all. Lavoisier had simply not found a way to split them yet. He said so himself: they were simple *as far as we know*. That honesty is what made the list scientific: it could be corrected.

#### Davy and the dancing globules

:::scene London, October 1807

Correction came with a new tool: electricity. In 1800 Alessandro Volta had invented the battery, and soon chemists were passing electric currents through everything. At the Royal Institution in London, a young chemist named Humphry Davy built one of the most powerful batteries in the world and turned it on potash, a substance people had used for centuries to make soap and glass, and which Lavoisier had suspected might not be simple.

As the current flowed through slightly moist potash, tiny silvery beads appeared, some bursting into flame. Davy had found a new metal, potassium. His cousin Edmund, who was working with him, later told Davy's brother that Davy bounded about the room in delight. A few days later he did the same with soda and found sodium. In the next year came calcium, magnesium, strontium and barium. Some of the "simple" substances on Lavoisier's list were metals combined with oxygen, and electricity could pull them apart.

#### Element, compound or mixture: step by step

By now chemists had three clear words, the same ones you use in Chapter 2.

1. **An element** is a substance made of only one kind of atom: all the atoms have the same number of protons. Oxygen gas, gold and diamond are elements.
2. **A compound** is a substance made of two or more elements *chemically joined* in a fixed ratio. Water is always two hydrogens for every oxygen. A compound is a new substance with new properties: sodium is a metal that bursts into flame in water, chlorine is a poisonous green gas, but sodium chloride is the salt on your table.
3. **A mixture** is two or more substances simply mingled together, in any proportions, each keeping its own properties. Salt water, air and a fruit salad are mixtures. You can usually separate a mixture by physical means: filtering, evaporating, picking pieces out.

:::careful
> "Oxygen gas, O₂, is a compound because it is made of molecules."

Molecules can be elements. O₂ contains only oxygen atoms, so it is an element, even though the atoms come in pairs. A compound needs at least two *different* elements joined together.
:::

:::home Unmix a mixture
Mix a teaspoon of salt with a teaspoon of ground black pepper on a plate. Now separate them, without picking. Here is one way: rub an inflated balloon on your hair or a wool jumper and hold it a centimetre above the plate. The pepper, which is lighter, jumps up and sticks to the balloon; most of the salt stays behind. (Another way: stir the mixture into water. Salt dissolves, pepper floats. Pour off the water and leave it in a sunny place to dry, and the salt comes back.) You cannot do this with a compound. No balloon will pull the sodium out of salt.
:::

#### The list today

The number of known elements grew steadily: about 30 by 1800, 63 when Mendeleev made his table in 1869, 92 natural elements up to uranium by the 1940s, and then a new kind of discovery. Scientists began *making* elements that are not found in nature, by smashing nuclei together. The 118th, oganesson, was named in 2016. Some of the newest elements last less than a thousandth of a second before they fall apart.

Every one of them follows Boyle's rule. It cannot be broken down by chemistry into anything simpler, and every atom of it has the same number of protons.
