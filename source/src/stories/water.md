:::scene Prague, winter 1610

On a snowy day in Prague, the astronomer Johannes Kepler was crossing a bridge when a snowflake landed on his coat. He noticed, as people had before him, that it had six points. Every snowflake he looked at had six. Why six, and never five or eight? He wrote a little book about it as a New Year's present for a friend, published in 1611, and guessed that it had something to do with how tiny particles pack together. He could not go further. Nobody knew what water was made of.

It took three more centuries to understand Kepler's snowflakes. The answer turned out to explain far more than snow: why ice floats, why lakes freeze from the top down, why water boils at such a high temperature, and why you sweat on a hot Mumbai afternoon.

#### Step 1: water is an odd liquid

By most of chemistry's rules, water should be a gas at room temperature. Look at its relatives. Hydrogen sulfide, H₂S, has molecules shaped just like water's but heavier, and it boils at about −60 °C. Methane, CH₄, with almost the same mass as water, boils at −161 °C. Yet water, with a light little molecule of three atoms, does not boil until 100 °C. Something is holding water molecules to each other unusually tightly.

And then there is ice. Almost every substance shrinks when it freezes: its particles pack more neatly as a solid than as a liquid, so the solid sinks in its own liquid. Water does the opposite. Ice takes up about 9% more space than the water it came from. That is why ice floats, why a full bottle of water in the freezer cracks, and why pipes burst in cold countries.

:::scene Berkeley, California, 1920

The explanation came from the laboratory of the great chemist G. N. Lewis, the man who first drew bonds as shared pairs of electrons. In 1920 two young chemists working there, Wendell Latimer and Worth Rodebush, suggested that a hydrogen atom could be held between two oxygen atoms at once: firmly bonded to one, and weakly attracted to the other. This weak link between molecules came to be called the ==hydrogen bond==. In the 1930s Linus Pauling made the idea famous and showed how much of chemistry and biology depends on it.

#### Step 2: where the hydrogen bond comes from

In a water molecule, the oxygen atom pulls the shared electrons towards itself harder than the hydrogen atoms do. That leaves the oxygen slightly negative and each hydrogen slightly positive. The molecule is also bent, like a boomerang, so the two slightly positive hydrogens are on one side and the slightly negative oxygen on the other.

Now put lots of water molecules together. The slightly positive hydrogen of one molecule is attracted to the slightly negative oxygen of the next. Opposite charges attract: that is all a hydrogen bond is. It is much weaker than the real bonds inside a water molecule, about twenty times weaker, but every water molecule can make several of them, and together they hold the liquid tightly.

:::fig c3_boiling_points() | Three molecules with ten electrons each and almost the same mass, but very different boiling points. Water's hydrogen bonds (dotted) hold its molecules together so strongly that it boils at 100 °C.

That is why water boils so late. To turn water into steam you have to pull every molecule away from its neighbours, breaking all those hydrogen bonds. The O–H bonds *inside* each molecule do not break at all: steam is still H₂O.

#### Step 3: why ice floats

When water freezes, each molecule settles into a position where it makes four hydrogen bonds with four neighbours, arranged in a very regular, open pattern of six-sided rings. The rings hold the molecules slightly further apart than they are in the jostling liquid. More space between molecules means the same mass fills a bigger volume, so ice is less dense than water, and it floats. Those six-sided rings are also the reason every snowflake has six points: Kepler's puzzle, solved.

:::work
**How much of an ice cube is underwater?**
Ice has a density of about 0.917 grams per millilitre; liquid water about 1.000.
A floating object sinks until it pushes aside its own weight of water.
So the fraction underwater is 0.917 ÷ 1.000 ≈ 0.92: about nine-tenths of an ice cube, or an iceberg, is below the surface.
:::

This one strange property matters enormously. When a lake freezes in winter, the ice forms on top and floats there, like a lid, keeping the water underneath liquid. Fish and plants survive the winter below. If ice sank, lakes would freeze solid from the bottom up.

#### Step 4: why sweat cools you

Breaking hydrogen bonds takes energy. When sweat evaporates from your skin, the water molecules that escape carry that energy away with them, taking it from your body, and you cool down. Evaporating about a litre of sweat takes roughly 2.4 million joules: as much energy as it takes to warm 7 litres of water from room temperature to boiling. On a humid monsoon day the air is already so full of water vapour that sweat evaporates slowly, which is why humid heat feels so much stickier and harder to bear than dry heat.

:::careful
> "Boiling breaks water molecules apart into hydrogen and oxygen."

Boiling only breaks the weak hydrogen bonds *between* molecules. The molecules themselves stay whole. Splitting water into hydrogen and oxygen is a completely different, chemical change, which needs electricity or a great deal of heat.
:::

:::home Ice that grows
Fill a small plastic cup (not glass) exactly to a line you draw with a marker, about two-thirds full. Put it in the freezer overnight. In the morning the ice will stand clearly above your line, often with a little bump in the middle. Water expanded as it froze: the hydrogen bonds pushed the molecules into their open six-sided pattern.
:::

:::home A floating paper clip
Fill a bowl with water. Lay a small square of tissue paper flat on the surface and gently place a dry metal paper clip on it. As the tissue soaks and sinks, the paper clip is often left floating, resting on the skin of the water. Hydrogen bonds pulling the surface molecules together make that skin. Add a drop of washing-up liquid at the edge and watch what happens.
:::

So water, the most ordinary substance in your life, is one of the strangest in all of chemistry. Its weak little hydrogen bonds decide whether lakes freeze, whether you can cool down on a hot day, and why every snowflake that ever fell has had six points.
