:::scene Eaglesfield, Cumberland, 1766

John Dalton was born in a small thatched cottage in a village in the north of England. His father was a handloom weaver, and the family were Quakers, a Christian group who dressed plainly, refused to swear oaths, and believed in education and simple living. They were poor.

Dalton was a quiet, serious boy who loved numbers. A wealthy Quaker neighbour taught him mathematics and let him read his books. And then, at the age of twelve, Dalton did something that seems astonishing today: he opened his own school and began teaching other children, some of them older than he was. It did not last long, but teaching became his life's work. For the rest of his life, even when he was famous, he earned his living by teaching.

#### The weather diary

At fifteen he moved to Kendal to teach at a Quaker school with his brother. There he met John Gough, a blind philosopher who taught him science, and Dalton began a habit that he kept until the day before he died: he wrote down the weather. Every day, for 57 years, he recorded the temperature, the air pressure, the rain and the wind. Historians estimate the diary holds around 200,000 observations.

All that watching of the air led him to wonder what air actually is. Why do oxygen and nitrogen and water vapour stay mixed instead of settling into layers? Why does water vapour behave as if the other gases were not there? These questions about gases led him, slowly, to atoms.

#### Seeing colours differently

:::scene Manchester, 1794

In 1793 Dalton moved to Manchester, a booming city of cotton mills, to teach at a new college. In his first paper to the city's scientific society, he described something odd about his own eyes.

He had noticed it when he bought his mother a pair of stockings he thought were a sober dark blue, suitable for a Quaker woman. She was startled: they were bright scarlet. (This story is told in several versions, and may have been improved in the retelling.) Dalton tested himself and his brother and found that they both saw colours differently from most people. Pink geranium flowers looked sky-blue to him by day.

He wrote one of the first scientific descriptions of colour blindness. For a long time, the condition was even called "Daltonism". Dalton guessed that the liquid inside his eyes must be tinted blue, and asked that his eyes be examined after his death. They were, and the liquid was perfectly clear: his guess was wrong. But one eye was kept in a jar. In 1995, scientists extracted DNA from it and found that he lacked the gene for one of the three colour-sensing cells in the eye. Two hundred years after he described himself, his own eye told the rest of the story.

#### Atoms, with weights

Dalton's great idea came together between about 1803 and 1808. Philosophers had talked about atoms since ancient Greece, but only as an idea. Dalton made atoms into something chemists could *use*.

His idea, in plain words:

1. Every element is made of tiny atoms.
2. All the atoms of one element are identical, and they are different from the atoms of every other element. Above all, they have different **weights**.
3. Atoms cannot be created, destroyed or split in a chemical reaction. They are only rearranged.
4. Compounds form when atoms of different elements join in small whole numbers: one and one, one and two, two and three.

Point 4 explained something chemists had noticed but could not understand: why substances always combine in fixed proportions. And point 2 meant you could compare atoms by weighing. In 1803 Dalton drew up the first table of relative atomic weights, with hydrogen as 1. Many of his numbers were wrong, because he guessed the wrong formulas (he thought water was HO, one hydrogen and one oxygen), but his method was right, and later chemists corrected the numbers.

He drew each atom as a little circle with a pattern inside: a dot for hydrogen, a plain ring for oxygen. Those symbols were soon replaced by letters, but the idea behind them lasted.

#### A modest celebrity

Dalton became famous across Europe. He was elected to scientific academies, given a royal medal and a government pension. When he was presented to King William IV, there was a problem: Quakers did not wear the colourful court dress the occasion required. Dalton wore the scarlet robe of an Oxford doctor instead. Since he was colour-blind, it is said, it looked a respectable grey to him.

He never married and lived simply, lodging for decades with a friend's family, playing bowls every Thursday, and teaching right to the end. When he died in Manchester in 1844, the city gave him a huge public funeral, and tens of thousands of people filed past his coffin. A shy weaver's son had become the man who made atoms real.

:::home Test colour vision the gentle way
Dalton was colour-blind and never knew it until he was in his twenties. You can explore how colours are seen. Collect objects that are red, green, brown and orange, and ask family members to sort them by colour without saying the names out loud. Then try under a dim lamp, where everyone's colour vision gets worse. About 1 in 12 boys and men, and far fewer girls and women, see some reds and greens differently: if anyone in your family sorts them differently, they are in good company with Dalton.
:::
