:::scene The University of Manchester, 1909

Ernest Rutherford grew up on a farm in New Zealand, one of twelve children. By 1909 he was a famous physicist in Manchester, and he already had a Nobel Prize for his work on radioactivity. His favourite tool was the **alpha particle**: a tiny, fast, positively charged bullet shot out by radioactive substances such as radium. (We now know an alpha particle is the nucleus of a helium atom.) Rutherford liked to fire alpha particles at things and see what happened.

His assistant, Hans Geiger, had a promising young student, Ernest Marsden, who was twenty years old and needed a project. Rutherford suggested one, more or less to keep him busy: fire alpha particles at a thin sheet of gold foil, and see whether any of them bounce off at a big angle.

Nobody expected him to find any. According to the best idea of the time, Thomson's "plum pudding" atom, the positive charge in an atom was spread out thinly, like dough. A fast alpha particle should punch straight through, perhaps wobbling slightly. It was like firing bullets at a cloud of mist.

#### Step 1: counting flashes in the dark

There were no electronic detectors yet. To see an alpha particle, you let it hit a small screen coated with zinc sulfide. Where it hits, there is a tiny flash of light, a *scintillation*, too faint to see in daylight. So Geiger and Marsden sat in a completely dark room for half an hour to let their eyes adjust, then peered through a microscope at the screen and counted flashes, one by one, for minutes at a time. It was slow, tiring work, and you could only do it accurately for a short time before your eyes tired.

Most of the flashes came where they expected: almost straight through the foil. But then Marsden moved the screen round to the *same side* as the source, and kept seeing flashes. Some alpha particles were coming back.

#### Step 2: "as if you fired a shell at tissue paper"

Only a very few bounced back: for thin gold foil, roughly one in many thousands. But that they bounced back at all was shocking. Rutherford said later that it was the most incredible thing that had ever happened to him, "almost as incredible as if you fired a fifteen-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you."

A heavy, fast alpha particle can only be turned right round by something even heavier and very strongly charged. A thinly spread cloud of charge could never do it. So the positive charge, and most of the mass, must be packed into something tiny.

#### Step 3: Rutherford's new atom

Rutherford spent nearly two years working out the mathematics. If all the positive charge sat in a minute point at the centre of each atom, he calculated how many alpha particles should be turned through each angle. Geiger and Marsden then checked his predictions with more painstaking counting, publishing their results in 1913. They matched.

In 1911 Rutherford published his new picture of the atom:

1. Almost all the mass of an atom, and all its positive charge, are concentrated in a tiny centre. By 1912 he was calling it the **nucleus**, from the Latin word for a small nut or kernel.
2. The electrons are spread out around it, far away.
3. Between them is mostly empty space.

How tiny is tiny? A gold atom is about 0.29 nanometres across. Its nucleus is about 20,000 times smaller across. If a gold atom were the size of a cricket ground, its nucleus would be about the size of a small pea on the centre of the pitch, yet it would hold more than 99.9% of the atom's mass.

#### Step 4: why most particles go straight through

Now the result makes sense. An alpha particle flying through the foil passes through thousands of atoms, but each atom is almost all empty space with a tiny nucleus in the middle. Most of the time the alpha particle never comes anywhere near a nucleus, so it sails straight through. Only on the rare occasions when it heads almost straight at one does the enormous push between two positive charges turn it sharply, or send it back.

:::careful
> "If atoms are mostly empty space, why can't I push my hand through the table?"

Because "empty" here means "empty of nuclei", not "empty of everything". The space is filled by the atoms' electrons, and when the electrons of your hand come close to the electrons of the table, they push each other apart very strongly. An alpha particle is a tiny, fast nucleus; your hand is a crowd of atoms with their own electrons on the outside.
:::

:::home Find the hidden nail
This is a model of Rutherford's idea, not of the real sizes. Prop a large tray or a sheet of stiff cardboard on four books, so marbles can roll underneath. Ask someone to hide a few heavy objects (a mug, a small jar) under it, without telling you where. Roll marbles under the tray from one side, many times, along different lines, and watch where they come out. Most roll straight through. A few hit something and bounce off at an angle. From the bounces alone, try to work out where the hidden objects are. You have just done, in a small way, what Geiger and Marsden did with gold.
:::

The nucleus explained the mass of atoms. It also raised a new puzzle: if the electrons are spread out around a positive nucleus, why don't they fall straight into it? Answering that took a young Danish physicist, Niels Bohr, and a whole new kind of physics.
