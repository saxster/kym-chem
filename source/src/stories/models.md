:::scene A Mumbai local train, the morning rush

Look at the route map above the doors of a Mumbai local train. The stations are evenly spaced dots on straight coloured lines. The real tracks curve, the real stations are close together in some places and far apart in others, and the real city is full of buildings, roads and the sea, none of which appear on the map at all. The map is "wrong" about almost everything.

And yet it is exactly what you need. It tells you which stations come in which order, where to change trains, and how many stops are left. A map that showed every building and every curve would be much harder to use. The route map works *because* it leaves things out.

That is what scientists mean by a ==model==. A model is a simplified picture, drawing, set of rules or piece of mathematics that captures the part of the truth you need for a particular job, and leaves out the rest on purpose.

#### Step 1: every model has a job

A globe is a model of the Earth. It gets the shapes of the continents right, but it is far too smooth: on a globe the size of a football, Mount Everest would be a bump about as high as one or two sheets of paper are thick. A model car shows the shape of a car but not how the engine works. Neither is a failure. Each one is good at its own job.

The pictures of atoms in this book are the same. The counting diagrams in Chapter 1 are good at one job: counting protons, neutrons and electrons. They are useless for size (the real nucleus would be invisibly small on the page) and wrong about where electrons are. That is fine, as long as you know which job a picture is for.

#### Step 2: models improve when evidence surprises them

The history of the atom is a parade of models, each one replaced when an experiment showed something it could not explain.

:::fig s_model_ladder() | Five models of the atom. Each was the best explanation of the evidence at the time, and each was improved when a new experiment surprised it.

John Dalton pictured atoms as solid, uncuttable balls. That explained why chemicals combine in fixed proportions. Then J. J. Thomson found electrons coming out of atoms, so atoms could not be solid and uncuttable: he imagined a positive ball with electrons dotted through it. Then Rutherford's gold-foil experiment showed a tiny, heavy centre, so the model became a nucleus with electrons around it. Niels Bohr added energy levels to explain the colours of light that atoms give out. Today we describe electrons as clouds of likelihood, worked out with quantum physics.

Notice that the old models were not thrown away in shame. Dalton's solid balls are still perfectly good for balancing chemical equations. Each model is the right tool for some jobs.

#### Step 3: how to read any model

Whenever you meet a new diagram or rule in this book, ask three questions:

1. What job is this model doing? (Counting? Showing shape? Predicting a number?)
2. What does it get right, and how do we know?
3. Where does it stop being trustworthy?

:::careful
> "If scientists keep changing their models, they must not really know anything."

Changing a model is how science gets more right, not less. Each new model explains everything the old one did, plus something new. A map gets better when someone surveys a road that was missing; that does not make the old roads disappear.
:::

The British statistician George Box summed it up in a famous line: "All models are wrong, but some are useful." The skill is knowing which ones are useful, for what, and why.

:::home Make a model, then break it
Make a "model" of a water molecule from a large orange and two small grapes held on with toothpicks, bent at an angle. Write down three things your model shows truthfully (for example: one oxygen, two hydrogens, a bent shape) and three things it gets wrong (sizes, colours, the fact that real atoms are not solid fruit). Then eat the model.
:::

Chemistry is built from models like these. The trick, all the way through this book, is to use each one boldly for its own job, and to remember that it is a map, not the city.
