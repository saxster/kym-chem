:::scene Big Sur, California, 1955

Charles David Keeling was in his late twenties, and he was camping. Not for a holiday: he was collecting air. Day and night, in the forests and mountains of California, he filled glass flasks with air and carried them back to his laboratory, where he measured, with an instrument he had built himself, how much carbon dioxide each flask contained.

Other people had measured carbon dioxide before, and their numbers were all over the place. Keeling was more careful than anyone. He found that if you measured clean air, away from cities and forests breathing on it, the amount was almost the same everywhere he went: about 310 parts in every million. That steadiness was the first surprise. It meant the whole atmosphere was well mixed, and that a careful person in the right place could measure the whole planet at once.

#### Step 1: the volcano with the cleanest air

In 1958 Keeling, now working at the Scripps Institution of Oceanography with the ocean scientist Roger Revelle, set up an instrument near the top of ==Mauna Loa==, a huge volcano in Hawaii. At 3,400 metres up, in the middle of the Pacific Ocean, the air has travelled thousands of kilometres from the nearest city. It is about as "average" as air gets.

The first measurements, in March 1958, were about 313 parts per million. Keeling expected the number to stay put. Instead, within a couple of years, he saw two things that nobody had seen before.

#### Step 2: the Earth breathes

The first discovery was beautiful. Every year, the amount of carbon dioxide fell from late spring to autumn, and rose again through winter, by about 6 parts per million. Why? The northern half of the Earth has far more land, and far more forests and fields, than the southern half. In the northern summer, billions of leaves are growing, and plants take carbon dioxide out of the air to build themselves. In winter, leaves fall and rot, and the carbon dioxide comes back. Keeling was watching the whole planet breathe in and out, once a year.

#### Step 3: the line keeps rising

The second discovery was more worrying. Underneath the yearly breathing, the line kept climbing, year after year, without ever coming back down. It is now called the ==Keeling curve==, and the measurements have continued without a break ever since. After Charles Keeling died in 2005, his son Ralph carried on the work.

:::fig s_keeling_curve() | The Keeling curve: yearly average carbon dioxide at Mauna Loa (NOAA measurements), from about 316 parts per million in 1959 to about 421 in 2023. The yearly up-and-down wiggle is drawn smoothly here; in the real record it is about 6 parts per million.

The rise comes from burning coal, oil and gas. Every fuel made of carbon compounds turns its carbon into carbon dioxide when it burns, and humans now burn enormous amounts. Before the industrial age, air bubbles trapped in ancient ice show that the level was about 280 parts per million. It has now passed 420: half as much again.

#### Step 4: what "parts per million" means, step by step

The number looks small, so let us hold it in our hands.

:::work
**420 parts per million**
Out of every million molecules in the air, about 420 are carbon dioxide.
As a percentage: 420 ÷ 1,000,000 × 100 = 0.042%.
One breath of air (about half a litre) contains roughly 1.2 × 10²² molecules.
The carbon dioxide in it: 1.2 × 10²² × 420 ÷ 1,000,000 ≈ 5 × 10¹⁸ molecules.
:::

So carbon dioxide is a tiny fraction of the air, and yet there are still about five million million million molecules of it in every breath you take. A small fraction of a huge number is still a huge number. And small fractions can matter enormously: carbon dioxide traps heat radiating from the Earth's surface, so even a small change in how much there is changes the climate.

#### Step 5: the ocean as a sponge

Not all of the carbon dioxide we release stays in the air. Roughly a quarter to a third of it has dissolved into the oceans. That has slowed the rise in the air, but it has a cost, because carbon dioxide dissolving in water makes a weak acid, carbonic acid.

:::figwide c11_carbonate() | What happens to carbon dioxide in seawater. Most of the hydrogen ions it releases are mopped up by carbonate ions, so the pH falls only a little, but the carbonate that shell-building animals need gets used up.

This is called ==ocean acidification==. The sea is not acidic: its pH is still above 7, so it is slightly basic. But it is becoming less basic. The average surface pH has fallen from about 8.2 to about 8.1. Because each step on the pH scale is a factor of ten, that small-looking drop means about 26% more hydrogen ions (10 to the power 0.1 is about 1.26). Corals, oysters and tiny sea snails called pteropods build their shells from calcium carbonate, and they need carbonate ions to do it. As carbonate gets scarcer, building shells gets harder. Since 1988 scientists at a station called ALOHA, in the ocean north of Hawaii, have measured the seawater regularly, and they have watched its pH slowly fall, just as Keeling watched the air.

:::careful
> "If the oceans are absorbing carbon dioxide, that solves the problem."

It slows the rise in the air, but it moves the problem into the sea rather than removing it, and the ocean cannot keep absorbing at the same rate forever. Chapter 11 follows this chemistry step by step.
:::

:::home Make carbon dioxide change a colour
Ask an adult to help you chop a few leaves of red cabbage and soak them in warm tap water for half an hour, then strain the purple water into a clear glass. This is a natural pH indicator: it turns pinker when something acidic is added and greener or bluer when something basic is added. Put a straw in the glass and blow gently through it (blow out only, never suck, and do not drink it) for a few minutes. The carbon dioxide in your breath dissolves and makes a little carbonic acid, and the colour shifts slightly towards pink. Compare it with a glass of the same juice you did not blow into. That is ocean acidification in a glass, made with your own breath.
:::

Keeling's lesson for every chemist is about patience and care. He did not discover anything by being clever in a single afternoon. He discovered it by measuring the same thing, very carefully, in the same place, every day for decades, and refusing to let the numbers be sloppy. Some of the most important facts about our planet were found exactly that way.
