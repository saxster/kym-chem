:::scene Bristol, England, 1898

A famous scientist, Sir William Crookes, stands up to give the most important speech of the year to Britain's scientists, and he gives them a fright. The world's population is growing fast, he says, and so is its hunger for wheat. Wheat needs nitrogen, and the soil runs out of it. The farms of Europe and America are being kept going with nitrogen dug out of the ground on the other side of the world: piles of seabird droppings, called guano, from islands off Peru, and a salty mineral, Chile saltpetre, from the Atacama desert. Those supplies, Crookes warns, will run out within a few decades. Unless chemists find another source, the world faces hunger.

Then he points out something maddening. Every breath you take is about four-fifths nitrogen. There is unimaginably more of it in the air than any farm could ever need. The problem is that plants cannot use it.

#### Why air nitrogen is so hard to use

Nitrogen in the air is made of N₂ molecules: two nitrogen atoms joined by a triple bond, one of the strongest bonds in chemistry. Plants (and you) need nitrogen to build proteins and DNA, but only in forms where that triple bond has already been broken, such as ==ammonia==, NH₃, or the nitrates made from it. A few bacteria, and lightning, can break the bond. Humans could not, at least not cheaply.

#### The table-top in Karlsruhe

:::scene Karlsruhe, Germany, 1909

The German chemist Fritz ==Haber== decided to try the most direct route of all: join nitrogen straight onto hydrogen.

:::eq
N₂ + 3 H₂ ⇌ 2 NH₃
:::

That double arrow is the whole difficulty. The reaction is reversible: ammonia breaks back down into nitrogen and hydrogen as well as forming. Haber used everything the equilibrium story teaches. The forward reaction turns four gas molecules into two, so squeezing the gases hard pushes the balance towards ammonia: Haber worked at about 200 times normal air pressure. The forward reaction also gives out heat, so a cooler mixture gives more ammonia at equilibrium; but a cooler mixture reacts too slowly to be useful. So he compromised on a few hundred degrees, and added a ==catalyst== (the rare metal osmium) to make the reaction fast enough. He also kept removing the ammonia as it formed, so the mixture kept making more.

With his assistant Robert Le Rossignol, a young Englishman who was brilliant at building high-pressure equipment, Haber built a machine about the size of a kitchen table. In July 1909 it produced a slow, steady trickle of liquid ammonia from air and hydrogen, and it kept on running.

#### From table-top to factory

A table-top machine is not a factory. The chemical company BASF gave the job to an engineer, Carl ==Bosch==, who had to solve problems nobody had faced before. Hot hydrogen at enormous pressure soaked into steel and made it brittle, so the first reactors cracked; Bosch designed a steel tube with a soft-iron lining to protect it. Osmium was far too rare, so his colleague Alwin Mittasch tested thousands of possible catalysts, in many thousands of experiments, and settled on a cheap one based on iron. In 1913 the first factory opened at Oppau. The Haber–Bosch process, as it is called, had begun.

#### Step 1: why it matters so much

Today the world makes well over a hundred million tonnes of ammonia every year, most of it turned into ==fertiliser==. In India, much of it becomes urea, the white granules scattered on fields of rice and wheat. Researchers estimate that about half the people alive today are fed by crops grown with fertiliser from this one reaction, and that roughly half the nitrogen atoms in your own body first passed through a Haber–Bosch factory. Chemistry, quite literally, made bread from air.

#### Step 2: the chemistry, step by step

:::work
1. Nitrogen comes from the air; hydrogen today comes mostly from natural gas.
2. The gases are squeezed to a couple of hundred times atmospheric pressure, because four molecules (N₂ + 3 H₂) become two (2 NH₃): squeezing favours fewer molecules.
3. They are heated to about 400 to 450 °C: hot enough to be fast, not so hot that the balance swings back to nitrogen and hydrogen.
4. An iron catalyst lowers the energy hill so the reaction is quick.
5. The ammonia is cooled until it turns liquid and is taken away; the leftover gases go round again.
:::

#### The other side of the story

Fritz Haber's story does not end happily, and it would not be honest to pretend it does. When the First World War began in 1914, Haber put his skills to work for Germany's army. He led the development of poison gas, and on 22 April 1915, near the town of Ypres in Belgium, chlorine gas was released against enemy soldiers for the first time. Thousands were killed or injured. Haber's wife, Clara Immerwahr, was herself a talented chemist, the first woman to earn a doctorate at the University of Breslau, and she is said to have opposed his work on gas. She died just days after that first attack, a tragedy historians still write about.

Haber received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for making ammonia, and many scientists protested. In 1933, after the Nazis came to power and ordered him to dismiss his Jewish staff, he resigned from his own institute; he had been born Jewish himself. He left Germany and died the following year in Switzerland.

Haber's life shows something worth remembering. Chemistry is knowledge, and knowledge can be used to feed people or to hurt them. The same man did both. What science is used for is always a choice made by people, and that is why scientists, and everyone else, have to care about how it is used.

:::careful
> "The Haber process turns all the nitrogen and hydrogen into ammonia."

It cannot: the reaction is an equilibrium, so at any moment some ammonia is breaking down again. In one pass through the reactor only a modest fraction becomes ammonia. That is why the factory takes the ammonia out and sends the unreacted gases round again and again.
:::

:::home Nitrogen you can see
Plants show you when they are short of nitrogen: their older leaves turn pale yellow. If you have two pots of the same small plant (or can sprout two pots of mung beans), water one with plain water and the other with water containing a tiny pinch of houseplant fertiliser (follow the packet, ask an adult, and wash your hands afterwards). Over two or three weeks, compare the colour and growth. The nitrogen in that fertiliser was almost certainly pulled out of the air by the reaction in this story.
:::

The next time you eat a chapati, think of the triple bond in N₂, Haber's table-top, and Bosch's giant reactors: some of the nitrogen in that wheat was air not so long ago.
