:::scene Clapham Common, London, 1773

On a windy day the American inventor Benjamin Franklin, who was living in London, carried a little cruet of oil down to a pond on Clapham Common. The wind was whipping up small waves. He poured out no more than a teaspoon of oil onto the water, and watched something remarkable happen. The oil spread, and spread, and kept spreading, until it covered about half an acre of the pond: roughly the area of eight tennis courts. Where it had spread, the waves went flat, "as smooth as a looking glass", he wrote.

Franklin did not do the sum that was hiding in his experiment. More than a hundred years later, the English physicist Lord Rayleigh did it, with his own careful measurements of oil on water. It is the first clue in our story.

#### Step 1: how thin can oil get?

If a teaspoon of oil spreads into a film, the film's thickness is just its volume divided by its area. Let us do it with Franklin's numbers.

:::work
A teaspoon holds about 5 millilitres = 0.000 005 cubic metres (5 × 10⁻⁶ m³).
Half an acre is about 2,000 square metres.
Thickness = volume ÷ area = 5 × 10⁻⁶ ÷ 2,000 ≈ 2.5 × 10⁻⁹ m, about 2.5 nanometres.
:::

The oil cannot spread any thinner than a single layer of its molecules. So an oil molecule can be no more than about 2.5 nanometres long: two and a half millionths of a millimetre. For the first time, a kitchen-sized experiment had put a ceiling on the size of a molecule.

#### Step 2: from size to number

If you know roughly how big a molecule is, you can estimate how many there are in a given amount. In 1865 the Austrian scientist Josef Loschmidt did exactly that for the molecules in air, using measurements of how fast gases spread and how much they shrink when turned into liquid. His answer for the number in a mole was rough, but it was in the right neighbourhood. For the first time, somebody had put an actual number on the invisible.

#### Step 3: many roads, one answer

The real proof came early in the twentieth century, when scientists found completely different ways of measuring the same number, and they all agreed.

- **Jiggling grains (around 1908).** Jean Perrin in Paris watched tiny grains of resin bobbing about in water, shoved this way and that by invisible water molecules. From how far they wandered, using a formula that Albert Einstein had worked out in 1905, he could work out how many molecules there must be in a mole. He got roughly 6 to 7 × 10²³.
- **Charge (1909 onwards).** The American physicist Robert Millikan measured the electric charge on a single electron by watching tiny oil droplets float between charged metal plates. Chemists already knew how much charge it takes to deposit one mole of silver in electrolysis: about 96,485 units, called coulombs. Divide the charge for a mole by the charge for one electron, and you get the number of electrons in a mole:

:::work
96,485 ÷ 0.000 000 000 000 000 000 160 2 (1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ coulombs per electron)
= 6.022 × 10²³
:::

- **Crystals (from the 1910s).** X-rays could measure the exact spacing between atoms in a crystal. Knowing the spacing, you can count how many atoms fit in a given lump.

Perrin listed more than a dozen independent methods, and they all landed near the same value. That, more than anything, convinced the last doubters that atoms were real. Perrin proposed calling the number after Amedeo Avogadro, and in 1926 he won the Nobel Prize for this work.

#### Step 4: the most perfect sphere in the world

In the 2000s, scientists from many countries joined a project to measure the number better than ever before. They made spheres of almost pure silicon-28, each with a mass of about 1 kilogram and a diameter of about 9.4 centimetres, polished so perfectly that if one were the size of the Earth, its highest mountain would be only a few metres tall. They measured each sphere's size with lasers and the spacing of its atoms with X-rays, then simply counted: about 2 × 10²⁵ atoms in each sphere.

Their measurements were so good that in 2019 the world agreed to stop measuring and *fix* the number exactly:

:::eq
**Avogadro's constant = 6.022 140 76 × 10²³ per mole, exactly**
:::

#### Step 5: feeling the size of it

Here is the honest truth: nobody can picture 6 × 10²³. But you can compare it with other huge things, and feel your way.

:::work
**A glass of water and the oceans.** A 250 mL glass of water holds about 8 × 10²⁴ water molecules. All the oceans on Earth together hold about 5 × 10²¹ glassfuls of water.
So one glass of water contains more than 1,500 times as many molecules as there are glasses of water in all the oceans.
:::

:::work
**Marbles.** If you had a mole of marbles, each 1 centimetre across, and spread them evenly over the whole surface of the Earth, land and sea, they would make a layer nearly a kilometre deep.
:::

:::work
**Sand.** One careful estimate puts the number of grains of sand on all of Earth's beaches at around 7 or 8 × 10¹⁸. A mole is about 80,000 times more than that.
:::

And yet a mole of water is about 18 grams: one gulp. Your gulp holds more molecules than there are sand grains on all the world's beaches, many thousands of times over. That tells you, more vividly than any number, how tiny a molecule is.

:::careful
> "Avogadro's number is just a very big number that someone chose."

It is two things at once. Since 2019 its exact value is fixed by agreement, like the number of centimetres in a metre. But that value was not picked out of the air: it was chosen to match more than a century of measurements, so that a mole of carbon-12 atoms still has a mass of 12 grams (to within a tiny fraction of a millionth). The [mole](#story-mole) story shows why that matching matters.
:::

:::home Measure a molecule-thin layer, almost
Ask an adult to help. Fill a large, clean baking tray with water and sprinkle a very light dusting of ground black pepper over the surface. Dip the tip of a toothpick in a drop of washing-up liquid or cooking oil and touch it to the centre. Watch how far the pepper races away from such a tiny amount. Thin films spread enormously, which is exactly what let Franklin, and later Rayleigh, use them to measure molecules.
:::

So when this book writes 6.022 × 10²³, remember a teaspoon of oil on a windy pond, a dozen different experiments all agreeing, and a silicon sphere polished almost perfectly round. That is how we know.
