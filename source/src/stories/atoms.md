:::scene Abdera, a Greek town on the coast of Thrace, about 450 BCE

Imagine you have a piece of cheese and a very sharp knife. Cut it in half. Cut one half in half again. Keep going. The pieces get smaller and smaller, until they are too small to see. Can you keep going for ever, or is there a smallest piece of cheese that cannot be cut?

About two and a half thousand years ago, a Greek thinker called Leucippus and his student Democritus gave an answer. You cannot cut for ever, they said. In the end you reach tiny pieces that are solid all the way through and cannot be divided. They called them *atomos*, which means "uncuttable", and that is where our word **atom** comes from. Everything, they said, is made of atoms moving through empty space. Sweet things are made of smooth round atoms, sour things of sharp, jagged ones. Water flows because its atoms are slippery.

This was a remarkable guess. But it was only a guess. Democritus had no way to test it, and the most famous philosopher of the next generation, Aristotle, did not believe it. Aristotle taught that matter is continuous, like an endless smooth jelly, and that everything is made of four elements: earth, water, air and fire. Aristotle's ideas were taught in schools for nearly two thousand years. Atoms were mostly forgotten.

#### Far away, the same idea

Greece was not the only place where people wondered about this. In India, the philosopher Kanada, founder of the Vaisheshika school, also taught that matter is made of tiny, eternal, indivisible particles, which he called *paramanu*. Nobody knows exactly when Kanada lived; scholars' guesses range over several centuries before the Common Era. His particles combined in pairs and threes to make bigger things, an idea that sounds surprisingly modern. Like Democritus, though, Kanada was reasoning, not measuring.

#### Step 1: why a guess is not enough

Here is the important difference between philosophy and science. A philosopher can say "matter is made of atoms" and argue for it beautifully. A scientist has to ask: *if that is true, what should I be able to measure?* Only when an idea predicts something you can check does it start to become science.

For atoms, that took until about 1800, and it came from weighing.

:::scene Manchester, England, 1803

John Dalton was the son of a poor weaver. He started teaching at a village school when he was twelve years old, the same age you are now. Later he taught mathematics and science in Manchester, and in his spare time he studied the weather, kept a daily weather diary for 57 years, and discovered that he and his brother were colour-blind (it is still sometimes called Daltonism).

Dalton was puzzling over gases, and over a strange regularity that chemists had found when substances combine.

#### Step 2: the clue in the numbers

When elements combine to make compounds, they always do so in fixed proportions by mass. Water is always about 1 gram of hydrogen for every 8 grams of oxygen, no matter where the water comes from. And when two elements can make more than one compound, the amounts come in simple whole-number ratios. Carbon makes two gases with oxygen: in one, 12 grams of carbon combine with 16 grams of oxygen; in the other, the same 12 grams of carbon combine with 32 grams of oxygen. Exactly twice as much. Never 1.7 times, never 2.3 times.

Why whole numbers? Dalton saw the answer. If each element is made of identical atoms, all with the same mass, and compounds form when atoms join in small whole numbers, then the masses *must* come in simple ratios. One carbon atom with one oxygen atom makes one gas (we now call it carbon monoxide); one carbon atom with two oxygen atoms makes the other (carbon dioxide). Twice the oxygen atoms, twice the mass of oxygen.

#### Step 3: Dalton's atomic theory

In 1808 Dalton published his ideas in a book called *A New System of Chemical Philosophy*. In simple words:

1. Every element is made of tiny atoms.
2. All the atoms of one element are identical, with the same mass. Atoms of different elements have different masses.
3. Atoms are not created or destroyed in chemical reactions; they just rearrange.
4. Compounds form when atoms of different elements join in small whole numbers.

He drew each kind of atom as a little circle with its own mark, and made the first table of relative atomic masses, with hydrogen as 1. Some of his numbers were wrong, because he guessed some formulas wrong (he thought water was HO, one hydrogen to one oxygen), but the *idea* was right, and it gave chemists a way to reason about things they could not see.

:::careful
> "Dalton proved that atoms exist."

Not quite. Dalton showed that atoms were a very good way to *explain* the numbers chemists measured. Many scientists used atoms as a handy bookkeeping idea but doubted they were real objects. Some famous chemists, such as Wilhelm Ostwald, argued against real atoms until the early 1900s. They changed their minds only after Jean Perrin's measurements of Brownian motion, around 1908, showed that atoms behave exactly like real, countable particles.
:::

#### Step 4: what we know now

Two of Dalton's ideas needed improving later, which is exactly how science is supposed to work. Atoms *can* be cut: they are made of protons, neutrons and electrons, as you will see in Chapter 1. And atoms of the same element are not all identical in mass: they come as isotopes. But his central idea, that chemistry is atoms rearranging in whole numbers, is the foundation of everything in this book.

:::home Cut it as small as you can
Take a sugar cube and crush it with the back of a spoon into the finest powder you can. Look at a pinch of it under a magnifying glass, or with your phone camera zoomed in. Every grain is still a sugar crystal with flat faces. Now stir a spoonful of the powder into warm water until it disappears. Taste a drop: the sugar is still there, in pieces far too small to see. Where would Democritus say the cutting stops? (The answer, in this case, is a single sugar molecule, about a millionth of a millimetre across.)
:::

Democritus asked the right question; Dalton found a way to test it. It took two thousand years to get from one to the other, and another hundred years before the last doubters were convinced.
