:::scene Paris, February 1896

Henri Becquerel came from a family of physicists who studied things that glow. Early in 1896, the whole scientific world was excited about X-rays, discovered a few weeks before, and Becquerel wondered whether glowing minerals might give off X-rays too. His idea was to put crystals of a uranium salt in sunlight, so that they would glow, then lay them on a photographic plate wrapped in thick black paper. If the plate darkened, something had passed through the paper.

It did darken. But then Paris had a run of cloudy days. Becquerel put his wrapped plates and uranium crystals away in a drawer to wait for sunshine. On 1 March, for reasons of his own, he developed the plates anyway, expecting nothing. They were strongly fogged, with the shapes of the crystals clearly visible. The uranium had not needed sunlight at all. It was giving off some invisible radiation all by itself, all the time, in the dark.

#### Step 1: a young scientist picks up the trail

Marie Skłodowska had grown up in Warsaw, in a Poland ruled by Russia, where women could not go to university. She studied in secret, worked as a governess to save money, and moved to Paris, where she studied physics and mathematics and married the physicist Pierre Curie. For her doctoral research she chose Becquerel's mysterious rays.

She measured the rays with an electrical instrument Pierre and his brother had invented, and found two crucial things. First, the strength of the rays depended only on how much uranium was present, not on what it was combined with or whether it was hot or cold. So the rays came from inside the uranium atoms themselves. Second, a uranium ore called pitchblende gave off *more* radiation than its uranium could explain. Something even more powerful was hidden in it. She invented a word for this property: **radioactivity**.

#### Step 2: hunting in a shed

Marie and Pierre set out to find the hidden substances. They worked in a leaky, freezing shed, grinding, dissolving and boiling down tonnes of pitchblende waste, a bit at a time, following the radioactivity wherever it went. In July 1898 they announced a new element and named it **polonium**, after Marie's homeland, which did not then exist as a country on the map. In December 1898 they announced a second, **radium**, which glows faintly in the dark. It took Marie years more work to collect a tenth of a gram of pure radium compound.

Marie Curie shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1903 with Pierre and Becquerel, and won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1911 on her own. She is still the only person to have won Nobel Prizes in two different sciences.

#### Step 3: the atom transforms

In Montreal, Ernest Rutherford and the chemist Frederick Soddy studied radioactive thorium in 1901 and 1902. Working with them, Rutherford's student Harriet Brooks, one of Canada's first women nuclear physicists, helped show that the "emanation" thorium and radium gave off was a radioactive gas. Rutherford and Soddy found that radioactive atoms were turning into atoms of *different elements*. Soddy later remembered saying, in amazement, "Rutherford, this is transmutation!": the old alchemists' dream of changing one element into another, happening by itself.

For more than a century chemistry had rested on the idea that elements never change. In chemical reactions, that is still true. But inside an unstable nucleus, a radioactive atom can throw out particles and become a different element.

#### Step 4: the half-life, step by step

Radioactive atoms do not all break down at once. Each one has a fixed chance of breaking down in any given time, a bit like a coin toss. For a large crowd of atoms, that gives a beautifully steady rule: after a certain time, half of them have broken down. That time is the **half-life**.

:::fig s_halflife() | Carbon-14 has a half-life of about 5,730 years. Whatever amount you start with, half is left after 5,730 years, a quarter after 11,460 years, an eighth after 17,190 years, and so on.

:::work
**Half-life with carbon-14 (half-life about 5,730 years)**
Start: 100% of the carbon-14.
After 5,730 years: 50%.
After 2 × 5,730 = 11,460 years: half of 50% = 25%.
After 3 × 5,730 = 17,190 years: 12.5%.
It never quite reaches zero; it keeps halving.
:::

#### Step 5: a clock in every living thing

In 1949 the American chemist Willard Libby turned this into a clock. A little of the carbon in the air is radioactive **carbon-14**, made high in the atmosphere. Plants take it in, animals eat plants, and so every living thing has the same small share of carbon-14. When it dies, it stops taking in new carbon, and its carbon-14 starts to halve every 5,730 years. Measure how much is left, and you know how long ago it died. If a piece of ancient wood has a quarter of the carbon-14 of living wood, it died about 11,460 years ago. Libby won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1960, and his method is used to date everything from Egyptian mummies to cave paintings.

:::careful
> "After two half-lives, everything is gone."

Half and half again is a quarter, not zero. After two half-lives, a quarter is left. Radioactive substances fade away more and more slowly, never quite vanishing.
:::

:::home The coin half-life
Take 32 coins and put them all in a box. Shake, then tip them out: remove every coin that lands heads up. Count what is left, write it down, and put the rest back. Repeat. Each shake is one "half-life". Roughly half disappear each time: 32, about 16, about 8, about 4... With real atoms there are so many that the halving is almost perfectly smooth.
:::

Radioactivity came at a cost. Nobody yet knew how dangerous it was. Marie Curie carried tubes of radium in her pockets and died in 1934 of a blood disease almost certainly caused by her years of exposure; her notebooks are still radioactive today and are kept in lead-lined boxes. Yet her work also saved lives: radium became one of the first treatments for cancer, and during the First World War she set up mobile X-ray units to help surgeons find bullets and broken bones, and drove them to the front herself.
