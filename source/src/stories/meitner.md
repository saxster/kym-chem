:::scene Vienna, 1878

Lise Meitner was born in Vienna in November 1878, the third of eight children in a Jewish family (as an adult Lise was baptised a Protestant). Her father was a lawyer who loved chess. As a girl she was fascinated by the colours in a puddle of oil and the way light behaved, and she wanted to understand them.

There was a problem. In Austria at that time, girls were not allowed to go to the kind of school that led to university, and universities did not admit women at all. In 1897 the rules changed, and women were allowed to study. But Lise had missed years of the schooling she needed. So she studied privately, cramming eight years of mathematics, physics, Latin and Greek into two, and in 1901 she passed the university entrance exam. In 1906 she became only the second woman ever to earn a doctorate in physics at the University of Vienna.

#### The basement

:::scene Berlin, 1907

In 1907 she went to Berlin, meaning to stay a short while. She stayed for more than thirty years. She began to work with a young chemist, Otto Hahn, on radioactivity. She was the physicist; he was the chemist. They made an excellent team.

But the head of the chemistry institute did not allow women in his laboratories. So Meitner and Hahn set up their equipment in a former carpentry workshop in the basement, which had its own entrance from the street. She was not supposed to go upstairs where the students worked. When the rule was lifted a couple of years later, she simply carried on.

Slowly, things changed. Together with Hahn she discovered a new element, protactinium, in 1917 and 1918. In 1926 she became a full professor of physics in Berlin, one of the first women in Germany to do so. Physicists in the 1920s talked of her as one of the leading experts on radioactivity in the world. Einstein reportedly called her "the German Marie Curie".

#### Escape

In 1933 the Nazis came to power in Germany and began driving Jewish people out of their jobs. Because Meitner was an Austrian citizen, she was protected for a while. But in March 1938 Germany took over Austria, and that protection vanished. She was not allowed to leave the country legally, and it was becoming dangerous to stay.

In July 1938, helped by friends, she slipped across the border into the Netherlands by train, with a small suitcase and very little money. Hahn had given her a diamond ring that had belonged to his mother, to sell if she needed to. She went on to Sweden, where she found a place to work but few colleagues and almost no equipment. She was nearly sixty, and she had lost her home, her laboratory and her work.

#### The puzzle in the letter

Hahn and his assistant Fritz Strassmann carried on in Berlin. They were firing neutrons at uranium, the heaviest natural element, expecting to make elements slightly heavier or slightly lighter than uranium. But in December 1938 they found something they could not believe: barium. Barium is barely more than half as heavy as uranium. How could you get it by tapping uranium with a neutron? It was like throwing a pebble at a boulder and seeing it break cleanly into two big rocks. Hahn wrote to Meitner: what could it mean?

:::scene Kungälv, Sweden, Christmas 1938

Meitner was spending Christmas in a small town on the west coast of Sweden, and her nephew, the physicist Otto Frisch, came to visit. She showed him Hahn's letter. They went for a walk through the snowy woods to think, Frisch on skis and Meitner on foot (she insisted she could walk just as fast). Somewhere along the way they sat down on a tree trunk and began to calculate on scraps of paper.

They imagined the uranium nucleus not as a hard ball but as a wobbling drop of liquid. Add one more neutron, and the drop starts to wobble and stretch, like a water drop pulled into a peanut shape. If it stretches far enough, the electric push between the two ends, both full of positive protons, drives them apart, and the drop snaps into two smaller drops. One of them could be barium.

Then Meitner worked out how much energy would be released. She remembered that the two pieces together weigh slightly *less* than the uranium nucleus did. Using Einstein's famous rule, E = mc², that a tiny amount of mass can turn into a huge amount of energy, she calculated the energy from that missing mass. It matched the energy the flying pieces should have. A single uranium nucleus splitting releases millions of times more energy than a single molecule in any chemical reaction.

Frisch borrowed a word from biology, where a living cell splits in two, and called it **fission**.

#### Left out

Within months, scientists everywhere understood that fission could release enormous energy, for power stations, and also for weapons. Meitner was invited to join the British and American project to build an atomic bomb. She refused. She wanted, she said, nothing to do with a bomb.

In 1945, Otto Hahn alone was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry (the prize for 1944) for the discovery of fission. Meitner was left out. Historians who have since studied the Nobel committee's papers believe this was a serious mistake, partly because the committee did not properly understand her contribution as a physicist. She stayed friends with Hahn, but it hurt.

#### What became of her

Meitner lived to be nearly ninety, and spent her last years in Cambridge, near family. Her nephew wrote the words for her gravestone: "a physicist who never lost her humanity". In 1997 element 109 was named **meitnerium**. Only two elements are named after real women: curium, which honours Marie and Pierre Curie together, and meitnerium, the only one named for a woman alone.

:::home Split a drop
Put a few drops of water on a smooth plate or a sheet of baking paper so they form a round bead. Gently drag the bead with the tip of a toothpick and watch it stretch. Pull it into a long peanut shape, then draw the toothpick through the narrow middle. The drop snaps into two rounder drops. This is the picture Meitner and Frisch had in their heads as they walked in the snow: a liquid drop stretching until it splits. (A real nucleus is pushed apart by electric charge, not pulled by a toothpick, but the shape of the idea is the same.)
:::

When this book talks about the nucleus, and about whether one element can really become another, remember the woman on the tree trunk in the snow, doing sums on scraps of paper.
