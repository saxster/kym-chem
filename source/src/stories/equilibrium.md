:::scene The Natron Lakes, west of the Nile, 1799

In 1798 Napoleon Bonaparte invaded Egypt, and he brought along not only soldiers but more than a hundred scientists, engineers and artists. One of them was Claude-Louis ==Berthollet==, one of the best chemists in France. In the desert west of the Nile he visited a string of salty lakes, still called the Natron Lakes today, and saw something that puzzled him: a white crust of soda (sodium carbonate, used for centuries to make glass and soap, and by the ancient Egyptians to preserve mummies) forming around their shores.

What puzzled him was this. In the laboratory, everyone knew a reliable reaction: mix soda with calcium chloride and you get chalky calcium carbonate and common salt. It went one way, and only one way, or so chemists thought. But around these lakes there was plenty of common salt and plenty of limestone (calcium carbonate) in the rock. And there, in the desert, soda was being *made* from them. The laboratory reaction seemed to be running backwards.

#### Berthollet's big idea

Berthollet's explanation was bold. Chemists of his time believed each substance had a fixed "preference" for others, so a reaction always went the same way. Berthollet argued instead that reactions can go *both* ways, and that which way they actually go depends on the conditions, especially *how much* of each substance is around. In the lakes there was a huge amount of salt, enough to push the reaction backwards. He published his ideas in 1803. Geologists today think the full story of how those lakes make soda is more complicated, but Berthollet's central insight, that chemical reactions are ==reversible==, was right, and it changed chemistry.

#### The Norwegians who did the sums

:::scene Christiania (now Oslo), 1864

Sixty years later, two Norwegian chemists who were also brothers-in-law, Cato Guldberg and Peter Waage, turned Berthollet's idea into mathematics. They measured how far reactions went with different amounts of chemicals, and reasoned about how fast they must run forwards and backwards, and found a simple rule: the more crowded a substance is, the faster it reacts, because its particles meet their partners more often. Put a forward reaction and a backward reaction in the same container, each speeding up or slowing down with how much of its starting material is present, and something remarkable happens.

#### Step 1: the escalator picture

Imagine two escalators between the ground floor and the first floor of a shopping mall, one going up and one going down. Lots of people start on the ground floor, and nobody upstairs. At first, crowds ride up and hardly anyone rides down. But as people collect upstairs, more of them wander onto the down escalator. Eventually the number riding up each minute equals the number riding down. From then on, the number of people on each floor stays the same, even though people never stop moving.

That is a ==dynamic equilibrium==: two opposite changes happening at the same rate, so the totals stop changing while the particles keep on changing. The word "equilibrium" means balance; "dynamic" means it is a moving balance, not a stopped one.

#### Step 2: unequal amounts, equal rates

Here is the part people often get wrong. At equilibrium, the *rates* are equal, but the *amounts* usually are not.

:::work
In Chapter 5's made-up reaction, each A turns into B at a rate set by 2 (per second), and each B turns back at a rate set by 1.
For the two rates to match, there must be twice as much B as A: 2 × (amount of A) = 1 × (amount of B).
Starting with 10 units of A, it settles at about 3.33 units of A and 6.67 units of B.
Check the rates: 2 × 3.33 = 6.67 and 1 × 6.67 = 6.67. Equal. The amounts are not.
:::

#### Step 3: push it, and it pushes back

:::scene Paris, 1884

The French chemist Henry ==Le Châtelier== noticed a pattern in how equilibrium mixtures respond when you disturb them. Add more of one ingredient, and the mixture shifts to use some of it up. Take a product away, and the mixture shifts to make more. Squeeze a mixture of gases, and it shifts towards the side with fewer gas molecules. Warm it, and it shifts in the direction that absorbs heat.

This pattern, Le Châtelier's principle, is not because the mixture "wants" to resist you. It follows from the rates. Add more A, and the forward reaction speeds up (more A particles bumping into each other), while the backward one has not changed yet. So more A turns into B than the other way, until the two rates match again. Chapter 5's live figure lets you watch it happen.

:::careful
> "At equilibrium, the reaction has stopped."

It looks stopped, because the amounts stay the same. But both reactions are still going, at equal speeds. Chemists proved it in the twentieth century by adding a few atoms of a slightly heavier isotope to a mixture already at equilibrium: soon the heavy atoms turned up in both the reactants and the products, which could only happen if the reactions were still running both ways.
:::

:::home A fizzy-drink equilibrium
In a sealed bottle of soda water, carbon dioxide gas above the liquid and carbon dioxide dissolved in it are in equilibrium: molecules leave the water and dissolve back at equal rates. Open the bottle and you remove the gas above, so the balance shifts: dissolved gas comes out as bubbles. Try it with two unopened bottles, one from the fridge and one that has sat in a warm room for a few hours. Open them slowly over a sink, with an adult. The warm one fizzes much more, because less gas can stay dissolved in warm water: warming shifts the balance towards the gas.
:::

Berthollet's lakes, Guldberg and Waage's sums and Le Châtelier's rule all say the same thing: most chemical change is a two-way street, and the traffic never stops. In the story of Fritz Haber, a chemist uses exactly these ideas to feed half the world.
