:::scene Your kitchen, making chai

Chai for two people needs, say, 1 cup of water, 1 cup of milk and 2 teaspoons of tea leaves. Now four people want chai. You do not need a scientist to tell you what to do: double everything. Two cups of water, two of milk, four teaspoons of tea. If you only doubled the water, the chai would be weak and watery.

You have just used one of the most powerful ideas in science. When two quantities go up and down together, always by the same factor, they are ==proportional==. And there is a second pattern, its mirror image, that is just as important. Between them, they explain a surprising amount of chemistry.

#### Step 1: going up together

Two quantities are directly proportional if doubling one doubles the other, tripling one triples the other, and halving one halves the other.

:::work
People: 2, 4, 6. Tea leaves: 2, 4, 6 teaspoons. (Always 1 teaspoon per person.)
Moles of water: 1, 2, 3. Mass: 18, 36, 54 grams. (Always 18 grams per mole.)
:::

The giveaway is that the *ratio* stays the same: tea per person, grams per mole. Draw a graph of one against the other and you get a straight line that passes through zero.

#### Step 2: going opposite ways

Now a different situation. You have a fixed job, say 12 samosas to make, and more friends join in to help. With 1 person it takes 12 minutes; with 2 people, 6 minutes; with 3 people, 4 minutes; with 4 people, 3 minutes. As one number doubles, the other halves. These are ==inversely proportional==.

:::work
People × minutes = 1 × 12 = 2 × 6 = 3 × 4 = 4 × 3 = 12, always.
:::

Here the *product* stays the same. On a graph it makes a curve that swoops down and flattens out, never quite touching the axes.

:::fig c3_boyle() | Squeezing a gas: pressure and volume are inversely proportional at fixed temperature. Halve the volume and the pressure doubles; pressure × volume stays the same.

#### Step 3: the two patterns in gases

In 1662, Robert Boyle found that if you squeeze a trapped bubble of air into half the space, its pressure doubles: pressure and volume are inversely proportional (when the temperature stays the same). About 140 years later, Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac and others found that if you heat a trapped gas, its pressure rises steadily with temperature: directly proportional, as long as you measure temperature in the right way.

#### Step 4: the trap, and why kelvin exists

That "right way" matters. Try doubling the temperature of a gas from 10 °C to 20 °C. Does its pressure double? No: it only rises by about 3.5%. Proportion only works if zero on your scale means *none of the thing at all*. Zero degrees Celsius is just the freezing point of water, not "no temperature".

The kelvin scale starts at absolute zero, where the molecules have no more energy to give up. On that scale, 10 °C is 283 K and 20 °C is 293 K, and pressure really is proportional to temperature: double the kelvins, double the pressure.

:::work
Is going from 10 °C to 20 °C "doubling"?
In kelvin: 283 K to 293 K. The ratio is 293 ÷ 283 ≈ 1.035, a rise of about 3.5%, not 100%.
To double the pressure you would need 566 K, which is about 293 °C.
:::

:::careful
> "If two things both increase, they must be proportional."

Not necessarily. Your height increased as you got older, but you are not twice as tall at 12 as you were at 6. Proportional means increasing *by the same factor*, with a straight-line graph through zero. Always check the ratio (for direct) or the product (for inverse).
:::

:::home Proportion with a balloon and a fridge
Blow up a balloon, not too tightly, and measure its distance around the middle with a piece of string. Put it in the freezer for half an hour (ask first), then measure again quickly. It has shrunk, because the cold gas inside takes up less space. Now let it warm up in the sun. In kelvin, the freezer is about 255 K and a warm room about 298 K: a change of about 17%, so expect a noticeable, but not enormous, change in size.
:::

Whenever this book says "double this and that doubles" or "halve this and that doubles", it is using one of these two patterns. Look for what stays the same: the ratio, or the product.
