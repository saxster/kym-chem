:::scene Stockholm, 1813

Imagine trying to write down a recipe in which every ingredient has a different secret picture, and every cook uses different pictures. That was chemistry for most of its history. Then, in 1813, a Swedish chemist named Jöns Jacob ==Berzelius== suggested something so simple that it seems obvious now: use letters. This is the story of how chemistry got its alphabet.

#### Secret signs

The alchemists of the Middle Ages, who hoped to turn ordinary metals into gold, wrote with symbols borrowed from astronomy. Gold was a circle with a dot in the middle, the sign of the Sun. Silver was a crescent Moon. Mercury, the liquid metal, shared its sign with the planet Mercury. Many of these signs were meant to be mysterious. Alchemists often wanted to keep their methods secret, and a recipe nobody else can read is a recipe nobody else can steal.

That was fine for secrets, and hopeless for science. Science only works when other people can read exactly what you did and repeat it.

#### Dalton's circles

In 1808 John Dalton, the Manchester schoolteacher who brought back the idea of atoms, drew each element as a little circle with its own marking. Hydrogen was a circle with a dot in the centre. Oxygen was a plain empty circle. Carbon was a circle filled in black. Nitrogen had a line down the middle. To show a compound, he put circles side by side, so water was a dotted circle next to an empty one.

It was a big step: for the first time, a symbol meant *one atom*. But Dalton's circles were hard to print, easy to confuse, and impossible to type. Imagine writing out sugar, which has 45 atoms, as 45 little circles.

#### Berzelius's letters

Berzelius was one of the most careful chemists who ever lived. Over about ten years he analysed some two thousand compounds and worked out the relative weights of the elements far more accurately than anyone before. He needed a quick way to write what he found, and in 1813 he proposed one: use the first letter of each element's name, in capitals, adding a second small letter when two elements start with the same letter.

He used the elements' Latin names, which every educated European could read. That is why some symbols puzzle English speakers today:

To show how many atoms of each kind a compound contained, Berzelius wrote a small number after the symbol. He wrote it raised up, like H²O; later the custom changed to writing it low, H₂O, which is how the whole world writes it today. Dalton never liked the letters, and kept drawing his circles. But the letters won, because anyone can write them with a pen, print them in a book or type them on a keyboard.

#### Step 1: reading a chemical formula

A ==chemical formula== is a list of the atoms in one molecule (or, for a crystal like salt, the ratio of atoms). Read it like this:

:::work
H₂O: two hydrogen atoms (the small 2 belongs to the H just before it) and one oxygen atom (no number means 1).
CO₂: one carbon, two oxygens.
C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ (table sugar): 12 carbons, 22 hydrogens, 11 oxygens: 45 atoms in every molecule.
Mg(OH)₂: brackets group atoms together, and the 2 after the bracket doubles everything inside. So: 1 magnesium, 2 oxygens, 2 hydrogens.
:::

#### Step 2: the big number in front

A number written full-size *in front* of a formula counts whole molecules. 3 CO₂ means three separate carbon dioxide molecules: 3 carbons and 6 oxygens in all. That is completely different from changing the small number inside: CO₂ is carbon dioxide, which you breathe out; CO is carbon monoxide, a poison. The small numbers say what the substance *is*. The big number says *how many* of it you have.

#### Step 3: sentences made of formulas

Put formulas together with a plus sign and an arrow and you have a ==chemical equation==, a sentence describing a reaction:

:::eq
2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O
:::

Read it aloud: "two hydrogen molecules and one oxygen molecule react to make two water molecules." Count the atoms on each side: 4 hydrogens and 2 oxygens on the left, 4 hydrogens and 2 oxygens on the right. Lavoisier's law, that nothing is lost, becomes a rule you can check with your finger: every kind of atom must balance.

:::careful
> "To balance an equation, I can change H₂O into H₂O₂."

Never change the small numbers. H₂O₂ is hydrogen peroxide, a completely different substance. You may only change the big numbers in front, which decide *how many* molecules take part.
:::

Every formula and equation in this book is written in Berzelius's alphabet: a language shared by every chemist on Earth, in every country, which a twelve-year-old in Mumbai and a professor in Stockholm can read in exactly the same way.
