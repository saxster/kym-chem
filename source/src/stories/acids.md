:::scene London, the 1660s

Robert Boyle had a favourite trick, and it involved flowers. He would crush violets, soak them, and make a deep purple syrup. Then he added a few drops of different liquids. Vinegar, lemon juice and "spirit of salt" turned the purple syrup red. Other liquids, made from wood ash or lime, turned it green. In 1664 he wrote about it in a book on colours, and he noticed something important: the colour change sorted liquids into two families, no matter where they came from.

People had known the first family for thousands of years. They tasted sour (the Latin word *acidus* means sour), they fizzed when poured on chalk, and they could eat away metals. The second family felt slippery, like soap, and could cancel out the first. We now call them ==acids== and bases. Boyle had found a way to tell them apart without tasting them: an ==indicator==, a dye that changes colour.

This is the story of how it took two and a half centuries to work out what an acid actually *is*.

#### The colour detectives

Boyle was not the first to use plant colours. A purple dye called ==litmus==, made from lichens (crusty growths that cover rocks and tree bark), had been used by dyers for centuries. Litmus turns red in acids and blue in bases, and litmus paper is still in school labs today. Red cabbage, hibiscus flowers, beetroot and even turmeric all contain natural indicators. They work because their colour molecules change shape slightly when an acid or a base gives or takes a tiny particle, which you will meet at the end of this story.

But knowing *how to spot* an acid is not the same as knowing *what one is*. That puzzle had several wrong answers first.

#### Wrong answer 1: oxygen makes acids

In the 1770s Antoine Lavoisier, the great French chemist, burned sulfur, phosphorus and carbon in air and dissolved the products in water. Every time, he got an acid. He concluded that the element he had been studying must be the secret ingredient of all acids, so he named it *oxygène*, from Greek words meaning "acid-maker". It was a brilliant guess. It was also wrong, and the name stuck anyway: every time you say "oxygen", you are repeating Lavoisier's mistake.

#### Wrong answer, corrected

In 1810 the young English chemist Humphry Davy took one of the strongest acids known, muriatic acid (we call it hydrochloric acid, HCl), and tried as hard as he could to find oxygen in it. There was none. The greenish gas everyone thought was an oxygen compound turned out to be a new element, which Davy named chlorine. So acids did not need oxygen. But they all contained something else: hydrogen. A few decades later, the German chemist Justus von Liebig suggested that the key was hydrogen that could be swapped for a metal.

#### Closer: the Swede with the risky idea

In the 1880s a Swedish student named Svante Arrhenius proposed that when an acid dissolves in water, it breaks up into ions, and that every acid releases the same kind of ion: a hydrogen ion, H⁺. Bases, he said, release hydroxide ions, OH⁻, and when the two meet they make water. His examiners were unimpressed and gave his thesis nearly the lowest pass. Twenty years later, in 1903, the same ideas won him the Nobel Prize.

Arrhenius's idea explained a lot. But it had a hole in it. Ammonia, NH₃, is clearly a base: it turns litmus blue and cancels acids. Yet it contains no OH at all. How can it release hydroxide?

#### The answer: passing a proton

In 1923 two chemists working separately, Johannes ==Brønsted== in Copenhagen and Thomas ==Lowry== in Cambridge, published the same idea within months of each other. Forget what the substance contains, they said. Look at what it *does*.

Let's go through it step by step.

#### Step 1: what an H⁺ really is

A hydrogen atom is one proton and one electron. Take the electron away and what is left is a bare proton: the smallest, most concentrated speck of positive charge in chemistry. That is all "H⁺" is.

#### Step 2: an acid gives away a proton

When hydrogen chloride meets water, the proton leaves the chlorine and jumps onto a water molecule. The electrons it shared with the chlorine stay behind.

:::eq
HCl + H₂O → Cl⁻ + H₃O⁺
:::

The water, now carrying an extra proton, becomes a hydronium ion, H₃O⁺. The chlorine, keeping the electrons, becomes a chloride ion, Cl⁻. Count the charges: zero on the left, (−1) + (+1) = 0 on the right. Nothing was created or lost; one proton simply moved house.

#### Step 3: a base takes a proton

A base is anything that accepts a proton. Ammonia does it beautifully:

:::eq
NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻
:::

Ammonia takes a proton *from* water, leaving OH⁻ behind. So ammonia makes hydroxide without containing any. The hole in Arrhenius's idea is closed.

#### Step 4: water can play both parts

Look again at the two equations. In the first, water *took* a proton, so it acted as a base. In the second, water *gave* a proton, so it acted as an acid. Whether something is an acid or a base depends on its partner. Chemistry is full of relationships like this, the way the same person can be a teacher in one room and a student in another.

:::careful
> "Acids are dangerous and bases are safe."

Both families include gentle and fierce members. Lemon juice and vinegar are acids you eat; the acid in a car battery can burn skin. Baking soda is a mild base; oven cleaner and drain cleaner are strong bases that can blind. What matters is how strong and how concentrated a substance is, not which family it belongs to.
:::

#### Back to the colour detectives

Now you can see what Boyle's violets were doing. Indicator molecules are themselves weak acids. When there are plenty of spare protons around, they hold onto one and have one colour; when protons are scarce, they lose it and their shape and colour change. The colour is a message from the molecules about how many protons are around.

:::home Make a red cabbage rainbow
Ask an adult to help. Chop a few leaves of red cabbage into small pieces, put them in a jug and cover them with hot water from the tap. Squash them with a spoon, wait ten minutes, then strain off the purple juice. That juice is your indicator. Pour a little into several clear glasses. Add a spoonful of vinegar to one, lemon juice to another, plain water to a third, and a spoonful of baking soda dissolved in water to a fourth. Watch the colours: pink and red for acids, purple for neutral, blue and green for bases. Do not taste any of it, and do not mix in cleaning products.
:::

From Boyle's violets to Brønsted and Lowry's proton, the story of acids is the story of a question that kept getting a better answer. In Chapter 7 you will use that answer to understand strong and weak acids, and the pH scale that measures them.
