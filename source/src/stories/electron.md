:::scene The Cavendish Laboratory, Cambridge, 1897

In the late 1800s, one of the most fashionable toys in science was a glass tube with almost all the air pumped out of it. Push a metal wire into each end, connect them to a high voltage, and something eerie happens. A glow appears. Rays seem to stream from the negative wire, which was called the *cathode*, and where they hit the glass at the far end, the glass shines a ghostly green. Nobody could see the rays themselves, so they were simply called **cathode rays**.

The English scientist William Crookes built beautiful versions of these tubes. He put a little metal cross in the path of the rays, and it cast a sharp shadow on the glowing glass, so the rays travelled in straight lines. He put a tiny paddle wheel inside, and the rays made it turn, as if they were pushing on it. But what *were* they? German scientists mostly thought they were a kind of light, a wave. British scientists mostly thought they were a stream of tiny particles. The argument went on for years.

#### Step 1: a question you can test

Joseph John Thomson, known to everyone as J. J., was the head of the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge. He decided to settle it with a simple test. Light is not pushed sideways by electric charges or by magnets. Charged particles are. So: can you bend cathode rays?

Magnets bent them easily; that was already known. Electric charges were harder, because earlier experimenters had failed to make the rays bend near charged metal plates. Thomson guessed why: there was still too much gas in their tubes, and the gas was spoiling the experiment. With better pumps, he got a much emptier tube, put two metal plates inside, charged one positive and one negative, and watched. The glowing spot on the end of the tube moved, *towards the positive plate*.

Things that are pulled towards positive charge must be negative. Cathode rays, Thomson concluded, are streams of tiny, negatively charged particles.

#### Step 2: weighing something you cannot see

Then Thomson did something brilliant. By bending the rays with a magnet one way and an electric field the other way, and adjusting the two until they cancelled out, he could work out the ratio of the particles' charge to their mass. He could not find either one separately, but he could find how much charge each particle carried *for its weight*.

The answer was astonishing. For its mass, each particle carried an enormous charge: over a thousand times more charge for its mass than a charged hydrogen atom in a solution (the lightest charged thing anyone knew). If the charge was about the same size, then these particles were more than a thousand times lighter than the lightest atom. Today we know the exact figure: a hydrogen atom has about 1,836 times the mass of one of these particles.

And here is the most important part. Thomson got exactly the same particles whatever metal he used for the wires and whatever gas was left in the tube. Aluminium, iron, platinum; air, hydrogen, carbon dioxide: the particles were always identical. So they must be a part of *every* atom.

#### Step 3: the atom is not uncuttable after all

For two thousand years, "atom" had meant "the thing that cannot be cut". Thomson had just found a piece of one. The particle was soon given a name that another physicist, George Johnstone Stoney, had suggested years earlier for the smallest unit of electric charge: the **electron**.

On 30 April 1897, Thomson announced his results at the Royal Institution in London. Some of the audience thought he was joking. He was not. He had found the first subatomic particle, and he won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1906.

#### Step 4: a first model, and why it was wrong

If atoms contain tiny negative electrons, but atoms themselves are neutral, there must be positive charge in there somewhere to balance them. Thomson suggested the atom was a ball of spread-out positive charge with electrons dotted through it, like raisins in a pudding. It became known as the **plum pudding model**.

It was a sensible guess, and it was wrong. Twelve years later, one of Thomson's former students, Ernest Rutherford, would fire particles at gold foil and find that the positive charge is not spread out at all. But that is the next story.

:::careful
> "Electrons are tiny balls that orbit the atom."

Thomson's experiment showed that electrons have charge and mass, so in that experiment they behave like particles. But later work showed they also behave like waves, and inside atoms they do not follow tracks at all. The best description we have says where an electron is *likely* to be found, not where it is. "Particle" is a useful word for electrons, as long as you do not picture a tiny marble.
:::

:::home Pile up some electrons
Rub a balloon on your hair or a wool jumper, then hold it near some small torn bits of tissue paper, or near a friend's hair. The rubbing moves electrons from one thing to the other, so the balloon ends up with extra electrons: a negative charge. That is the same particle Thomson found, piled up by the trillion on a balloon's surface.
:::

Every time you switch on a light, electrons are moving through the wire. Every chemical reaction in this book is, at heart, electrons rearranging. They were found because a scientist asked a simple question about a mysterious glow, and then designed an experiment clever enough to answer it.
