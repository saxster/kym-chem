:::scene Berlin, February 1828

Friedrich Wöhler was 27 years old, and he had been trying to make something quite ordinary: a salt called ammonium cyanate. He mixed his chemicals, heated the solution, and let it cool. White crystals appeared. But when he tested them, they were not ammonium cyanate at all. They behaved exactly like a substance that, until then, had only ever come from living bodies: ==urea==, the main waste chemical in the urine of mammals, including you.

On 22 February 1828 he wrote excitedly to his old teacher, the great Swedish chemist Jöns Jacob Berzelius: he could make urea "without needing kidneys, or even an animal, whether man or dog."

Why was that so exciting? To understand, you need to know what most chemists believed at the time.

#### Step 1: two kinds of chemistry?

Around 1806 Berzelius had started using a name for the chemistry of substances that came from living things: sugar, oils, wax, alcohol, urea. He called it ==organic chemistry==, because these substances came from *organisms*. Everything else, such as salt, metals, rocks and water, was "inorganic".

Many people felt there was a deep difference between the two. Rocks and salts could be made and remade in a flask. But the substances of life seemed different: they burned easily, they rotted, and nobody could make them from scratch. Perhaps, people thought, only a living body could make them, using some special "life force" that ordinary chemistry did not have. This belief is called ==vitalism==.

#### Step 2: Wöhler's crystals

Wöhler had started with substances that nobody would call "living". Yet out came urea, a substance of life. A flask had done what was supposed to need a kidney.

It also showed something else that puzzled chemists. Ammonium cyanate and urea contain exactly the same atoms in the same numbers: one carbon, one oxygen, two nitrogens and four hydrogens, CH₄N₂O. Heating had simply rearranged them. Two different substances with the same formula: Wöhler and his friend Justus von Liebig had already stumbled on this kind of puzzle with silver salts, and it led to the idea of *isomers*.

:::careful
> "Wöhler's urea killed vitalism overnight."

This is how the story is often told, but it is too neat. Wöhler's starting chemicals had themselves been prepared, a few steps back, from animal materials, so a determined vitalist could still argue that the "life force" had sneaked in. Belief in vitalism faded slowly over the next few decades, as chemists made more and more substances of life from simple starting materials. In 1845 Hermann Kolbe made acetic acid (the acid in vinegar) starting from carbon itself, and in the 1850s Marcellin Berthelot made many more, including fats. Wöhler's crystals were the first famous crack in the wall, not a single blow that knocked it down.
:::

#### Step 3: so what does "organic" mean now?

Once chemists saw that the same rules apply everywhere, the old meaning of "organic" stopped making sense. But the name stayed, with a new meaning. Today, **organic chemistry is the chemistry of carbon compounds**, especially carbon joined to hydrogen, and often to oxygen and nitrogen as well.

Why give carbon a whole branch of chemistry to itself? Because carbon is a champion builder. A carbon atom forms four bonds, and it bonds strongly to other carbon atoms. So carbon atoms can link into chains, branches and rings of almost any size, with other atoms decorating them. The great majority of the many millions of compounds chemists have described contain carbon.

:::fig c10_frameworks() | Three ways to arrange carbon atoms: a chain, a branched chain and a ring. Carbon's four bonds make all of them possible.

So the word "organic" in a chemistry book means "built on carbon". It does not mean "alive", and it does not mean "grown without pesticides", which is the meaning on a vegetable packet in the supermarket. Plastic, petrol and paracetamol are all organic compounds in the chemist's sense.

#### Step 4: the same rules all the way through

The big lesson of this story is simple, and it runs through this whole book. The atoms in your body are the same kinds of atom as in a rock, the air or a test tube. They obey the same rules: they are counted the same way, they attract and repel the same way, and they rearrange the same way in reactions. Living things are made of extraordinarily complicated and beautifully organised chemistry, but it is chemistry. There is no separate "life chemistry" with different laws.

That is not a sad thought. It is what allows medicine to work. If the molecules of life follow ordinary rules, then chemists can study them, copy them, and design new molecules that fix things when they go wrong.

:::scene Kolkata, 1944

Here is one person who did exactly that. Asima Chatterjee grew up in Kolkata and became fascinated by the medicinal plants of India. In 1944 she became the first woman to be awarded a Doctor of Science degree by an Indian university, the University of Calcutta. She spent her career working out the structures of carbon compounds made by plants, including alkaloids from the Madagascar periwinkle (which led elsewhere to important cancer drugs), and her team developed medicines against epilepsy and malaria from plant compounds. Her work rested on Wöhler's discovery: a molecule made by a plant is a molecule like any other, and it can be studied, understood and, sometimes, made in a flask.

#### Step 5: reading an organic molecule

Every organic molecule is built from two parts, and once you can spot them, organic chemistry stops looking like alphabet soup:

1. **A carbon skeleton**: the chain, branch or ring of carbon atoms, with hydrogens filling the spare bonds.
2. **Functional groups**: small groups of other atoms attached to the skeleton, such as –OH or –COOH. These are the parts that do most of the chemistry.

Ethane, C₂H₆, is a two-carbon skeleton and nothing else: a gas that does not mix with water. Swap one hydrogen for an –OH group and you get ethanol, a liquid that mixes perfectly with water. Same skeleton, new functional group, new behaviour.

:::fig c10_functional() | Ethane and ethanol: the same two-carbon skeleton, but one –OH group turns a gas into a liquid that mixes with water.

:::home Find the organic chemistry in your kitchen
Go round your kitchen with an adult and sort things into two piles in your head: substances built on carbon, and substances that are not. Sugar (C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁), cooking oil, flour (mostly starch, long chains of sugar units), vinegar (acetic acid, CH₃COOH) and butter are all organic. Salt (NaCl), baking soda (NaHCO₃: it contains one carbon atom, but chemists usually count simple carbonates as inorganic) and water are not. Here is a clue you can see without doing anything risky: look at a piece of toast that has been left in the toaster too long. The black, crumbly part is mostly carbon, left behind when the heat broke apart the carbon compounds in the bread. That is the carbon skeleton of organic molecules, with everything else driven off.
:::

Wöhler went on to a long career, but those white crystals from 1828 were his most famous result. They opened the door to the chemistry of carbon, which is the subject of Chapter 10, and to the medicines, plastics and fabrics that organic chemists have built since.
