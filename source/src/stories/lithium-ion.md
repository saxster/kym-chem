:::scene Stockholm, 9 October 2019

When the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for 2019 was announced, one of the three winners was 97 years old: John Goodenough, the oldest person ever to receive a Nobel Prize. He shared it with Stanley Whittingham, who was 77, and Akira Yoshino, who was 71. The prize was for something you have almost certainly used today: the ==lithium-ion== battery.

The story behind it took place in an oil company's laboratory in America, a university in England and a chemical company in Japan, and it begins with a worry about oil.

#### A battery from an oil company

In the early 1970s, oil prices shot up and people began to worry about what would happen when oil ran short. At the research labs of the oil company Exxon in New Jersey, a young English chemist named Stanley Whittingham was looking for new ways to store energy. He discovered that lithium ions could slip in between the layers of a material called titanium disulfide, like letters sliding into the slots of a letter rack, and slip out again. He used this to build a ==rechargeable== battery with a lithium metal electrode, giving about 2 volts.

It worked, but it had a dangerous habit. Lithium metal reacts fiercely with water and air, and as the battery was charged again and again, thin spikes of lithium could grow inside it and cause short circuits and fires.

#### More voltage: a discovery in Oxford

At the University of Oxford in 1980, John Goodenough and his team, including the Japanese physicist Koichi Mizushima, reasoned that a metal *oxide* might hold lithium ions more tightly and give a bigger push. They tried lithium cobalt oxide. It gave about 4 volts, twice as much. Oxford did not see much value in it at the time and did not patent it for itself.

#### Making it safe: a carbon home for lithium

The last piece came from Japan. In 1985 Akira Yoshino, working for the chemical company Asahi Kasei, replaced the dangerous lithium metal with a special kind of carbon that lithium ions could slip into, between its layers, without ever becoming a lump of reactive metal. With Goodenough's oxide on one side and Yoshino's carbon on the other, the battery was light, powerful and much safer. The electronics company Sony started selling lithium-ion batteries in 1991, first in video cameras. Today they are in phones, laptops, electric scooters and cars, and in huge banks that store solar power.

Here is how one works, step by step.

#### Step 1: why lithium?

Lithium is the lightest metal of all: a mole of lithium atoms has a mass of only 6.94 grams, and each atom can give one electron. Compare lead, used in old car batteries: a mole of lead has a mass of 207 grams and gives two electrons.

:::work
Lithium: 1,000 g ÷ 6.94 g per mole ≈ 144 moles of electrons per kilogram.
Lead: (1,000 g ÷ 207.2 g per mole) × 2 ≈ 9.7 moles of electrons per kilogram.
Kilogram for kilogram, lithium can move about 15 times as many electrons.
:::

A whole battery has much more in it than just the metal, so a real lithium-ion battery stores several times as much energy per kilogram as a lead-acid one, not 15 times. But that lightness is why you can carry one in your pocket.

#### Step 2: two shelves for lithium

A lithium-ion cell has two electrodes, and both are like bookshelves with gaps between the shelves. On one side are layers of carbon, graphite. On the other are layers of a metal oxide, such as cobalt oxide. Lithium ions can slide in between the layers of either one. Between the two electrodes is a liquid called the electrolyte, which lets lithium ions through, and a thin plastic separator that keeps the electrodes from touching.

#### Step 3: discharging, while you use your phone

When the battery is charged, more lithium ions occupy sites in the graphite host. On discharge, Li⁺ travels through the electrolyte to the oxide host while compensating electrons leave the graphite through the outside circuit and do useful work in the phone. Electrons enter and reduce the oxide host. Lithium stays ionic; the electronic charge of the hosts changes to balance the ion movement. This is not ordinary deposition of lithium metal.

#### Step 4: charging, when you plug it in

The charger pushes everything back: it drives electrons back to the graphite through the wire, and lithium ions follow through the electrolyte, sliding back between the carbon layers. The reaction runs uphill, storing energy, ready for next time. Within the working range, the same lithium ions can shuttle back and forth many times. Side reactions slowly trap some lithium and degrade the cell, so reversibility is not perfect.

:::work
**How much lithium moves when you use a full charge?**
For an ideal single-cell capacity account, 4,000 mAh is 4 ampere-hours of charge. Actual delivered capacity depends on the current and operating conditions.
Charge = 4 A × 3,600 s = 14,400 coulombs.
One mole of electrons carries about 96,485 coulombs, so that is 14,400 ÷ 96,485 ≈ 0.149 moles of electrons.
That means 0.149 moles of lithium ions, about 0.149 × 6.94 ≈ 1.0 gram of lithium, crossing from one side to the other.
:::

About one gram of lithium moving back and forth runs your phone for a day.

:::careful
> "Charging fills the battery up with electrons from the wall."

The battery has the same number of electrons when it is full as when it is flat. Charging only moves them, together with the lithium ions, from one electrode to the other, into the arrangement with more stored energy. The stored energy comes from the changed arrangement, rather than from adding an extra inventory of electrons to the cell.
:::

:::home Be a battery detective
Look at the labels on a power bank, a phone box or a laptop charger (do not open or damage any battery). Find the numbers marked mAh or Wh and V. Use the calculation above only for a stated single-cell capacity. A whole power bank may contain several cells, and its output mAh can be quoted at a different voltage; do not infer its total transported lithium from an unspecified mAh label alone. Then remember the safety rule every battery engineer knows: never puncture, crush or heat a lithium-ion battery, and tell an adult if one ever looks swollen.
:::
