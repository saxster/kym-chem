:::scene Utrecht, 1874

In September 1874 a 22-year-old Dutch chemist called Jacobus van ’t Hoff published a little pamphlet, only a dozen or so pages long, with an idea so simple it seems obvious today and so new that it made some famous chemists furious. The idea was this: **molecules are not flat**. The four bonds of a carbon atom do not lie on paper like the arms of a plus sign. They point out into space, towards the four corners of a ==tetrahedron==.

A few months later, in Paris, a young French chemist called Joseph Achille Le Bel published the same idea, worked out quite independently. When two people reach the same answer on their own, it is often a sign that the answer is right, and that the puzzles of the time were pointing straight at it.

So what were those puzzles?

#### Step 1: chemists drew molecules flat

By the late 1850s, August Kekulé in Germany and Archibald Scott Couper in Scotland had worked out that a carbon atom forms four bonds, and that carbon atoms can join to each other in chains. Chemists began to draw molecules as diagrams of atoms joined by lines, called **structural formulas**. Methane, CH₄, looked like a carbon with four hydrogens arranged like a plus sign.

That was a huge step forward. But flat drawings could not explain some strange observations.

#### Step 2: the mystery of the mirror-image crystals

Back in 1848 the young Louis Pasteur had been looking at crystals of a salt of "racemic acid", a form of tartaric acid that turned up in the crust that forms inside wine barrels. Under his magnifying lens he noticed that the crystals came in two shapes, which were mirror images of each other, like a left glove and a right glove. Patiently, with tweezers, he sorted them into two piles. Dissolved in water, the two piles behaved differently with polarised light: one twisted the light to the left, the other twisted it to the right, by exactly the same amount.

Yet the two substances had *the same formula*, and on paper they looked identical. Something about the molecules themselves had to be "left-handed" or "right-handed". But a flat molecule cannot be left- or right-handed. Flip a flat drawing over and it matches itself.

#### Step 3: the answer is a shape in space

Van 't Hoff and Le Bel saw how to solve it. If a carbon atom's four bonds point to the corners of a tetrahedron, then a carbon with four *different* things attached can be built in two ways, which are mirror images of each other and cannot be turned to match, just like your two hands. Hold your hands palm to palm: they are mirror images. Now try to place your right hand exactly on top of your left, both palms down, thumbs on the same side. You cannot. That "handedness" is the secret of Pasteur's crystals.

Why a tetrahedron? Because the four bonds are pairs of electrons, and electrons repel each other. Four things pushing each other away around a centre settle as far apart as they can be, and in three dimensions that means the corners of a tetrahedron, with every bond at an angle of about 109.5° to every other. On a flat plus sign they would only be 90° apart: crowded closer than they need to be.

:::careful
> "The 109.5° angle is just a number to memorise."

It is not arbitrary. It is the angle you get when four things around a point get as far from each other as they possibly can. Mathematicians can calculate it exactly: it is the angle whose cosine is −1/3, which works out at 109.47°. Nature found it long before mathematicians did.
:::

#### Step 4: not everyone was pleased

In 1877 the powerful German professor Hermann Kolbe wrote a famously scornful review of van ’t Hoff's idea. He sneered that van ’t Hoff, then teaching at the veterinary school in Utrecht, seemed to have mounted Pegasus, the flying horse, borrowed from the veterinary school's stables, and flown off into fantasy. Kolbe thought that guessing at the arrangement of atoms in space was not science.

Kolbe was a great chemist, but this time he was wrong, and the evidence piled up against him. In 1901, van ’t Hoff became the very first person ever to receive the Nobel Prize in Chemistry. (He was honoured for later work on reaction rates and solutions, not for the tetrahedron, but the tetrahedron is what every chemistry student remembers him for.) About forty years after his pamphlet, X-ray crystallography let scientists measure the positions of atoms directly, and the tetrahedral carbon was there, just as he had said.

#### Step 5: drawing molecules, step by step

Today chemists use several kinds of drawing, each good for a different job:

1. **The molecular formula**, such as C₄H₁₀, only counts the atoms. It tells you nothing about how they are joined.
2. **A structural formula** shows every atom and every bond as a line. It tells you exactly which atom is joined to which, but it is drawn flat.
3. **A skeletal formula** is a shorthand for big molecules. Each corner or end of a zig-zag line is a carbon atom; hydrogens on carbons are not drawn at all (you work them out, because every carbon has four bonds). Only other atoms, such as O or N, are written as letters.
4. **A 3D model**, like the ones you can turn in the live figures of Chapter 10, shows the true shape.

:::work
**Reading a skeletal formula**
A zig-zag line with three segments has 4 corners and ends: that is 4 carbon atoms.
Each end carbon has 1 bond in the drawing, so it needs 3 hydrogens. Each middle carbon has 2 bonds drawn, so it needs 2 hydrogens.
Total hydrogens: 3 + 2 + 2 + 3 = 10. So the zig-zag is C₄H₁₀: butane.
:::

:::home Build a tetrahedron with balloons
Blow up four balloons to about the same size and tie them together at their knots (twist the knots round each other, or tie them with a short piece of string). Let go and watch: the balloons push each other into a tetrahedron, all by themselves. Look at it from different sides; no direction is special. That is what the four bonds around a carbon atom do, and for the same reason: things that push each other away settle as far apart as they can. Now try it with three balloons (they make a flat triangle) and with two (a straight line). Those are the shapes of the carbons in ethene and in carbon dioxide.
:::

So when you see a zig-zag chain in this book, remember the two young chemists who first imagined molecules standing up off the page. Every molecule in your body has a shape in space, and in living things that shape matters enormously: for many medicines, one of the two mirror-image forms works much better than the other.
