:::scene Glasgow, 1850s and 1860s

William Ramsay was born in Glasgow in 1852. His family were engineers and scientists: his father was a civil engineer, one uncle was a famous geologist, and his grandfather had been a chemical manufacturer. William loved languages, music and mathematics as a boy, and it is said that he first became interested in chemistry while reading about how gunpowder is made. By his mid-teens he was studying at the University of Glasgow, and he went on to study chemistry in Germany.

He became a professor in Bristol, then at University College London. He was known as a superb experimenter with glass: he could blow and shape fine glass apparatus himself.

#### A puzzle about nitrogen

:::scene Cambridge and London, 1892 to 1894

The story begins with somebody else. Lord Rayleigh was a physicist who was measuring the densities of gases very, very precisely. He made nitrogen in two different ways. One way was to take air and remove the oxygen, water and carbon dioxide. The other was to make nitrogen from chemicals such as ammonia.

The two samples should have been identical. They were not. The nitrogen from air was heavier, by about half a percent. A litre of it weighed about 1.257 grams; a litre of the chemical nitrogen about 1.251 grams. A tiny difference, but Rayleigh was a careful man and it never went away.

Rayleigh published a letter asking if anybody could explain it. Ramsay offered to help. Each worked on it separately, in close touch. Their guess: the nitrogen from air was not pure nitrogen. Something else, something heavier, was hiding in it.

#### Argon, the lazy one

Ramsay passed nitrogen from air over hot magnesium, again and again, which slowly removes nitrogen by turning it into a solid. At the end, a small amount of gas remained that would not react with anything. Its spectrum (the pattern of coloured lines it gives off when electricity passes through it; see *spectra*) matched no known element.

In 1894 Rayleigh and Ramsay announced a new element. They called it **argon**, from the Greek for "lazy" or "idle", because it refused to react. It makes up nearly 1% of air. People had been breathing it for ever, and chemists had been weighing it along with their nitrogen for over a century without knowing it was there. (In fact Henry Cavendish, in 1785, had noticed a small bubble of air that would not react, but nobody followed it up.)

#### Helium, from the Sun to Earth

In 1868 astronomers had seen a yellow line in the spectrum of the Sun that matched no element on Earth, and named the unknown element **helium**, after the Greek for "Sun". In 1895 Ramsay dissolved a uranium mineral in acid and collected the gas it released. He found in it the very same yellow line. Helium was not only in the Sun: it was here, trapped in rocks.

#### A whole new family

Now Ramsay had two unreactive gases that did not fit anywhere in Mendeleev's periodic table. He made a bold guess: they belonged to a whole new **family**, a new column, and there should be more members to find.

:::scene University College London, 1898

With his young assistant Morris Travers, Ramsay cooled air until it turned into a liquid, then let it boil off slowly, collecting each gas as it came away. In 1898, in just a few weeks, they found three more:

* **neon** ("new"), which glows a brilliant red-orange in a tube with electricity (the first "neon signs");
* **krypton** ("hidden");
* **xenon** ("stranger").

Travers later recalled the moment they first saw neon's crimson glow. The whole column of what we now call the **noble gases** was complete, except for radon, which others found among radioactive elements and which Ramsay helped to study.

In 1904 Ramsay won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry and Rayleigh the Nobel Prize in Physics, in the same year. Ramsay is one of very few scientists to have discovered a whole group of the periodic table.

Mendeleev had at first doubted argon. In the end he accepted the new column, and it fitted his table beautifully: it slotted in between the halogens and the alkali metals.

#### India

Ramsay also has a link to India. In about 1900 he travelled there to advise on plans for a new research institute that the industrialist Jamsetji Tata hoped to found. His advice helped decide where it should go: Bangalore. The Indian Institute of Science opened there in 1909. (Accounts differ about how much influence his report had, but his visit is well recorded.)

:::home Find the lazy gas in your room
You cannot see argon, smell it or taste it, but it is all around you. Air is about 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen and nearly 1% argon (0.93%, to be more exact). Measure the room you are sitting in with a tape measure, or estimate it. Multiply length × width × height in metres to get cubic metres, and multiply by 1,000 to get litres. Then find 0.93% of that. For a classroom 10 m by 8 m by 3 m: 240 cubic metres is 240,000 litres of air, and about 2,200 litres of it is argon, enough to fill more than ten bathtubs. Every breath you take is nearly one hundredth argon, and it comes out again unchanged.
:::
