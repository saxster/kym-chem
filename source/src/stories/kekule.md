:::scene London, 1825

The puzzle began with Michael Faraday. In 1825 he found a new liquid in the oily leftovers of a lighting gas made by heating whale and fish oil, which a London company delivered in metal cylinders. It smelled sweet and caught fire easily. He measured carefully what it was made of, and his numbers, read with what we know today, mean it has equal numbers of carbon and hydrogen atoms. We now write it C₆H₆ and call it **benzene**.

Here was the trouble. By the 1850s chemists had learned that every carbon atom forms four bonds and every hydrogen atom forms one. A chain of six carbons, like the ones in petrol, needs fourteen hydrogens to use up all those bonds. Benzene had only six. How could six carbons and six hydrogens fit together so that every carbon had its four bonds? And why was benzene so stable, when other molecules short of hydrogen reacted eagerly?

:::scene Ghent, Belgium, 1865

August Kekulé, a German chemist teaching in Ghent, had already helped work out that carbon makes four bonds and that carbon atoms can link into chains. In 1865 he proposed something new: the six carbons of benzene join into a **ring**, a closed hexagon, each carbon holding one hydrogen. To give each carbon four bonds, he suggested the ring had double and single bonds alternating around it.

It was a brilliant idea, and it explained a huge number of facts about benzene and the many substances made from it, including Perkin's dyes.

#### The daydream

Twenty-five years later, in 1890, chemists held a great celebration in Berlin for the anniversary of Kekulé's ring. In his speech, Kekulé told a story. He said he had been dozing by the fire, and saw rows of atoms dancing and twisting like snakes. Then one snake seized its own tail and whirled before his eyes, and he woke with the idea of a ring.

It is a wonderful story, and it is famous all over the world. But it is Kekulé's own account, told a quarter of a century later at a party in his honour. Some historians think he embellished it, or invented it to make a point about imagination. We cannot know for sure.

:::careful
> "So the discovery came from a dream."

Even if the dream happened exactly as he said, it came after years of hard thinking about how carbon atoms join. A dream does not hand you an answer you have not prepared for. Kekulé himself is reported to have ended his speech by telling chemists to dream, but to test their dreams before publishing them.
:::

#### Seeing the ring

For decades the ring was a brilliant guess that fitted the facts. Then, in 1929, the Irish-born crystallographer Kathleen Lonsdale used X-rays on a crystal of a benzene compound and showed that the ring really is flat and hexagonal. She also found that all six bonds around the ring are the same length. That doesn't fit Kekulé's picture of alternating single and double bonds exactly. The modern picture is that the shared electrons are spread evenly around the whole ring, which is why chemists often draw benzene as a hexagon with a circle inside. That evenly spread cloud of electrons is also what makes benzene so stable.

:::home Build a benzene ring
Take six paper clips (the carbons) and link them into a closed ring. Hook one small elastic band or a scrap of paper (a hydrogen) onto the outside of each. Now try to make a straight chain of six clips with only six "hydrogens" so that every clip has four connections: you will find you cannot do it without closing the loop or doubling up connections. That was Kekulé's puzzle.
:::
