:::scene Tiruchirappalli, South India, 1888

Chandrasekhara Venkata Raman was born on 7 November 1888 near Tiruchirappalli, in what is now Tamil Nadu. His father taught mathematics and physics, and the house was full of books. Raman was a remarkable student. He finished school at eleven, went to Presidency College in Madras (now Chennai), and graduated at sixteen, top of his class, with a gold medal in physics. While still a student he published a research paper about light in a British scientific journal.

In those days there were very few jobs for scientists in India. So Raman took a competitive exam for the government's finance service, came first, and in 1907 became an assistant accountant-general in Calcutta (now Kolkata). He was eighteen.

#### Physics after office hours

:::scene Calcutta, 1907

Not long after he arrived, Raman noticed a sign on a building on Bowbazar Street: the Indian Association for the Cultivation of Science. It had laboratory equipment but very few people using it. He walked in and asked if he could do experiments there.

For the next ten years, Raman lived two lives. By day he was a government accountant. In the early mornings and in the evenings, he was a physicist, working in the Association's laboratory, studying sound and vibration: why violins and Indian drums like the tabla and mridangam make the beautiful sounds they do. He published paper after paper. In 1917 he finally left the finance service, taking a large cut in pay, to become a professor of physics at the University of Calcutta.

#### A question on a ship

:::scene The Mediterranean Sea, 1921

In 1921 Raman sailed to Britain for a conference. On the voyage he looked down at the deep blue Mediterranean Sea and asked himself a question. Why is it blue?

The usual answer was that the sea is blue because it reflects the blue sky. Raman was not satisfied. He had a small Nicol prism with him, a crystal that can block light reflected off a surface at a slant, and he used it on deck to cut out the reflected skylight. The sea still looked blue. So the colour had to come from the water itself. He suggested that light is scattered by the water molecules, just as sunlight is scattered by air molecules to make the sky blue. (We now know that water's colour also comes from the way it soaks up a little red light, but Raman's question started him on a long journey.)

Back in Calcutta, he and his students began to study how light is scattered by all kinds of liquids and gases.

#### A new colour

:::scene Calcutta, February 1928

When light hits molecules and bounces off in a new direction, it almost always keeps exactly the same colour. Blue light comes out blue; green comes out green. But Raman and his colleague K. S. Krishnan noticed that a tiny fraction of the scattered light, perhaps one part in a million or less, came out a *slightly different colour*.

They used sunlight focused through a telescope lens, and coloured filters, and later a mercury lamp, and looked very carefully. The extra colours were real. On 28 February 1928 Raman announced the discovery. Today, 28 February is celebrated in India as National Science Day.

What was happening? Think of a ball bouncing off a drum. Usually it bounces back with the same energy. But occasionally it sets the drum vibrating, and comes back with a little less energy, having given some away. Light does the same with molecules. Every molecule vibrates in its own particular ways, so it takes away its own particular amounts of energy, and the scattered light comes out shifted by exactly those amounts. The shifted colours are a fingerprint of the molecule.

This is the **Raman effect**. Today, Raman instruments are used everywhere: to identify chemicals without touching them, to check medicines for fakes, to study paintings without damaging them, and even on Mars rovers to study rocks.

#### The prize

In 1930 Raman was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics, the first Asian scientist to win a Nobel Prize in a science. There is a well-known story that he was so sure he would win that he booked his ship tickets to Sweden months before the announcement. It is often told, but it is hard to know whether it is quite true.

He went on to lead the Indian Institute of Science in Bangalore (now Bengaluru), and in 1948 he founded his own research institute there, the Raman Research Institute, where he worked until he died in 1970. He spent much of his later life studying the colours of flowers, gemstones and crystals. He loved diamonds, and built up a large collection to study.

:::home Make a sunset in a glass
You need a clear glass of water, a few drops of milk and a torch, and a dark room. Stir three or four drops of milk into the water. Shine the torch through the side of the glass and look at the glass from the side: the water has a faint bluish glow. Now look through the glass straight towards the torch: the light coming through looks yellowish or orange. The tiny drops of fat in the milk scatter blue light sideways more than red, just as air scatters sunlight to make the blue sky and the orange sunset. Raman spent his life studying scattered light like this, and he found something hidden in it that no one had noticed before.
:::
