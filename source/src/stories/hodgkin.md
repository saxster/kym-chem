:::scene Cairo, 1910

Dorothy Crowfoot was born on 12 May 1910 in Cairo, Egypt. Her father worked for the education service there and later in Sudan, and both her parents were passionate about archaeology. For much of her childhood, though, Dorothy and her sisters lived in England, looked after by relatives and a nanny, while their parents worked abroad. She saw them for only part of each year.

When she was ten, at a small class in her town, she grew crystals for the first time: alum and copper sulfate, slowly forming in a dish. She loved them. She went home and set up a little laboratory in the attic.

At thirteen she visited her parents in Khartoum, in Sudan, and was shown round a laboratory there that analysed soils and minerals. A chemist gave her a kit for testing minerals, and she brought it home with her.

#### Asking to join the boys

At her school in Beccles, in the east of England, girls were expected to study domestic science, not chemistry. Dorothy and one other girl asked to be allowed into the boys' chemistry class, and the school agreed. She went on to study chemistry at Somerville College, Oxford, one of the colleges for women at a time when Oxford's colleges were separate for men and women.

#### Reading the dots

:::scene Cambridge and Oxford, 1930s

At Oxford, and then at Cambridge with a brilliant and unconventional scientist called J. D. Bernal, she learned a technique called **X-ray crystallography**. The idea is simple to say and very hard to do. You shine a beam of X-rays through a crystal. The X-rays bounce off the regularly arranged atoms inside and come out as a pattern of dots on a photographic film. The pattern of dots depends on exactly where the atoms are. So if you are clever enough, you can work backwards from the dots to the positions of the atoms, and see the shape of the molecule.

For a small, simple crystal like table salt, this was already being done. For the big, complicated molecules of living things, it seemed almost impossible: thousands of dots, each needing pages of calculations by hand, long before computers.

In 1934, working with Bernal, she took the first X-ray photograph of a crystal of a protein, pepsin, a digestive enzyme. Then she went back to Oxford, where she spent the rest of her career.

#### Penicillin

:::scene Oxford, 1945

During the Second World War, penicillin, the first antibiotic, was saving thousands of lives, but nobody knew the exact structure of its molecule, so nobody could be sure how to make it in a factory. Chemists argued about it. Hodgkin and her team worked it out from the X-ray patterns, finishing in 1945. Part of the molecule was a small, strained ring of four atoms that many chemists had thought unlikely. The X-rays showed it was there.

Next came **vitamin B12**, which the body needs to make healthy blood. Its molecule is far bigger, with more than ninety atoms (not counting hydrogens) and a single cobalt atom at its heart. With the help of some of the first computers, she solved it in 1956. In 1964 she was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry, only the third woman to win it, after Marie Curie and her daughter Irène Joliot-Curie.

#### Thirty-four years of insulin

Her longest puzzle was **insulin**, the hormone that controls the sugar in your blood, which people with type 1 diabetes need as a medicine. She took her first X-ray photographs of insulin crystals in 1935. It was far too complicated for the methods of the time. She kept at it, improving the methods, training students, waiting for computers to become powerful enough. In 1969, thirty-four years after she started, she and her team finally published its structure.

She did all of this with hands that were often painful. In her late twenties she developed rheumatoid arthritis, which made her joints swollen and stiff, especially her hands and feet. She simply found ways to keep working.

#### A scientist of the whole world

Hodgkin believed that science belonged to everyone. She travelled widely and helped train crystallographers in many countries, including India and China, where scientists were also working on insulin. She worked for peace, and for scientists on different sides of political divides to keep talking to one another.

She married Thomas Hodgkin, a historian, and they had three children. She died in 1994.

:::home Grow your own crystals
You need salt, warm water from the tap, a clean jar and a saucer. Stir salt into half a glass of warm tap water, a spoonful at a time, until no more will dissolve and a little stays at the bottom. Pour some of the clear liquid onto a saucer (leave the undissolved salt behind) and put the saucer somewhere it will not be disturbed. Over a few days, as the water evaporates, small crystals appear. Look at them with a magnifying glass: many are almost perfect little cubes. Their shape comes from the way sodium and chloride ions stack inside, which is exactly the kind of thing Dorothy Hodgkin learned to read from X-rays.
:::

Whenever this book shows you the shape of a molecule, remember that someone had to *see* it first, and that for some of the most important molecules in medicine, that someone was Dorothy Hodgkin.
