:::scene Manchester and Copenhagen, 1912 to 1913

When Rutherford found the nucleus, he created a terrible problem. According to the physics everyone trusted, a negative electron moving around a positive nucleus should give off light continuously, lose energy, and spiral inwards. Calculations said it should crash into the nucleus in far less than a millionth of a second. Every atom in the universe should have collapsed long ago. Yet here we are, made of atoms that have lasted billions of years.

In 1912 a young Danish physicist, Niels Bohr, came to work with Rutherford in Manchester. He was twenty-six, softly spoken, and known for thinking aloud in long, careful sentences. He was not willing to throw away the nucleus, because the gold-foil evidence was too strong. So he did something braver: he decided that the old physics must be wrong for things as small as atoms.

#### Step 1: a clue hidden in light

Bohr found his clue in a pattern that had puzzled people for decades. If you make hydrogen gas glow with electricity and pass its light through a prism, you do not get a smooth rainbow. You get just a few sharp, bright coloured lines: a red one, a blue-green one, and two violet ones, at exactly the same places every time.

:::fig s_spectra() | Each element, made to glow, gives off light of only certain colours. Hydrogen's four visible lines are at 656, 486, 434 and 410 nanometres. Bohr realised these lines are a message from inside the atom.

In 1885 a Swiss schoolteacher, Johann Balmer, had found a simple formula that gave the positions of hydrogen's lines exactly, though nobody knew *why* it worked. When Bohr saw Balmer's formula, he later said, everything became clear to him.

#### Step 2: the staircase idea

Bohr's idea, published in 1913, was this. An electron in an atom cannot have just any energy. It can only have certain fixed amounts, like standing on the steps of a staircase but never between steps. These allowed energies are called **energy levels**. While an electron stays on one step, it gives off no light at all, so it does not spiral in. That rescued Rutherford's atom.

But an electron *can* jump between steps. When it drops from a higher step to a lower one, the difference in energy comes out as a tiny flash of light of one exact colour. Big drop: more energy, bluer light. Small drop: less energy, redder light. Because the steps are always at the same heights, the colours are always the same. Hydrogen's red line is an electron dropping from the third step to the second; the blue-green line is a drop from the fourth step to the second.

Using his staircase, Bohr calculated the positions of hydrogen's lines from scratch, and they matched Balmer's measurements almost perfectly. The word **quantum**, meaning a fixed amount, became the name for this new kind of physics. Bohr won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1922.

#### Step 3: from steps to shells

Chemists soon used Bohr's idea to explain the periodic table. Electrons in bigger atoms fill the steps from the bottom up, in groups called **shells**: the first shell holds up to 2 electrons, the next up to 8, and so on. This is the shell picture you meet in Chapter 1, and it explains why lithium and sodium, each with one electron alone in its outermost shell, behave so alike.

:::fig shells_li_na() | Shell counts for lithium and sodium. The rings show groups of electrons with similar energy. They are not tracks that electrons run along.

#### Step 4: the model improves again

Bohr's atom worked beautifully for hydrogen but struggled with bigger atoms, and it still pictured electrons circling like little planets. In the 1920s, physicists including Louis de Broglie and Erwin Schrödinger found that electrons also behave like waves. In 1926 Schrödinger wrote an equation that describes them, and its answers are not tracks at all. They are clouds: maps of where an electron is *likely* to be found. These clouds are called **orbitals**. Some are round, some are shaped like dumb-bells, and some are more complicated still.

Bohr's steps survived this upgrade: electrons still have only certain allowed energies. What changed was the picture of *where* they are. That is a perfect example of how science improves its models: the useful part stays, and the part that was wrong is replaced by something better.

:::careful
> "Electrons orbit the nucleus like planets around the Sun."

This is the most famous picture of the atom, and it is wrong in an important way. Electrons do not follow paths. The rings in shell diagrams are a way of grouping electrons by energy. The best picture we have, the orbital, says where an electron is likely to be, not where it is going.
:::

:::home See the lines yourself
Look at the reflection of a light in the shiny underside of an old CD or DVD (the tiny grooves split light into colours). An ordinary old-style bulb or daylight gives a smooth rainbow. A fluorescent tube or a street lamp with an orange glow often gives separate bright lines instead, because the light comes from glowing atoms of particular elements, each with its own staircase.
:::

So the reason you are not collapsing right now is a quantum one: the electrons in your atoms are standing on the lowest steps they can reach, and there is no lower step to fall to.
