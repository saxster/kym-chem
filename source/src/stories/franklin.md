:::scene London, 1920

Rosalind Elsie Franklin was born on 25 July 1920 in London, into a well-off Jewish family of bankers and publishers. She was clever, determined and very direct. By fifteen she had decided she wanted to be a scientist. Her father, the family story goes, was not keen at first, but she went to Newnham College, Cambridge, to study chemistry.

During the Second World War she worked on something that sounds ordinary but mattered a great deal: **coal**. She studied the tiny holes inside coal and charcoal, which affect how well they burn and how well they work in gas masks. That work earned her a doctorate.

#### Paris and the X-rays

:::scene Paris, 1947

After the war she went to work in a government laboratory in Paris, and later said those were some of the happiest years of her life. She learned to speak French, went walking in the mountains, and became an expert in **X-ray diffraction**: shining X-rays through a material and studying the pattern they make, to work out how its atoms are arranged. (The Dorothy Hodgkin story explains the idea.)

#### DNA

:::scene King's College London, 1951

In 1951 she moved to King's College London to study DNA, the molecule that carries genetic instructions in every living cell. Everyone knew it was important, but no one knew its shape.

Things went badly at King's from the start. Another scientist there, Maurice Wilkins, was already working on DNA, and the two were not told clearly who was in charge of what. They did not get on.

Franklin was extremely careful. Working with her PhD student Raymond Gosling, she found that DNA comes in two forms, depending on how wet it is, and she learned to keep the two apart, which had been confusing everyone. In May 1952 she and Gosling took an X-ray photograph of the wetter form, which was later labelled **Photo 51**. It shows a striking X-shaped cross of dark smudges. To someone who knew how to read it, that cross shouted one word: *helix*. From her measurements she could also work out the spacings inside the molecule, and she concluded that the phosphate backbone lay on the outside.

#### Without her knowledge

In Cambridge, two other scientists, James Watson and Francis Crick, were trying to build a model of DNA from wire and metal plates. In January 1953 Wilkins showed Watson Photo 51, without asking Franklin. Around the same time, a report containing her measurements, written for a committee, was passed to Crick. Franklin did not know that her results were being used.

With her data, Watson and Crick built their famous **double helix**: two twisted strands with pairs of bases joining them like the rungs of a ladder. It was right, and it is one of the greatest discoveries in the history of science. Their paper was published in April 1953, alongside a paper by Franklin and Gosling with their X-ray evidence. But for many years, few people realised how much the model had depended on her work.

It is fair to add that Watson and Crick did something Franklin had not yet done: they put all the pieces together into a model that explained how DNA can copy itself. And Franklin herself was very close. Her notebooks show she was moving towards the answer. Science is often like that: several people, each holding part of the truth.

#### What came after

In 1953 Franklin left King's for Birkbeck College, also in London, and began studying **viruses**, including the tobacco mosaic virus that attacks plants. With her colleague Aaron Klug she produced some of the most important early pictures of how a virus is built. She was respected and productive, and she was happy in her work.

In 1956 she became seriously ill with cancer. She kept working as long as she could. She died on 16 April 1958. She was 37.

In 1962 Watson, Crick and Wilkins shared the Nobel Prize. Nobel Prizes are not awarded to people who have died, so Franklin could not have been included. Aaron Klug, carrying on the virus work they had started together, won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1982, and many people, Klug among them, have felt that if she had lived she might well have shared it.

Today, her part in the story is told properly. Schools, research centres and a Mars rover have been named after her.

:::home See a diffraction pattern
On a dark evening, look at a distant street light or a small bright torch through a piece of very fine fabric, such as a thin net curtain, a fine mesh sieve, or the fabric of an umbrella held up close to your eye. Instead of a single point of light you will see a cross or a grid of spots. The regular pattern of threads spreads the light into a regular pattern of spots. Photo 51 was made in the same way: the regular pattern of atoms in DNA spread the X-rays into its famous X.
:::
