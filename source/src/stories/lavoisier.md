:::scene Paris, 1774

In his laboratory in Paris, a tax official spends his early mornings and his evenings doing something odd for a rich man: weighing things. Antoine Lavoisier owns the finest balances in Europe, accurate enough to notice a few hundredths of a gram, and he uses them on everything. Beside him works his wife, Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier. She keeps the laboratory notebooks, draws the apparatus so well that her pictures are still printed in history books, and learns English so she can read what chemists in Britain are writing. Together they are about to overturn the most popular idea in chemistry.

#### The puzzle of burning

When wood burns, most of it seems to disappear: a heavy log becomes a little grey ash. So the chemists of the 1700s had a sensible-sounding explanation. Anything that can burn, they said, contains a fiery substance called ==phlogiston== (from the Greek for "burned"). Burning is phlogiston escaping into the air. Wood is full of it, so wood burns well and leaves a light ash behind. Air can only soak up so much phlogiston, which is why a candle in a closed jar goes out.

It was a clever theory, and it explained a lot. But there was an awkward fact. When metals are heated in air, they slowly turn into a powdery crust that chemists called a *calx* (we would say an oxide). And the calx weighs *more* than the metal did. If phlogiston was leaving, why did the metal get heavier? Some chemists suggested phlogiston must have negative weight. Lavoisier thought that was nonsense. He suspected something was not leaving at all, but *arriving*, from the air.

#### Two discoveries of the same gas

The answer was found by two other men, at almost the same time, without knowing about each other. In Sweden, the pharmacist Carl Wilhelm ==Scheele== heated several substances and collected a gas in which a candle burned brilliantly. He called it "fire air". He found it first, around 1771 or 1772, but his book about it was delayed at the printer and only came out in 1777.

In England, the minister and chemist Joseph ==Priestley== used a large magnifying lens to focus sunlight onto the red calx of mercury, on 1 August 1774. A gas came off. A candle in it burned with a dazzling flame, and a mouse sealed in a jar of it stayed lively far longer than in ordinary air. Priestley, a firm believer in phlogiston, called it "dephlogisticated air": air with no phlogiston in it, so hungry for more that things burned fiercely.

That autumn Priestley visited Paris and told Lavoisier about his experiment over dinner. Lavoisier understood at once what Priestley had missed.

#### The sealed-vessel experiments

Lavoisier repeated the work with his balances, and he did one thing that made all the difference: he kept everything sealed. In one famous experiment he heated mercury in a closed flask connected to a measured volume of air, for twelve days. A red crust slowly grew on the mercury, and the air shrank by about a sixth. The air that was left would not let a candle burn or a mouse breathe. Then he heated the red crust strongly on its own, and out came almost exactly the amount of gas the air had lost: Priestley's gas.

So burning and rusting are not something *leaving*. They are something *joining*: a part of the air combining with the metal. Lavoisier named that part ==oxygen== in 1777. (He chose a Greek name meaning "acid-maker", because he thought every acid contained it. That part turned out to be wrong, as you will find in Chapter 7, but the name stuck.) ==Combustion==, burning, is a substance combining rapidly with oxygen.

#### Step 1: why weighing settles the argument

Here is the reasoning, slowly. Suppose you burn 2.4 grams of magnesium ribbon in air. It flares white and leaves a white powder.

:::work
Phlogiston says: the magnesium loses its phlogiston, so the powder should weigh *less* than 2.4 g.
The balance says: the white powder weighs about 4.0 g. It got *heavier*, by about 1.6 g.
Where did 1.6 g come from? From the air: 1.6 g of oxygen joined the magnesium to make magnesium oxide.
Check with atoms: 2.4 g of magnesium is 0.1 mol; it joins 0.1 mol of oxygen atoms, 1.6 g; together 4.0 g.
:::

And the burning log? It gets lighter only because most of what it turns into, carbon dioxide and water vapour, are gases that float away. Burn it in a sealed container and weigh the whole thing, and the total does not change.

#### Step 2: the law

In 1789 Lavoisier published his great book, the *Elementary Treatise of Chemistry*. In it he set out the idea that runs through every chapter of this one: in a chemical change, matter is neither created nor destroyed. The total mass after a reaction is the same as the total mass before. We call this the ==conservation of mass==. Today we can say why it is true: chemical reactions only rearrange atoms, and every atom that goes in comes out.

:::careful
> "Burning destroys things, so burning must lose mass."

Burning changes things into new substances, often gases you cannot see. If you catch everything, gases included, the total mass stays the same. A candle seems to vanish only because its wax becomes carbon dioxide and water vapour that drift away.
:::

:::home The bag that does not get lighter
You need a zip-lock sandwich bag, a teaspoon of baking soda, a few tablespoons of vinegar and a kitchen scale. Wrap the baking soda in a small piece of tissue and put it in one corner of the bag. Pour the vinegar into the other corner, keeping them apart, squeeze out most of the air and seal the bag firmly. Weigh the bag. Now tip it so the vinegar soaks the tissue: the mixture fizzes and the bag puffs up with carbon dioxide. Weigh it again: the same. Finally open the bag, let the gas escape and weigh once more: now it is lighter, because the gas has left. Lavoisier's whole argument, on your kitchen table. (Use small amounts so the bag does not burst.)
:::

#### A sad ending

Lavoisier's day job was collecting taxes for the king, as a member of a private company called the General Farm, which was hated by ordinary people. During the French Revolution the tax collectors were arrested, and on 8 May 1794 Lavoisier was executed by guillotine, aged 50. The mathematician Joseph-Louis Lagrange is said to have remarked that it took only a moment to cut off that head, and a hundred years might not produce another like it. Marie-Anne survived, lost almost everything, and spent years afterwards making sure her husband's work was published and remembered.

Every time this book asks you to count the atoms on both sides of an equation, you are using the idea the Lavoisiers proved with their balance: nothing is lost, it only changes partners.
