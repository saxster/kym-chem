:::scene Basel, 1738

Look at the air in your room. It seems perfectly still. Now imagine being told that it is a howling storm: countless tiny particles hurtling about faster than a passenger jet, smashing into each other billions of times every second, and drumming on your skin, the walls and the pages of this book without a moment's rest. That is what the ==kinetic theory== of gases says. "Kinetic" comes from the Greek word for motion.

The first person to take the idea seriously was the Swiss mathematician Daniel Bernoulli. In a book published in 1738 he imagined a gas as a swarm of tiny particles flying about in a closed cylinder and bouncing off a piston. He showed, with mathematics, that their bombardment would push on the piston, and that squeezing the gas into a smaller space would make the push stronger in exactly the way Boyle had measured. It was a brilliant explanation. Almost nobody paid attention. For another hundred years most scientists thought heat was an invisible fluid called "caloric", and many doubted that atoms existed at all.

#### Step 1: pressure is a drumming of particles

Think of hailstones on a tin roof. Each stone makes a tiny bang; thousands of them make a steady roar. A gas does the same to its container. Each particle that hits the wall gives it a tiny push; trillions upon trillions of them make a steady pressure. Bernoulli's picture explains both of Boyle's discoveries at once: squeeze the gas and the hits come more often; heat it and they come faster and harder.

:::scene Zürich, Aberdeen and Vienna, the 1850s to the 1870s

The idea came back to life in the 1850s, when scientists had finally accepted that heat is a kind of motion. The German physicist Rudolf Clausius worked out how far a particle travels before it bumps into another. In 1859 a young Scottish physicist, James Clerk Maxwell, made the crucial step. He realised that the particles in a gas cannot all be moving at the same speed. They are constantly colliding, swapping speed like players in a giant game of billiards, so at any moment some are slow, most are middling and a few are very fast. Maxwell worked out exactly how the speeds are spread out, using mathematics alone, decades before anyone could measure a single molecule.

In Vienna, Ludwig Boltzmann took Maxwell's ideas much further. He showed how the everyday laws of heat, which engineers used to design steam engines, could all come from the averaged-out behaviour of enormous numbers of particles. His equations are some of the most beautiful in physics. But many powerful scientists of his day, including the chemist Wilhelm Ostwald, still refused to believe atoms were real, and Boltzmann spent years defending them. He died in 1906, only a few years before the experiments of Jean Perrin convinced the doubters that he had been right all along.

#### Step 2: how fast is fast?

Using Maxwell's mathematics we can work out the ==speed of molecules==. In the air of a warm room the nitrogen molecules move, on average, at about 480 metres every second. That is roughly 1,700 kilometres an hour: about twice the cruising speed of a passenger jet. Oxygen molecules are a little heavier and a little slower; water vapour molecules, which are lighter, are faster.

:::work
**How often does an air molecule bump into another one?**
In ordinary air, a nitrogen molecule travels only about 70 nanometres (70 billionths of a metre) between collisions.
At about 480 metres a second, that means about 7 thousand million collisions every second, for every molecule.
:::

:::careful
> "If air molecules move faster than a jet, a smell should cross a room instantly."

Each molecule is fast, but it never gets far before crashing into another one and shooting off in a new direction. Its path is a hopeless zig-zag. A perfume molecule released on one side of a still room would take a very long time to wander to the other side on its own; in real rooms, gentle air currents carry smells much faster than the zig-zagging alone could.
:::

#### Step 3: temperature is average motion

Here is the deepest idea in the story. Temperature is a measure of how much energy of motion the particles have, on average. Hotter means the particles are jiggling and flying faster. Colder means slower. At absolute zero they would have the least motion possible.

Because the energy of motion depends on the *square* of the speed, doubling the kelvin temperature does not double the speed. It multiplies it by about 1.4 (the square root of 2). And at any temperature there is always a spread: a few fast particles, many middling ones, a few slow ones. That spread matters later in this book: it is why a few particles are always energetic enough to escape from a liquid as vapour, or to get over the energy barrier of a chemical reaction.

:::home Hot water and cold water
Fill one clear glass with cold water from the fridge and another with warm water from the tap. Let them stand for a minute so the water is still. Then, at the same moment, add one drop of food colouring to each, without stirring. Watch for a few minutes. The colour may spread faster in the warm glass. Molecular diffusion contributes, but circulating water and density differences introduced by the drop can carry visible colour too. This activity does not isolate diffusion: record the pattern, then ask what controlled comparison could distinguish those transport processes.
:::

None of the scientists in this story could see a single molecule. They imagined one, did the mathematics carefully, and found that it explained pressure, heat and temperature. Then the experiments caught up and proved them right. When this book shows you a box of bouncing particles, you are looking at Bernoulli's, Maxwell's and Boltzmann's storm.
