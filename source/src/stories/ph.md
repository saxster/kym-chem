:::scene The Carlsberg Laboratory, Copenhagen, 1909

The Carlsberg brewery in Copenhagen had something unusual for a beer company: its own science laboratory, set up in 1875 by the brewery's founder, J. C. Jacobsen, who believed that better science would make better beer. In 1901 a chemist called Søren Peder Lauritz Sørensen became head of its chemistry department.

Sørensen was studying enzymes: the proteins that turn grain into sugar and sugar into alcohol. He kept noticing that his enzymes worked beautifully in some solutions and hardly at all in others, and the difference seemed to depend on how acidic the liquid was. So he needed to measure acidity, and to write it down, again and again, for hundreds of experiments. That is where his problem began.

#### Step 1: the numbers are horrible

Acidity depends on how many hydrogen ions, really ==hydronium== ions, ==H₃O⁺==, are floating in each litre. Chemists count them in moles per litre. Here are some real values:

:::work
Stomach acid: about 0.01 mol/L
Lemon juice: about 0.006 mol/L
Pure water: 0.000 000 1 mol/L
Baking soda solution: about 0.000 000 005 mol/L
:::

Imagine writing numbers like 0.000 000 005 in a lab notebook a hundred times a day, and trying not to lose a zero. The numbers stretch over more than a hundred million million times from the most acidic to the most basic. Sørensen wanted something shorter.

#### Step 2: just count the zeros

Sørensen's idea was simple. Write the concentration as a power of ten, and use the number of decimal places, the power, as the measure.

:::work
0.1 mol/L = 10⁻¹ → pH 1
0.01 mol/L = 10⁻² → pH 2
0.001 mol/L = 10⁻³ → pH 3
0.000 000 1 mol/L = 10⁻⁷ → pH 7
:::

That is the whole ==pH scale==. A pH number tells you "the concentration of hydronium is ten to the minus this many moles per litre". For numbers in between, like 0.006, the pH comes out in between too (about 2.2). Mathematicians call this kind of counting-the-power a logarithm, but you do not need the word to use it: pH just counts how many places the decimal point has moved.

Nobody is completely sure what the "p" originally stood for. Sørensen wrote it as p<sub>H</sub>, with a small H, and people have guessed it meant *potenz* (German for power), *puissance* (French for power), or simply the letter he used for a quantity in his equations. Whatever its origin, it stuck.

#### Step 3: the upside-down part

Because pH counts how many places the number has shrunk, a *smaller* pH means *more* hydronium. pH 1 is far more acidic than pH 3. This trips almost everyone up the first time.

#### Step 4: every step is ten times

Each step of 1 on the pH scale is a factor of ten. So:

:::work
pH 4 has 10 times as much hydronium as pH 5.
pH 3 has 10 × 10 = 100 times as much as pH 5.
pH 2 has 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000 times as much as pH 5.
:::

That is why a small-looking change in pH can be a big deal. When the surface of the ocean goes from pH 8.2 to 8.1, the hydronium in it has gone up by about 26%.

:::careful
> "pH 6 is twice as acidic as pH 3, because 6 is twice 3."

It is the other way round, and much more than twice. pH 3 has 1,000 times as much hydronium as pH 6. Remember: lower number, more acidic; each step, ten times.
:::

#### Step 5: neutral is 7, and why

Even perfectly pure water is never quite without ions. Now and then two water molecules pass a proton between them, making one H₃O⁺ and one OH⁻. At 25 °C this happens just enough to leave 10⁻⁷ mol/L of each. Equal amounts of the two means neutral, so neutral water is pH 7. Below 7, there is more H₃O⁺ than OH⁻: acidic. Above 7, there is less: basic.

#### Measuring it: a glass that feels acid

Sørensen measured acidity with electrical cells and with colour indicators, carefully chosen dyes that change at known pH values. A few years earlier, the German scientist Max Cremer had found that a thin wall of glass develops a tiny voltage that depends on the acidity of the liquid around it. In the same year as Sørensen's scale, 1909, Fritz Haber and his young Polish colleague Zygmunt Klemensiewicz in Germany turned this into a practical glass electrode, which became the heart of the pH meter.

The pH meter as a handy instrument came from an unexpected place. In the 1930s California's lemon growers needed to test the acidity of lemon juice quickly, and a young chemist named Arnold Beckman built them a sturdy electronic meter in 1934. It was so useful that it started a whole company of scientific instruments. Today a pH meter sits in almost every lab, hospital and water-treatment plant in the world.

:::home Put your kitchen in order
If you have made red cabbage indicator (see the story *Sour and slippery: what is an acid?*), use it to line up kitchen liquids from most acidic to most basic: lemon juice, vinegar, a fizzy drink, milk, tap water, and a little baking soda in water. Pinker means more acidic; bluer or greener means more basic. You are building your own pH scale, without any numbers.
:::

So pH is a shortcut invented by a brewer's chemist to save himself from writing zeros. Every time you see a pH number in Chapter 7, read it as "count the zeros": pH 2 means 0.01 mol/L of hydronium, and every step is ten times.
