:::scene Paris, 1808

Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac liked gases, and he liked measuring them. (He also liked danger: a few years earlier he had gone up alone in a hydrogen balloon to about 7,000 metres, higher than anyone had flown, to collect air samples.) Back on the ground, he noticed something strange about how gases react.

Mix hydrogen and oxygen and spark them, and they always combine in a neat ratio *by volume*: 2 litres of hydrogen to 1 litre of oxygen. Nitrogen and hydrogen make ammonia in the ratio 1 litre to 3. And the steam that comes out also has a simple volume: 2 litres of hydrogen plus 1 litre of oxygen give 2 litres of steam (measured hot, at the same pressure). Whole numbers, every time. Why should gases care about whole numbers?

#### Step 1: the puzzle with the volumes

John Dalton, in England, had just argued that elements are made of atoms. You might think he would be delighted by Gay-Lussac's whole numbers. He was not. He thought the result was impossible, and here is why.

If a litre of hydrogen contains some number of hydrogen atoms, and a litre of oxygen contains the same number of oxygen atoms, then 2 litres of hydrogen and 1 litre of oxygen should make 1 litre of water particles (each made from two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom). But the experiment gave *2* litres of steam. To get twice as many water particles as there were oxygen atoms, every oxygen atom would have to split in half. And atoms, by definition, cannot be split. Dalton decided Gay-Lussac's measurements must be wrong. They were not.

:::scene Vercelli, near Turin, 1811

Amedeo Avogadro had trained as a lawyer, like his father, before falling in love with physics. In 1811 he was teaching natural philosophy at a college in the small town of Vercelli (he later became a professor in Turin). He read both men's work and saw a way out that neither had seen. He made two bold guesses.

**Guess one:** equal volumes of any gases, at the same temperature and pressure, contain the same number of particles. (This is ==Avogadro's hypothesis==.)

**Guess two:** the particles of hydrogen gas and oxygen gas are not single atoms. Each is a pair of atoms stuck together: what we now write as H₂ and O₂.

#### Step 2: why the guesses solve the puzzle

Let us walk through it with small numbers, pretending each litre holds just 10 particles.

:::work
2 litres of hydrogen = 20 particles of H₂ = 40 hydrogen atoms
1 litre of oxygen = 10 particles of O₂ = 20 oxygen atoms
Each water molecule, H₂O, needs 2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom.
40 hydrogen atoms and 20 oxygen atoms make exactly 20 water molecules.
20 particles of steam take up 2 litres. That is exactly what Gay-Lussac measured.
:::

No atom splits. What splits is the *pair*: each O₂ molecule breaks into two O atoms, and each goes into a different water molecule. Avogadro had invented the word for the pairs too, more or less: he called them "constituent molecules". Today we write the reaction as 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O, and its numbers, 2 : 1 : 2, are exactly the volumes Gay-Lussac saw.

#### Step 3: fifty years of silence

Avogadro published his idea in 1811. It was correct, and it explained the measurements perfectly. And for half a century, almost everyone ignored it.

Why? Partly because it sounded wrong to the chemists of the day. Many of them believed that atoms of the same element repel each other, so two hydrogen atoms could never stick together into H₂. Partly because Avogadro wrote in a cautious, mathematical way, far from the big laboratories of Paris and London. And partly because without his idea, chemists could not agree on the formula of anything. Was water HO or H₂O? Different books gave different answers, and the same formula could mean different substances in different countries. In 1861 the chemist August Kekulé printed a list of nineteen different formulas that people had proposed for acetic acid, the acid in vinegar.

Avogadro died in 1856. He never knew that he had been right.

:::scene Karlsruhe, Germany, September 1860

The confusion became so bad that about 140 chemists from all over Europe met in the town of Karlsruhe for the first international chemistry conference, to try to agree on what atoms and molecules even were. For three days they argued, and they did not agree.

But one of them, the Italian chemist Stanislao Cannizzaro, had written a short pamphlet two years earlier. It showed, clearly and simply, how Avogadro's forgotten hypothesis sorted out everything: how to work out the true formulas of gases, and from them the true relative masses of atoms. As the meeting broke up, a friend of Cannizzaro's handed out copies.

A young German chemist named Lothar Meyer put one in his pocket and read it on the journey home. He later wrote that it was as if the scales fell from his eyes: suddenly everything was clear. Within a few years, with Cannizzaro's corrected atomic masses, Meyer and the Russian chemist Dmitri Mendeleev were each building tables of the elements that would become the periodic table.

#### Step 4: what the idea gives us today

Avogadro's hypothesis is why we know H₂ and O₂ are pairs. It is why chemists could finally agree on formulas like H₂O and CO₂, and so on atomic masses. And because equal volumes of gases hold equal numbers of particles, it led straight to the idea of a fixed *number* of particles for counting: the [mole](#story-mole). The enormous number in a mole is named in his honour, though Avogadro himself never knew how big it was. That number has a story of its own.

:::careful
> "Avogadro worked out Avogadro's number."

He did not. Avogadro only said that the number of particles in a given volume of gas is the same for every gas. How many particles that actually is was first estimated by Josef Loschmidt in 1865, nine years after Avogadro died, and the name "Avogadro's number" was suggested by Jean Perrin around 1909.
:::

:::home A volume puzzle with balloons
Ask an adult to help. Put two level teaspoons of baking soda into a balloon using a funnel. Pour some vinegar into an empty plastic bottle, stretch the balloon over its neck, then lift the balloon so the powder falls in. The fizzing makes a gas, carbon dioxide, which fills the balloon. Try again with twice the vinegar and twice the soda: does the balloon get about twice as big? Gases from reactions come in amounts you can measure by volume, just as Gay-Lussac found.
:::

So the next time you see Avogadro's name in this book, remember a patient Italian professor who solved a puzzle in 1811, and a pamphlet read on a train in 1860 that finally made the world listen.
