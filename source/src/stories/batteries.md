:::scene Bologna, Italy, the 1780s

In his laboratory at the University of Bologna, the doctor and anatomy professor Luigi Galvani was studying how nerves make muscles move, and his favourite subjects were frogs. One day (the details vary in different tellings) he touched the nerve of a dissected frog's leg with a metal instrument while a machine nearby was making electric sparks, and the dead leg kicked.

Galvani spent years on it. He found that a leg would twitch even with no spark machine at all, if he touched it with two *different* metals joined together, say a brass hook and an iron railing. In 1791 he published his conclusion: animals make their own electricity, stored in their muscles and nerves. He called it "animal electricity", and Europe went wild for it.

#### The professor who disagreed

About two hundred kilometres away in Pavia, the physics professor Alessandro Volta read Galvani's work, repeated it, and became suspicious. Why did it need two different metals? Perhaps, Volta argued, the electricity was not coming from the frog at all. Perhaps it was coming from the metals, and the frog's leg was simply a very sensitive detector, twitching whenever a current passed through it.

The two men argued for years, politely but stubbornly. And both were partly right: nerves really do work by electricity, as later scientists showed. But Volta's hunch about the metals led somewhere much bigger.

#### The pile

To prove his point, Volta needed to make electricity from metals with no animal involved. He stacked up discs: a disc of zinc, a disc of copper (or silver), a piece of cardboard soaked in salt water, then zinc, copper, wet cardboard again, and so on, dozens of layers high. When he touched the top and bottom of the stack at the same time, he felt a tingle, and it kept coming. This was the ==voltaic pile==, the first battery that could give a steady flow of electricity. He described it in a letter to the Royal Society in London in March 1800.

The news spread astonishingly fast. Within weeks, in London, William Nicholson and Anthony Carlisle built a pile and used it to split water into hydrogen and oxygen. In 1801 Volta demonstrated his pile in Paris to Napoleon Bonaparte, who was so impressed he gave him a medal and later made him a count. And every time you say "volt", you are saying Volta's name.

Let's see what was really going on inside the pile, step by step.

#### Step 1: two metals, two different grips

Some metals give up their outer electrons more readily than others. Zinc gives them up more readily than copper (Chapter 8 explains how chemists know). So if zinc and copper are connected and both touch a salty liquid, electrons tend to leave the zinc, which dissolves a little as Zn²⁺ ions, and arrive at the copper side.

#### Step 2: the long way round

If the two metals touch directly, the electrons hop straight across and the energy just warms things up a tiny bit. The trick of a ==battery== is to give the electrons only one route: through a wire, outside the cell. On the way, they can light a bulb, buzz a buzzer or twitch a frog's leg.

#### Step 3: closing the loop

Electrons cannot pile up on one side forever. As they leave the zinc, the liquid around it gains positive ions; as they arrive at the copper, the liquid there would build up negative charge. Something has to keep both sides balanced, or the flow would stop almost instantly. In Volta's pile, the salty cardboard did the job: ions moving through the liquid carried charge one way while electrons travelled the other way through the wire. A battery always has two paths: electrons through the wire, ions through the liquid.

#### Step 4: stacking up

One zinc-copper pair gives a push of about one volt. Stack many pairs on top of each other, so the electrons go through one after another, and the pushes add up. Volta's tall piles gave dozens of volts. Today's batteries do the same: a 9-volt battery is six small cells stacked inside one box.

#### The battery that didn't fade

Volta's pile had a frustrating problem: its current faded within an hour or so as bubbles of hydrogen formed on the copper and blocked it. In 1836 the London chemist John Frederic Daniell fixed it. He put the copper in a solution of copper sulfate and the zinc in weak sulfuric acid (later versions used zinc sulfate), and kept the two liquids apart with a barrier that let ions through but stopped the liquids mixing: at first a tube made from an ox's gullet, and later a pot of unglazed, porous clay. Instead of making hydrogen, the copper side now grew a fresh coat of copper metal as Cu²⁺ ions picked up the arriving electrons. The ==Daniell cell== gave a steady push of about 1.1 volts for hours.

Daniell cells powered the electric telegraphs that, from the 1840s, sent messages along wires across countries and later under oceans. In school labs today the porous pot is often replaced by a strip of paper soaked in salt solution, called a ==salt bridge==. It does the same job: it lets ions flow between the two sides, so the electrons can keep flowing through the wire.

:::careful
> "A battery stores electricity, the way a bottle stores water."

A battery stores chemicals that are ready to react. The electricity is made as the reaction happens, which is why a battery goes flat when its chemicals are used up. A flat battery is not empty of electrons: it has the same number as a full one. It has simply run out of the reaction that pushes them round.
:::

:::home Make a lemon battery (with an adult)
You need two or three lemons, the same number of zinc-coated (galvanised) nails or screws, the same number of strips of copper (copper wire, or a copper-coated coin), some wire with crocodile clips, and a small red LED. Push one nail and one piece of copper into each lemon, a few centimetres apart, not touching. Each lemon makes a cell of a little under 1 volt. Join the lemons in a chain: the copper of one to the nail of the next. Connect the free copper and the free nail at the ends to the LED's two legs (if it does not light, swap the legs round). Turn off the lights: the LED should glow faintly. You have rebuilt Volta's pile. Afterwards, throw the lemons away; do not eat them.
:::
