A map is useful because it tells you about places you have never been. Look at a map of Maharashtra and, even if you have never visited Nashik, you can guess it is inland, north-east of Mumbai, and higher up. The periodic table is a map of that kind. Its greatest power is not that it lists what we know; it is that it lets you work out what you *don't* know.

Mendeleev was the first to play this game. In 1871 he described an undiscovered element he called eka-silicon, the gap below silicon. He predicted atoms about 72 times as heavy as hydrogen and a density of about 5.5 grams per cubic centimetre. When germanium was found in Germany in 1886, its atoms came out at about 72.6 and its density at about 5.3. (His life story, and the story of the periodic table, tell the whole adventure.) Now it is your turn.

#### How to play

There are three rules, and you already know them all.

1. **Find the family.** Elements in the same column have the same outer electrons, so they have the same chemical habits.
2. **Follow the trend down the column.** Going down, atoms get bigger, the outer electrons sit further out and are held less tightly, so metals get more reactive and non-metals less so. Melting points, colours and densities usually change step by step.
3. **Expect surprises at the extremes.** Very heavy atoms have so many protons that their inner electrons move at a large fraction of the speed of light, and that changes things. (This is why gold is yellow instead of silvery, an effect of Einstein's relativity on its electrons.)

#### Round one: francium

:::scene Paris, 1939

Marguerite Perey had joined Marie Curie's laboratory as an assistant at nineteen. In 1939, carefully purifying a radioactive substance, she noticed radiation that did not fit any known element. She had found element 87, at the bottom of column 1, and named it francium after her country. In 1962 she became the first woman elected to the French Academy of Sciences, as a corresponding member.

Here is the puzzle: almost nobody has ever *seen* francium. Its longest-lived form falls apart with a half-life of about 22 minutes, so there is never more than a trace of it anywhere, and nobody has ever gathered enough to see a lump. So what is it like? Use the map.

:::work
Column 1 going down: lithium, sodium, potassium, rubidium, caesium, then francium.
Each is softer than the one above, melts at a lower temperature and reacts more violently with water.
Caesium melts at about 28 °C, a warm day in Mumbai.
**Prediction:** francium is a very soft, silvery metal that would melt on a warm day, and would react explosively with water.
:::

That prediction is almost certainly close to the truth, but no one can test it directly, because a visible piece of francium would heat itself, and break apart, from its own radioactivity. The map is the best description we have.

#### Round two: astatine

:::scene Berkeley, 1940

Emilio Segrè and his colleagues made element 85 in 1940 by firing particles at bismuth, and called it astatine, from the Greek for "unstable". It sits at the bottom of the halogens, under iodine. Like francium, it is so radioactive that only tiny amounts ever exist.

:::work
Column 17 going down: fluorine (pale yellow gas), chlorine (yellow-green gas), bromine (red-brown liquid), iodine (nearly black solid with a purple vapour), then astatine.
The colour gets darker and the element goes from gas to liquid to solid.
**Prediction:** astatine is a black solid, and it behaves like a weaker version of iodine.
:::

Good chemistry, and mostly right. But this is where rule 3 comes in. Some calculations suggest that if you could ever gather enough astatine to look at, it might behave like a metal, shiny and conducting, not like the other halogens. The bottom of the table is where the simple trends start to bend. Nobody has been able to check.

:::careful
> "If an element is in the table, someone must have seen it."

Several elements near the bottom have only ever existed a few atoms at a time, for seconds or less. For those, everything we "know" about how a lump of them would look and behave comes from position in the table and from calculations, not from seeing it. That is not cheating: it is the table doing exactly what Mendeleev built it to do.
:::

#### Round three: elements that do not exist yet

The table ends at element 118, oganesson, which completes row 7. What would element 119 be?

Use the map. A new row starts with a new s room, so element 119 would sit at the top of a new row 8, in column 1, under francium. It should be an alkali metal with one outer electron. The simple trend says it would be the most violent alkali metal of all. But calculations that include relativity suggest the opposite: its outer electron is pulled in closer than the trend predicts, so it may be *less* reactive than caesium and francium, more like potassium. That is a prediction about an element no one has made. As this book was written, in 2026, teams in Japan and Russia were trying, or preparing, to make it by smashing nuclei together, so far without success.

#### Is there an island out there?

Most of the heaviest elements fall apart in seconds or less. But physicists have long predicted that certain combinations of protons and neutrons should be unusually stable, the way full electron shells are. Somewhere beyond the elements we have made, there may be an **island of stability**, where superheavy nuclei last much longer: minutes, days, perhaps much more. Nobody has reached it yet. The heaviest atoms made so far have too few neutrons to land on it, and whether the island exists, and how long its atoms would last, is still an open question.

#### Will the table ever end?

Nobody knows. Each new element is harder to make than the last, and the nuclei get less stable. Some calculations suggest that beyond about 170 protons the atom itself would behave very strangely, because the nucleus pulls so hard on its innermost electrons. But no one is close to testing that, and it is quite possible the practical end will come long before. One of the most famous charts in science still has an unwritten edge.

:::home Play the prediction game
Cover the bottom of a printed periodic table with a sheet of paper so you can see only the top three rows. Choose any column. Write down two facts about each element you can see (gas, liquid or solid; metal or not; reactive or calm) and look for the trend. Then predict the next element down: its state at room temperature, whether it is a metal, and whether it is more or less reactive. Uncover it and look it up. Score yourself a point for each prediction that was right. Mendeleev would have scored very well.
:::

The periodic table is not a finished list to learn by heart. It is a tool for thinking, and its most exciting boxes are the ones where you have to reason your way to the answer.
