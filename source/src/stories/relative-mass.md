:::scene Manchester, 1803

John Dalton was a quiet Quaker schoolteacher who kept a weather diary every single day for over fifty years. He was also colour-blind (the condition was called "Daltonism" after him, because he was the first to describe it scientifically). And in his notebooks in Manchester, around 1803, he did something nobody had dared try. He started to make a list of how heavy atoms are.

He could not weigh one. Nobody could, and nobody would be able to for a long time. So Dalton asked a cleverer question: not "how heavy is an atom?" but "how heavy is one kind of atom *compared with another*?"

#### Step 1: comparing, not weighing

Think of a see-saw. You do not need to know anyone's weight in kilograms to discover that your father is about twice as heavy as you: if he sits half as far from the middle as you do, the see-saw balances. Comparing is often easier than measuring.

Dalton chose the lightest thing he knew, the hydrogen atom, and called its weight 1. Then he looked at compounds. If 1 gram of hydrogen combines with 8 grams of oxygen to make water, and water has one oxygen atom for every two hydrogen atoms, then:

:::work
Pretend the sample holds just 10 oxygen atoms. Then it holds 20 hydrogen atoms (two for every oxygen).
20 hydrogen atoms weigh 1 gram, so each hydrogen atom weighs 1 ÷ 20 = 0.05 g (pretend grams).
10 oxygen atoms weigh 8 grams, so each oxygen atom weighs 8 ÷ 10 = 0.8 g.
0.8 ÷ 0.05 = 16. One oxygen atom is 16 times as heavy as one hydrogen atom, whatever the real number of atoms is.
:::

Dalton himself thought water was HO, one of each, so he wrote oxygen as 7 (his measurements were also a little off). The method was brilliant; the formulas were the problem. It took Avogadro's idea, and the congress at Karlsruhe in 1860, before chemists agreed on formulas and so on the right numbers.

#### Step 2: arguing about the starting point

If everything is a comparison, you have to choose something to compare with, and chemists argued about this for 150 years.

- **Dalton** said hydrogen = 1.
- **Berzelius**, the great Swedish chemist who measured more atomic weights more carefully than anyone of his time, said oxygen = 100, because oxygen combines with almost everything, which made the measurements easier.
- Later chemists, led by the Belgian Jean Servais Stas, whose measurements were famously precise, settled on oxygen = 16, which kept hydrogen close to 1.

Then a new problem appeared. In the 1920s and 1930s physicists discovered that natural oxygen is a mixture of isotopes. Physicists began using one isotope, oxygen-16, as exactly 16; chemists kept using natural oxygen as 16. The two scales differed by about 3 parts in 10,000: tiny, but enough to cause confusion.

In 1961 both sides agreed on a new standard that everybody could use: one atom of **carbon-12** counts as exactly 12. The unit this defines is called the ==unified atomic mass unit==, written u, because it unified the two scales. One u is one-twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom.

#### Step 3: so what does an atom actually weigh?

Once Avogadro's constant was known, chemists could finally turn comparisons into real masses.

:::work
One mole of hydrogen atoms has a mass of about 1.008 g, and contains 6.022 × 10²³ atoms.
Mass of one hydrogen atom = 1.008 ÷ (6.022 × 10²³) ≈ 1.67 × 10⁻²⁴ g.
:::

That is 0.000 000 000 000 000 000 000 001 67 grams. A grain of sugar has more than a hundred million million million times that mass.

#### Step 4: why the numbers are not whole

Look at a periodic table and you see numbers like chlorine 35.45 and copper 63.55. If atoms are built from protons and neutrons, each with a mass close to 1 u, why are these not whole numbers? There are two reasons, one big and one small.

**The big reason: mixtures.** Natural chlorine is about three-quarters chlorine-35 and one-quarter chlorine-37. The periodic table gives the *average* for the natural mixture, which is 35.45, even though no single chlorine atom has that mass. (See the [weighted averages](#story-averages) tool for how the average is worked out.)

**The small reason: binding.** Even a single isotope is not exactly a whole number, except carbon-12, which is 12 by definition. When protons and neutrons pull together into a nucleus, a little mass is turned into energy and given off, following Einstein's famous E = mc². So a nucleus weighs slightly *less* than the particles it is made from. For carbon-12, the difference is a little under 1%.

:::careful
> "The relative atomic mass of oxygen is 16 grams."

Relative atomic mass has no units: it is a comparison, like "16 times as heavy as a hydrogen atom" (more precisely, 16 twelfths of a carbon-12 atom). The *grams* come in when you take a [mole](#story-mole) of atoms: a mole of oxygen atoms has a mass of 16 grams.
:::

:::home A see-saw for comparing masses
Make a balance from a ruler resting on a pencil. Put a coin at one end and see how many paper clips (or dried beans) you need at the same distance on the other end to balance it. You have just found the coin's mass *relative to a paper clip*, without knowing either mass in grams. Choose a different "standard" object and every number changes, but the comparisons stay true. That is exactly the argument chemists had for 150 years.
:::

Every relative atomic mass in this book, every molar mass, every "18 grams of water", rests on Dalton's simple idea: if you cannot weigh one, compare.
