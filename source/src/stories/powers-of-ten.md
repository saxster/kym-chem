:::scene Syracuse, Sicily, about 2,250 years ago

The Greek mathematician Archimedes once set himself a strange challenge: to count how many grains of sand it would take to fill the whole universe. People said the grains of sand were beyond number. Archimedes disagreed. The problem, he saw, was not the sand. It was that the Greek way of writing numbers ran out at a myriad, ten thousand. So in a little book called *The Sand Reckoner* he invented a way of building bigger and bigger numbers by stacking "myriads of myriads" on top of each other, and came up with an answer, which in our notation is less than 10⁶³ grains.

His answer about the universe was wrong (it is much bigger than he thought), but his idea was right: to handle huge numbers, you need a way of writing *how many times you multiply by ten*. Some centuries later, mathematicians in India developed the place-value digits we still use, with zero as a digit, and in 628 Brahmagupta wrote down rules for calculating with zero. That made writing big numbers easy. And today we have the neatest shorthand of all: powers of ten.

#### Step 1: counting the tens

You already know that 100 = 10 × 10 and 1,000 = 10 × 10 × 10. A power of ten just counts how many tens are multiplied:

:::work
10¹ = 10
10² = 10 × 10 = 100
10³ = 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000
10⁶ = a million (six tens multiplied; also six zeros after the 1)
10⁷ = ten million: one crore
:::

The little raised number tells you how many zeros follow the 1. In India you already use a few special names for powers of ten: a lakh is 10⁵ (1,00,000) and a crore is 10⁷ (1,00,00,000). Scientists just use the little number instead of a name, so it works for any size.

#### Step 2: going smaller

What about very small numbers? Each step *down* divides by ten, and we write it with a minus sign:

:::work
10⁰ = 1 (no tens at all)
10⁻¹ = 1 ÷ 10 = 0.1
10⁻² = 1 ÷ 100 = 0.01
10⁻³ = 1 ÷ 1,000 = 0.001, a thousandth (a millimetre is 10⁻³ metres)
10⁻⁹ = a billionth (a nanometre is 10⁻⁹ metres)
:::

The minus sign does not mean "negative". It means "divide by ten this many times".

:::figwide scale_ladder() | Each step to the right is ten times smaller. Ten powers of ten take you from your own height to a single water molecule.

#### Step 3: writing any number this way

Most numbers are not neat powers of ten. So we write them as an ordinary number between 1 and 10, multiplied by a power of ten. This is called ==scientific notation== (or *standard form*).

:::work
602,000,000,000,000,000,000,000 = 6.02 × 10²³ (count the places the decimal point moves: 23)
0.000 000 000 3 metres = 3 × 10⁻¹⁰ m (the point moves 10 places the other way)
:::

A quick check: a big power means a big number; a minus power means a small one.

#### Step 4: multiplying and dividing the easy way

Here is the real magic. To multiply powers of ten, *add* the little numbers. To divide, *subtract* them.

:::work
10³ × 10⁴ = 10⁷ (a thousand times ten thousand is ten million)
10²³ ÷ 10²¹ = 10²
(6 × 10²³) × (3 × 10⁻²³) = 18 × 10⁰ = 18
:::

That last line is a real chemistry sum: about 6 × 10²³ water molecules, each with a mass of about 3 × 10⁻²³ grams, make 18 grams of water. The giant number and the tiny number cancel out, and you are left with a gulp of water you can hold.

:::careful
> "10⁻³ is a negative number."

It is a small *positive* number: one thousandth. A powers-of-ten number is never negative or zero, however big the minus sign in the little number. 10⁻¹⁰⁰ is unimaginably small, but it is still more than nothing.
:::

:::home Fold your way to the Moon (on paper)
Take a sheet of paper, about 0.1 mm thick, and fold it in half again and again. Each fold doubles the thickness. You will probably manage six or seven folds before it gets too stiff. Now work it out with a calculator: after 10 folds the stack would be about 10 cm thick; after 20 folds, about 100 metres; after 42 folds, about 440,000 km: more than the distance to the Moon. Doubling grows fast, and powers of ten are how we keep track of numbers that grow like that.
:::

Every big and small number in this book, from 10⁻¹⁰ metres for an atom to 6.022 × 10²³ for a [mole](#story-mole), is written in this shorthand. Once you can count the tens, those numbers stop being scary and start being useful.
