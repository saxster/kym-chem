:::scene Jena, Germany, 1823

The chemist Johann Döbereiner lets a stream of hydrogen gas flow over a small piece of spongy platinum metal. Nobody has lit a match. Yet within moments the platinum begins to glow red, and the hydrogen bursts into flame. Döbereiner turns the trick into a gadget: a glass jar that makes hydrogen, a nozzle and a pinch of platinum. Press a lever and you have fire. "Döbereiner's lamp" was sold by the thousands as a lighter for pipes and candles, decades before cheap matches. But nobody understood how it worked. The platinum was not burning, and it was not used up. It just sat there and made the reaction happen.

#### The puzzle of things that do not happen

Here is a stranger puzzle. Hydrogen and oxygen react violently: Chapter 4 showed that a spark makes them explode. And yet you can mix them in a jar at room temperature and leave them for years, and almost nothing happens. This page is the same. Paper and oxygen react to give carbon dioxide and water and a great deal of heat; that is what burning is. You are surrounded by oxygen. So why doesn't this book burst into flame?

#### Berzelius gives it a name

In 1835 Jöns Jacob Berzelius, collecting together several strange cases like Döbereiner's lamp, gave the effect a name: ==catalysis==, from a Greek word meaning "loosening". A ==catalyst== is a substance that makes a reaction go faster without being used up itself. Berzelius could name it, but he could not explain it. The explanation needed a new idea about why reactions happen at all.

#### Arrhenius and the hill

:::scene Uppsala and Stockholm, Sweden, 1889

The Swedish chemist Svante Arrhenius (the same man who had explained ions in water a few years earlier) noticed that the speed of many reactions rises steeply with temperature, much more steeply than you would expect if it simply depended on how often particles collide. In 1889 he suggested the reason. For molecules to react, they must hit each other hard enough. Only collisions with more than a certain minimum energy can stretch and break the old bonds so that new ones can form. That minimum is called the ==activation energy==.

#### Step 1: picture a hill

Think of a ball resting in a valley, with a deeper valley on the other side of a hill. The ball would be lower, and more settled, in the far valley: that is the products, lower in energy than the reactants. But to get there, the ball must first be pushed *up* the hill. The top of the hill is the ==barrier==. A gentle nudge just rolls the ball back. Only a hard enough push gets it over.

:::fig c5_catalyst() | A reaction's energy hill. The reactants sit in one valley, the products in a lower one, but the path between them climbs over a barrier. A catalyst offers a lower route over the hill (dashed) between the same two valleys.
:::live barrier {"catalyst":true}

That is why the book does not burn. Most oxygen molecules hitting the paper are not moving fast enough to get over the barrier. A match is a way of giving a few molecules enough energy to get over; once the burning starts, the heat it releases pushes neighbouring molecules over too, and the fire spreads.

#### Step 2: why temperature matters so much

In any gas or liquid, molecules have a spread of energies: most are fairly slow, a few are fast. Only the fast few can get over the barrier. Warm things up, and the whole spread shifts: the number of molecules fast enough to get over rises sharply. A useful rule of thumb for many reactions near room temperature is that ten degrees warmer roughly doubles the speed.

:::work
**Why food lasts longer in the fridge.** The chemistry that spoils food runs, as a rough rule, about twice as fast for every 10 °C warmer.
From a 4 °C fridge to a 24 °C kitchen is 20 degrees: two doublings, 2 × 2 = 4 times faster.
To a 44 °C Mumbai rooftop in May: four doublings, 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 16 times faster.
(This is only a rule of thumb; real reactions vary.)
:::

#### Step 3: what a catalyst does

A catalyst does not push the ball harder. It opens a different path, a lower pass through the hills. With a lower barrier, many more molecules have enough energy to get across, so the reaction runs much faster. On Döbereiner's platinum, hydrogen and oxygen molecules stick to the metal surface, where their bonds are loosened, and they react easily there. The platinum comes out unchanged, ready for the next molecules.

Two things a catalyst does *not* do. It does not change where the valleys are: the products end up with exactly the same energy, with or without it. And it lowers the hill in *both* directions, so it speeds up the backward reaction too. Chapter 5 explains why that means a catalyst cannot change how far a reaction goes, only how fast it gets there.

#### Life's catalysts

Your body is full of catalysts called ==enzymes==: large protein molecules, each shaped to speed up one particular reaction. An enzyme in your blood called carbonic anhydrase helps carbon dioxide react with water more than a million times faster than it would on its own. Without enzymes, digesting a meal would take years. Cars carry catalysts too: the catalytic converter, coated with tiny amounts of platinum, palladium and rhodium, turns poisonous exhaust gases into less harmful ones.

:::careful
> "A catalyst gives the reaction extra energy."

It gives no energy at all. It provides a route that needs less. That is why it is not used up: it is a guide, not a fuel.
:::

:::home Two experiments with heat and enzymes
**Glowsticks.** Snap two identical glowsticks to start them (do not cut them open). Put one in a glass of warm tap water and one in a glass of ice water. After a minute, compare them in a dark room. The warm one glows brighter, because its light-making reaction runs faster; it will also run out sooner. The cold one glows dimly but for longer.

**Pineapple jelly.** With an adult, make two small bowls of jelly from gelatin powder. Stir pieces of fresh pineapple into one and pieces of tinned pineapple into the other, and put both in the fridge. The bowl with tinned pineapple sets; the one with fresh pineapple stays runny. Fresh pineapple contains an enzyme that cuts up the gelatin's protein chains. Tinned pineapple has been heated, which destroys the enzyme's delicate shape.
:::

So the next time you strike a match, remember the hill: the match is not the energy of the fire, it is the push that gets the first few molecules over the top.
