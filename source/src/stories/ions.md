:::scene London, 1833

Michael Faraday left school at 13 to work for a bookbinder. He read the books he was binding, especially the science ones, and taught himself so well that he got a job washing bottles for Humphry Davy at the Royal Institution. Twenty years later he was one of the greatest experimenters in the world.

In the early 1830s Faraday was studying something puzzling. Dry salt does not carry electricity. Pure water hardly carries it. But dissolve salt in water, dip in two metal plates connected to a battery, and a current flows. At the plates, new substances appear: gas bubbles at one, other chemicals at the other. Something in the liquid was carrying electric charge from one plate to the other. What?

#### Naming the travellers

Faraday did not know what these charge-carriers were, but he needed words to describe his experiments. He wrote to a friend, the scholar William Whewell at Cambridge, who was brilliant at inventing scientific words from Greek. Together they settled on a new vocabulary that every chemist still uses:

- **ion**, from the Greek for "going" or "traveller", for whatever carries the charge through the liquid;
- **anode** ("the way up") and **cathode** ("the way down") for the two plates;
- **electrode** for any plate, **electrolyte** for the liquid that carries current, and **electrolysis** for splitting substances with electricity.

Faraday also found exact rules. The amount of substance produced at a plate was always in proportion to the amount of electricity that passed. Electricity seemed to come in fixed portions, as if it too were made of particles. That hint pointed towards the electron, sixty years before anyone found it.

#### The student who said the unthinkable

:::scene Uppsala, Sweden, 1884

Fifty years later, a young Swede named Svante Arrhenius was measuring how well different salt solutions conduct electricity. His results led him to a startling idea: when salt dissolves in water, it *splits up on its own* into charged pieces, even when no electric current is flowing at all. Sodium chloride does not wait for a battery to pull it apart. As soon as it dissolves, it is already Na⁺ and Cl⁻, drifting separately through the water.

Most chemists thought this was nonsense. Sodium is a metal that bursts into flame in water, and chlorine is a poisonous gas. How could a glass of salty water be full of free sodium and chlorine? Arrhenius's thesis was given the lowest passing grade his university could award. He very nearly failed.

The answer to the objection is the key to the whole idea, and you already know it from Chapter 1: an ion is *not* the same as an atom. Na⁺ has one electron fewer than a sodium atom, and that makes all the difference. It does not react with water the way sodium metal does. Cl⁻ has one electron more than a chlorine atom, and it is not a poisonous gas. They are different particles with different behaviour.

A few scientists, especially Wilhelm Ostwald, then a professor in Riga, took Arrhenius seriously and tested his ideas. The evidence piled up. In 1903, nineteen years after nearly failing, Arrhenius won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for exactly the idea that had almost sunk him.

#### Ions, step by step

1. **An ion is an atom (or group of atoms) with a charge**, because it has gained or lost electrons. Lose electrons and you are positive; gain electrons and you are negative. The nucleus never changes.
2. **Positive ions are called cations** and negative ions **anions**. (Cations travel towards the cathode, anions towards the anode: Faraday and Whewell's words again.)
3. **In a solid salt crystal, ions are locked in place**, so they cannot carry charge. That is why dry salt does not conduct.
4. **Dissolve the salt (or melt it), and the ions can move.** Push them with a battery and positive ions drift one way, negative ions the other: an electric current made of moving atoms.
5. **Some ions are groups of atoms** that stay together and carry a charge as a team: sulfate, SO₄²⁻; hydroxide, OH⁻; ammonium, NH₄⁺.

:::careful
> "Salt water conducts electricity because electrons swim through it."

In metal wires, current is carried by electrons. In salt water, it is carried by ions, whole charged atoms moving through the liquid. The electrons stay at the plates, where they are handed on to or taken from the ions.
:::

:::home Salt that disappears and comes back
Stir a teaspoon of salt into half a glass of warm tap water until it vanishes. The salt has not gone: the ions have spread out among the water molecules, too small to see. Pour a little onto a dark plate and leave it somewhere warm and dry for a day or two. As the water evaporates, the ions find each other again and lock back together. Look at the white crust with a magnifying glass, or your phone's camera zoomed in: you should see tiny cubes, the shape of the sodium chloride lattice.
:::

Every time this book writes a charge on a symbol, Na⁺ or O²⁻, it is using Faraday's word and Arrhenius's idea: atoms that carry electricity by having one or two electrons too few, or too many.
