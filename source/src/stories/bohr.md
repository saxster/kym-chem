:::scene Copenhagen, 1885

Niels Bohr was born on 7 October 1885 in Copenhagen, the capital of Denmark. His father was a professor of physiology, and his mother came from a family of bankers and teachers. The house was full of scientists and arguments, and Niels and his younger brother Harald grew up listening to both.

The two brothers were inseparable, and both were very good footballers. Niels played in goal for a Copenhagen university club. Harald was even better: he played for Denmark at the 1908 Olympic Games, and the Danish team won the silver medal. For a while, Harald was the more famous of the two Bohrs. That changed.

Niels was not a fast talker or a quick writer. He thought slowly, out loud, turning a problem over and over, and he rewrote his papers again and again. His mother sometimes wrote out his work as he dictated it. But when he had understood something, he had understood it all the way down.

#### A problem nobody could solve

In 1912 Bohr went to Manchester to work with Ernest Rutherford, who had just discovered that atoms have a tiny nucleus with electrons around it. But there was a serious problem with that picture. According to the physics of the time, an electron circling a nucleus should give out light continuously, lose energy and spiral into the nucleus in a tiny fraction of a second. Every atom in the universe should collapse. They obviously do not.

There was a second puzzle too. When you heat hydrogen gas until it glows and split its light with a prism, you do not see a rainbow. You see a few sharp coloured lines, always at exactly the same places. Why only those colours?

#### The staircase

:::scene Copenhagen, 1913

Back in Copenhagen in 1913, Bohr made a bold suggestion. Suppose, he said, an electron in an atom cannot have *any* amount of energy, only certain fixed amounts, like the steps of a staircase. You can stand on step 2 or step 3, but not on step two-and-a-half. While the electron stays on one step, it gives out no light and does not spiral in. It simply stays.

But when an electron drops from a higher step to a lower one, the energy it loses comes out as a single packet of light. A big drop gives a packet with lots of energy (blue or violet light); a small drop gives less (red light). Because the steps are fixed, the drops are fixed, and so only certain colours can ever come out. Those are hydrogen's lines.

Bohr calculated the heights of the steps for hydrogen, and the colours his staircase predicted matched the measured lines almost perfectly. He could not fully explain *why* the staircase existed. That came later, with the new quantum mechanics of the 1920s, which replaced his neat circular orbits with fuzzier clouds. But the central idea, fixed energy levels and light coming out when electrons drop between them, is still at the heart of chemistry. It is why the electron shells of Chapter 2 exist, and why every element has its own fingerprint of colours.

#### The institute

In 1921 Bohr opened his own institute in Copenhagen, and for the next twenty years it was one of the most exciting places in the world to do physics. Young scientists came from Germany, Russia, Britain, America, Japan and India. They argued, played table tennis, went to the cinema together, and in the space of a few years built quantum mechanics. Bohr won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1922.

#### Two medals in a jar

:::scene Copenhagen, April 1940

In April 1940, German soldiers occupied Denmark. In Bohr's institute were two gold Nobel medals that did not belong to Bohr. The German physicists Max von Laue and James Franck had sent them to Copenhagen for safe keeping, because both men opposed the Nazis, and sending gold out of Germany was forbidden. If the soldiers found the medals, with the names engraved on them, von Laue and Franck could be in serious danger.

A Hungarian chemist at the institute, George de Hevesy, had an idea. Burying them might not work; the soldiers could search. So he *dissolved* them. He put the medals in **aqua regia**, "royal water", a fierce mixture of nitric acid and hydrochloric acid that is one of the few things that can dissolve gold. It took a while, but the medals vanished, leaving an orange-coloured liquid in a jar. De Hevesy put the jar on a shelf among all the other bottles of chemicals, and there it stayed, in plain sight, through the whole war. Nobody looked twice at it.

When the war ended, the jar was still there. The gold was taken out of the solution, and the Nobel Foundation used it to make new medals for von Laue and Franck.

That is a perfect piece of chemistry. Dissolving the gold did not destroy a single gold atom. The atoms were simply spread out through the liquid as tiny charged gold particles (ions), invisible but all still there, and a chemist could gather them up again. You can change what gold looks like, or where it is, but by chemistry alone you can never make it stop being gold.

#### Escape

Bohr's mother came from a Jewish family, and by 1943 he was in danger himself. In September that year, warned that he was about to be arrested, he and his wife escaped by small boat across the narrow sea to Sweden. Within days he was talking to the Swedish government about helping Denmark's Jewish people, thousands of whom were ferried across the same stretch of water to safety by ordinary Danes. Soon after, Bohr was flown to Britain, crammed into the bomb bay of an aeroplane.

After the war he worked hard, with mixed success, to persuade world leaders to share knowledge about nuclear energy openly rather than race each other to build weapons. He died in Copenhagen in 1962. Element 107 is called **bohrium**.

:::home Dissolve it, and get it back
You can repeat de Hevesy's trick with something much safer than gold. Stir a teaspoon of salt into half a glass of water until it disappears completely. Is the salt gone? Taste a drop on your fingertip: it is still there. Now pour a little of the salt water onto a saucer and leave it on a sunny windowsill for a few days. As the water evaporates, the salt comes back as tiny square crystals. Dissolving hides things; it does not destroy them.
:::

Every time this book talks about energy levels, shells, or why a flame turns a particular colour, it is climbing Bohr's staircase.
