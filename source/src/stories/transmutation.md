:::scene Montreal, Canada, 1901

In a basement laboratory at McGill University, two young scientists were puzzling over a radioactive substance called thorium. One was Ernest Rutherford, a farmer's son from New Zealand. The other was Frederick Soddy, a chemist from England. Thorium kept giving off a gas, and when Soddy tested the gas he realised it was not thorium at all. It was a different element. The thorium atoms were turning, all by themselves, into atoms of something else.

Soddy said many years later that he burst out, "Rutherford, this is transmutation!", and that Rutherford answered something like, "For goodness' sake, Soddy, don't call it transmutation. They'll have our heads off as alchemists." We only have Soddy's memory of the exact words, but the worry was real. *Transmutation*, changing one element into another, was the alchemists' word, and serious scientists had spent two hundred years laughing at it.

Yet here it was, happening on their bench.

#### What makes gold gold

To understand the rest of this story you need one fact. What decides which element an atom is? Not its colour, not its weight, not its electrons. It is the number of **protons** in its nucleus.

:::work
Gold: 79 protons. Mercury: 80 protons. Lead: 82 protons.
To turn mercury into gold, the proton number must decrease from 80 to 79. A nuclear process can remove a proton or convert one into a neutron; the later electron-capture example does the latter.
:::

Chemistry cannot do this, because chemistry only rearranges the electrons on the outside. To change the proton number you have to break into the nucleus, which is tiny, extremely tightly held together, and guarded by a powerful electric push against anything with a charge.

#### The first deliberate transmutation

:::scene Manchester, 1919

Radioactive atoms transmute themselves. Could a person make it happen on purpose? In 1919 Rutherford fired fast alpha particles, from a radioactive source, through nitrogen gas. Once in a long while an alpha particle hit a nitrogen nucleus squarely and stuck, and a proton flew out. The nitrogen nucleus became an oxygen nucleus. For the first time, a human being had deliberately turned one element into another. It was not gold, and it was only a handful of atoms, but the door was open.

#### Gold from mercury, 1941

Then came the neutron (found in 1932), a particle with no electric charge. Because it has no charge, nothing pushes it away, and it can slip into a nucleus easily. In 1941, at Harvard, the physicists Rubby Sherr, Kenneth Bainbridge and Herbert Anderson fired neutrons at mercury and made gold. There was a catch: the gold atoms they made were radioactive kinds that broke down within days. Nobody could have worn it as a ring.

In 1980 the American chemist Glenn Seaborg, who discovered several of the heaviest elements, used a particle accelerator in California to knock protons and neutrons off bismuth (83 protons) and leave gold behind. He made only a tiny number of atoms, and the electricity and machinery cost vastly more than the gold was worth.

#### The biggest machine in the world

:::scene CERN, near Geneva, 2025

The Large Hadron Collider is a ring 27 kilometres round, buried under the border between France and Switzerland. It smashes together nuclei of lead travelling at almost the speed of light. In May 2025 the scientists of its ALICE experiment announced something surprising (published in the journal *Physical Review C*).

Sometimes two lead nuclei do not hit each other at all. They whizz past very close. Each carries such an intense electric field that, as they pass, a burst of light energy can knock a few particles out of the other nucleus. If exactly three protons are knocked out, lead (82) becomes gold (79).

The ALICE team worked out that in the years 2015 to 2018 the collider had made about 86 billion gold nuclei this way. That sounds like a lot. Add them up, though, and they weigh about 29 picograms: 29 millionths of a millionth of a gram. A single grain of rice is roughly 700 million times heavier. And each gold nucleus lasted only a tiny fraction of a second before it hit the machine's walls and broke apart.

#### A claim from a fusion company

Now we come to the news. A company in the United States called Marathon Fusion works on nuclear fusion, the process that powers the Sun. In 2025 its scientists published a proposal based on computer calculations. Future fusion power plants will release enormous numbers of fast neutrons. If those neutrons struck a kind of mercury called mercury-198, they could knock a neutron out, leaving mercury-197. Mercury-197 is unstable. Over a couple of days its nucleus captures one of its own inner electrons; inside the nucleus that electron and a proton turn into a neutron, so 80 protons become 79. The result is gold-197, the ordinary, stable gold of rings and coins. Their calculations suggested a big fusion plant might make a few tonnes of gold a year as a by-product. That was a model on a computer, not a working machine.

On 21 October 2026 the company announced online that it had actually made gold from mercury, beating CERN's record "by a factor of 10". As this book was written, that claim had **not** been checked by other scientists. That does not mean it is false. It means we do not know yet.

:::careful
> "If a company says it did something, it did it."

In science a claim becomes knowledge only when others can check it. Scientists would want to see a detailed paper, reviewed by experts who did not do the work, showing exactly how much gold was made and how it was measured, and ideally an independent laboratory confirming it. Until then, a sensible scientist says: "Interesting, and unconfirmed."
:::

#### The real treasure

Isaac Newton spent years of secret work trying to transmute metals. He would have been astonished to learn that it can be done, and perhaps more astonished to learn why it was impossible for him: the change happens deep in the nucleus, at energies no furnace could reach.

Even if fusion gold becomes real, the gold is not the most precious thing in this story. Rutherford and Soddy learned what an element *is*. That understanding gave us nuclear medicine, carbon dating, smoke detectors and power from the atom. The alchemists were searching for gold. What they and their successors actually found was knowledge, and that turned out to be worth far more.
