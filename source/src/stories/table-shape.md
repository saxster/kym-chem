:::scene A classroom wall in Mumbai, any school day

Look at the periodic table on any classroom wall and it is a very odd shape for a chart. It is not a neat rectangle. There are two tall towers on the left, a lower block sunk into the middle, a set of six columns on the right with one lonely box perched at the top corner, and then, strangest of all, two long rows floating underneath the whole thing, as if someone had cut them out and forgotten to glue them back.

None of that is decoration. Every bump and gap is a picture of something real: the way electrons arrange themselves around a nucleus. Once you can read the shape, you can say a surprising amount about any element without having memorised anything.

#### Step 1: shells have rooms inside them

In Chapter 1 you met electron **shells**: groups of electrons at roughly the same energy, the first holding up to 2, the second up to 8. By the 1920s physicists, Niels Bohr among them, had found that each shell is itself divided into smaller groups, called ==sub-shells==. They are named with letters left over from the early study of spectra: **s**, **p**, **d** and **f**.

Think of a shell as a floor of a hotel, and the sub-shells as the rooms on that floor. The rooms come in fixed sizes:

- an **s** room has 2 seats
- a **p** room has 6 seats
- a **d** room has 10 seats
- an **f** room has 14 seats

Here is what those rooms actually look like. Each "seat pair" is a cloud where an electron is likely to be found: s rooms are round, p rooms are dumbbells pointing three ways.

:::live orbitals {}

The first floor has only an s room (2 seats). The second floor has an s room and a p room (2 + 6 = 8). The third floor has s, p and d (2 + 6 + 10 = 18). The fourth has all four kinds (2 + 6 + 10 + 14 = 32). Electrons fill the cheapest seats first, the ones with the lowest energy, just as water fills the bottom of a jug first.

#### Step 2: the order of filling builds the rows

Here is the twist that gives the table its shape. The rooms are not filled strictly floor by floor. The 3d room, on the third floor, turns out to be slightly *more* expensive than the 4s room on the floor above, so electrons fill 4s first and only then go back to 3d. (That simple rule is the one that builds the table, but the full story is subtler: once electrons are actually sitting in 3d, the 3d room ends up slightly *lower* than 4s, which is why an iron atom loses its 4s electrons first when it becomes an ion. You will meet that later.) In the same way, the 4f room waits until after 6s.

Each new row of the table begins when electrons start a new s room. Count what gets filled before the next s room opens, and you get the length of each row:

:::work
Row 1: 1s = **2**
Row 2: 2s, 2p = 2 + 6 = **8**
Row 3: 3s, 3p = 2 + 6 = **8** (the 3d room waits)
Row 4: 4s, 3d, 4p = 2 + 10 + 6 = **18**
Row 5: 5s, 4d, 5p = 2 + 10 + 6 = **18**
Row 6: 6s, 4f, 5d, 6p = 2 + 14 + 10 + 6 = **32**
Row 7: 7s, 5f, 6d, 7p = 2 + 14 + 10 + 6 = **32**
:::

That is the famous sequence 2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32, 32, and it adds up to 118, exactly the number of elements known today. The rows are not long or short by accident. They are as long as the rooms that get filled.

A group of electron places within a shell. There are four kinds, called s, p, d and f, holding 2, 6, 10 and 14 electrons.
:::

#### Step 3: the four blocks

Now the shape makes sense. Colour each element by the kind of room its newest electron went into, and the table splits into four regions, called blocks:

- the **s-block**: the two tall towers on the left (2 columns, because s rooms have 2 seats), plus helium
- the **p-block**: the six columns on the right (p rooms have 6 seats)
- the **d-block**: the ten columns sunk into the middle, starting only in row 4, because that is when the first d room fills
- the **f-block**: fourteen elements in each of rows 6 and 7

And the floating strip? If you put the f-block where it really belongs, inside rows 6 and 7, the table would be 32 columns wide: so long and thin that it would not fit on a classroom wall, and every box would be tiny. So printers cut the f-block out and lay it underneath. It is a convenience, not a separate kind of table. Some books do print the long, 32-column version, and it is worth looking at once: it shows the true shape, without the cut.

#### Step 4: reading the position

This is where the shape pays off. An element's position tells you how its outermost electrons are arranged, and those outer electrons decide most of its chemistry.

Take sulfur. It is in row 3, so its outer electrons are on the third floor. It is the fourth column of the p-block, so it has 2 electrons in its 3s room and 4 in its 3p room: 6 outer electrons in all. Oxygen sits directly above it, also with 6 outer electrons, and that is why sulfur and oxygen behave like cousins.

A printed box usually carries four things: the **atomic number** (the proton count), the **symbol**, the **name**, and the **relative atomic mass** (an average over the element's isotopes, which is why it is rarely a whole number). The atomic number fixes the position, the position tells you the outer electrons, and the outer electrons tell you the chemistry.

:::careful
> "The table is just a list of elements sorted by weight."

It is sorted by atomic number, the number of protons, which is why tellurium (heavier) comes before iodine (lighter). And it is not just a list: it is folded. Each fold, each new row, happens where a new shell of electrons begins. The columns line up elements whose outer electrons match.
:::

:::careful
> "Elements next to each other in a row are similar."

Usually the opposite. Neighbours in a row can be wildly different: sodium is a soft metal that fizzes in water, while chlorine, a few places along the same row, is a poisonous green gas. It is the *columns* that hold families. The exception is the f-block, where the newest electrons tuck into a deep inner room and barely change how the outside of the atom looks, so the fifteen elements from lanthanum to lutetium are so alike that chemists struggled for a century to tell them apart.
:::

#### A few honest wrinkles

The filling order is a very good rule, not a perfect law. A handful of elements bend it slightly; chromium and copper are the famous ones, each moving one electron from the 4s room into 3d. You do not need the details. What matters is that the rule explains the shape of the whole table, and the exceptions are small.

And there are questions about the table that chemists still argue about today.

- **Where does hydrogen go?** It has one electron, like lithium and sodium, so it usually sits on top of the first column. But it is a gas, not a metal, and in some ways it acts like the halogens, which are one electron short of a full shell. Some tables float it above the middle, belonging to no family.
- **Where does helium go?** Chemically it is a noble gas, so it sits above neon. But its two electrons fill an s room, which would put it above beryllium.
- **Which elements belong in group 3?** Under scandium and yttrium, some tables put lanthanum and actinium, and others put lutetium and lawrencium. A task group set up by IUPAC, the international body that looks after chemical names, published a progress report in 2021. It leaned towards lutetium and lawrencium as a possible compromise but did not settle the matter, and tables still differ.

:::scene Beauvais, France, the late 1920s

Some people have redrawn the table to show the electron rooms more honestly. Charles Janet, a French engineer and naturalist, drew a "left-step" table in the late 1920s in which every row ends with a filled s room, so helium sits above beryllium and the blocks line up as perfect steps. In 1964 the chemist Theodor Benfey drew the elements as one continuous spiral, with the d- and f-blocks as bulges. People have also drawn the table as pyramids, flowers and towers. They all hold the same information; the classroom version wins because it is the easiest to print and read.

:::home Build the shape from seats
Take a sheet of squared paper. Starting at the top left, colour 2 squares for row 1 (put the second one far over at the right, where helium goes). For row 2, colour 2 on the left and 6 on the right. Row 3: the same. For row 4, add a block of 10 between them. Row 5: the same. For rows 6 and 7, add 14 more, drawn as a separate strip underneath. Count your squares: you should have 118. You have just drawn the periodic table from nothing but the sizes of the electron rooms.
:::

So the next time you see the table on a wall, read its outline like a map. The towers are s rooms, the sunken middle is d rooms, the right-hand block is p rooms, and the floating strip is the f rooms that would not fit. The shape is the atom's own floor plan.
