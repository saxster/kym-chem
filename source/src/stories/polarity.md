:::scene A kitchen, any day

Pour a spoonful of cooking oil into a glass of water and stir as hard as you like. The moment you stop, the oil gathers into drops and floats to the top. Sugar disappears into water without a trace; oil never does. Water and oil are both clear liquids made of small molecules. Why do they refuse to mix?

The answer lies in a tug-of-war that happens inside every bond, and two scientists, a Dutch physicist and an American chemist, learned how to measure it.

#### An unfair tug-of-war

A bond, as Lewis taught, is a pair of electrons shared between two atoms. But sharing is not always equal. Some atoms pull on shared electrons more strongly than others, because their nucleus has more positive charge acting on the outer electrons, or because those electrons sit closer to it.

When the pull is unequal, the shared electrons spend more of their time near the stronger atom. That end of the bond becomes slightly negative, and the other end slightly positive. Chemists write these "slightly" charges with the Greek letter delta: δ⁻ and δ⁺. A bond like this is called **polar**, because it has two ends, like the poles of a magnet.

#### Debye measures the pull

:::scene Zürich and Utrecht, 1912

In 1912 the Dutch physicist Peter Debye worked out how molecules with a positive end and a negative end, which he called *dipoles* ("two poles"), would behave between electrically charged plates. They would tend to turn and line up, and that would change how much electric charge the plates could store. By measuring that change for different gases and liquids, and how it varied with temperature, he could calculate how strongly each molecule was polarised.

His method gave numbers. Water's dipole is large. Carbon dioxide's is zero, even though each C=O bond in it is polar: the molecule is straight, so the two pulls point in opposite directions and cancel. Methane's is zero too, because its four bonds point to the corners of a tetrahedron and cancel perfectly. The unit chemists use for dipoles is named after him: the debye. Water's is about 1.85 debye. Debye won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1936.

#### Pauling's scale

:::scene Pasadena, California, 1932

Debye had measured whole molecules. Linus Pauling wanted a number for each *atom*: how hard does it pull shared electrons? In 1932 he worked one out by comparing bond energies. If a bond between two different atoms is stronger than you would expect from the average of the two similar bonds, the extra strength comes from the unequal sharing. From those differences he built a scale he called **electronegativity**.

:::work
Some values on Pauling's scale (modern figures):
fluorine 3.98 (the strongest pull of all), oxygen 3.44, nitrogen 3.04, chlorine 3.16,
carbon 2.55, hydrogen 2.20, sodium 0.93.
:::

The bigger the difference between two atoms, the more polar the bond. Oxygen and hydrogen differ by 1.24, so O–H bonds are strongly polar. Carbon and hydrogen differ by only 0.35, so C–H bonds are nearly even. Sodium and chlorine differ by more than 2, so much that sodium's electron goes over to chlorine almost completely: that is an ionic bond. Polar bonds sit in the middle of a smooth range from perfectly even sharing to complete transfer.

#### Polar molecules, step by step

1. **Look at each bond.** Is one atom much more electronegative than the other? Then the bond is polar, with δ⁻ on the stronger-pulling atom.
2. **Look at the shape.** Draw an arrow along each polar bond, pointing to the δ⁻ end.
3. **Add up the arrows.** If they cancel (as in straight CO₂ or tetrahedral CH₄), the molecule is non-polar overall. If they do not cancel (as in bent H₂O or pyramid-shaped NH₃), the molecule is polar: it has a positive side and a negative side.

:::fig c2_dipoles() | Bond pulls in two molecules. In straight CO₂ they cancel; in bent H₂O they add up, so water has a negative side (the oxygen) and a positive side (the hydrogens).

#### Back to oil and water

Water molecules are strongly polar. The positive hydrogen side of one molecule is attracted to the negative oxygen side of its neighbours, so water molecules cling tightly to each other. Oil molecules are long chains of carbon and hydrogen, with nearly even C–H bonds: almost non-polar. When you stir oil into water, the water molecules simply hold on to each other and squeeze the oil out. "Like dissolves like": polar dissolves polar, non-polar dissolves non-polar.

:::careful
> "You can prove water is polar by holding a charged comb next to a thin stream from the tap: the stream bends."

The stream does bend, and it is a lovely thing to watch. But careful experiments (published in 2000) showed the bending comes mostly from electric charge that flows onto the stream from the tap, not from the water molecules turning. The real evidence that water is polar comes from measurements like Debye's.
:::

:::home Oil, water and soap
Half fill a clear jar with water, add a few spoonfuls of cooking oil and a drop of food colouring, put the lid on tightly and shake. Watch the oil separate again. Now add a squirt of washing-up liquid and shake once more. Soap molecules have a polar head, which sits happily in water, and a long non-polar tail, which sits happily in oil. They wrap around tiny oil droplets and let them spread through the water. That is how soap washes grease off your plate.
:::
