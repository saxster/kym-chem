:::scene Birstall, Yorkshire, 1733

Joseph Priestley was the son of a cloth-maker in the north of England. His mother died when he was six, and he was brought up partly by an aunt. He was a sickly boy with a bad stammer, and he could not go to Oxford or Cambridge, because those universities only admitted members of the Church of England, and his family were "Dissenters", Protestants who would not join it.

So Priestley taught himself. By his twenties he could read Latin, Greek, French, Italian, German and Hebrew, and he became a minister and a teacher. He wrote about grammar, history, religion, politics, electricity and education. Science was, at first, something he did in his spare time.

#### The brewery next door

:::scene Leeds, 1767

In Leeds, Priestley lived next to a brewery. Above the great vats of fermenting beer hung a layer of invisible gas, the same "fixed air" we now call carbon dioxide. It was heavier than air, it put out candles, and mice could not live in it.

Priestley began to play with it. He found that if he held a bowl of water above a vat for a while, some of the gas dissolved in the water, giving it a pleasant sharp, fizzy taste. Later he worked out a neater method, making the gas from chalk and acid and bubbling it through water. In 1772 he published a little pamphlet on how to do it.

He had invented soda water. He thought it might be a cure for scurvy on long sea voyages (it was not), and he never made money from it. A few years later a jeweller called Johann Jacob Schweppe built a business on carbonated water, and the name Schweppes is still on bottles today.

#### A gas that made a candle blaze

Priestley became fascinated by "airs", as gases were then called. He discovered or studied around ten new ones, among them the gas we call ammonia and the one that later became laughing gas. He made great use of a clever trick: collecting gases over mercury instead of water, so that gases that dissolve in water could be caught too.

:::scene Calne, Wiltshire, 1 August 1774

Using a large lens, Priestley focused sunlight onto a red powder, the "calx" of mercury. A gas came out. He put a candle in it, and the candle burned with an astonishingly bright flame. He put a mouse in a sealed jar of it, and the mouse lived much longer than it would have in the same amount of ordinary air. He even breathed a little of it himself and wrote that his chest felt "peculiarly light and easy" for some time afterwards.

He had found oxygen. But he did not call it that. Priestley believed in the old theory of phlogiston: the idea that things burn by giving off an invisible substance. He reasoned that this new air must contain no phlogiston at all, so it could soak up a great deal of it, and things burned fiercely in it. He called it "dephlogisticated air".

Later that year, in Paris, he described his experiment over dinner to Antoine Lavoisier. Lavoisier repeated it, measured everything, and realised what Priestley had missed: burning is not losing phlogiston but joining with this gas. Lavoisier named it oxygen. Priestley never accepted the new explanation, defending phlogiston for the rest of his life.

#### Mint and a mouse

Priestley also made a beautiful discovery about plants. He found that if a candle burned out in a closed jar, the air in it was "injured": a mouse could not live in it. But if he put a sprig of mint in that jar and waited some days, the air was "restored", and a candle could burn in it again. Plants, he realised, repair the air that burning and breathing use up. Later scientists, especially the Dutch doctor Jan Ingenhousz, showed that plants only do this in light. Priestley had glimpsed photosynthesis.

#### The riots

Priestley was an honest, kind and stubborn man, and his opinions made enemies. He supported the American and French revolutions and argued for religious freedom and for people's right to disagree with the government.

:::scene Birmingham, 14 July 1791

On the second anniversary of the storming of the Bastille, a mob in Birmingham, stirred up partly by Priestley's political enemies, attacked the homes and chapels of Dissenters. Priestley's house was burned, together with his laboratory, his library and years of notes. He and his family escaped, warned just in time.

He never felt safe in England again. In 1794, aged sixty-one, he sailed to America and settled in Northumberland, Pennsylvania, where he built a new laboratory and kept experimenting until he died in 1804. He knew some of the American founders, including Thomas Jefferson and Benjamin Franklin.

:::home Watch fizz being made
Priestley's "fixed air" is carbon dioxide, and you can make some in the kitchen. Put a teaspoon of baking soda into a glass and pour in a little vinegar. The bubbles are carbon dioxide. Now light nothing, and taste nothing: just watch, and listen to the fizz. If an adult opens a bottle of soda water beside it, you are seeing the same gas, dissolved under pressure and escaping. That is Priestley's invention, in every fizzy drink on Earth.
:::
