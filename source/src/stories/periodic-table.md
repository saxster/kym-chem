:::scene St Petersburg, February 1869

Dmitri Mendeleev had a problem, and it was a textbook. He was a chemistry professor in St Petersburg, and he was writing a book for his students called *The Principles of Chemistry*. He had finished the chapters on a few familiar elements, and now he had to decide what came next. About 63 elements were known, and they looked like a hopeless jumble: gases and metals, soft and hard, some that burst into flame in water and some that sat quietly for centuries. In what order could you possibly teach them?

Mendeleev was not the first person to wonder. The answer he found, and the daring way he used it, became the most famous chart in all of science. But the story starts fifty years earlier.

#### The first clues: families of three

In 1817, and again in 1829, a German chemist named Johann Döbereiner noticed something odd about certain elements that behave alike. Calcium, strontium and barium are all whitish metals that react with water in similar ways. When Döbereiner looked at their atomic weights, the middle one sat almost exactly halfway between the other two.

:::work
Calcium 40.1 and barium 137.3: halfway between is (40.1 + 137.3) ÷ 2 ≈ 88.7. Strontium is 87.6.
Chlorine 35.5 and iodine 126.9: halfway is about 81.2. Bromine is 79.9.
Lithium 6.9 and potassium 39.1: halfway is about 23.0. Sodium is 23.0.
:::

He called these families *triads*. Was it coincidence? It happened too often for that. Something was ordering the elements, but nobody could see what.

#### The chemist who was laughed at

In 1865 an English chemist, John Newlands, tried something bolder. He lined up all the known elements in order of atomic weight and noticed that similar elements seemed to turn up every eighth place, like the notes of a musical scale: do, re, mi, fa, so, la, ti, *do* again. He called it the Law of Octaves.

When he presented it to the Chemical Society in London in 1866, one member asked him, with a straight face, whether he had ever tried arranging the elements in alphabetical order, since that might give patterns too. The Society refused to publish his paper. Newlands was partly right and partly wrong: his pattern worked for the lighter elements and broke down for the heavier ones, and he left no room for elements nobody had found yet. More than twenty years later the Royal Society gave him a medal for it.

#### The patience game

Now back to Mendeleev. He wrote the name of each element on a card, with its atomic weight and its main properties: what it combined with, and in what proportions. Then, the story goes, he laid the cards out on a table again and again, like the card game called patience (or solitaire), looking for the arrangement where everything fitted.

:::careful
> "Mendeleev saw the whole periodic table in a dream."

This story was told by one of his friends years afterwards, and Mendeleev himself never seems to have described it that way. Historians treat it as a legend. What we do know is that he worked on the problem for weeks, with notes and drafts covered in crossings-out that still survive.
:::

On 17 February 1869, by the Russian calendar of the time (1 March by ours), he wrote out his first table. He put elements in order of atomic weight, but in rows and columns so that families lined up: lithium, sodium and potassium together; fluorine, chlorine, bromine and iodine together. And then he did two bold things that nobody before him had dared.

#### Two bold moves

**First, he left gaps.** Where the next element by weight did not fit the family pattern, Mendeleev did not force it in. He left a blank and said, in effect: *an element belongs here that nobody has found yet.* Below aluminium he left a gap and called the missing element *eka-aluminium* ("one beyond aluminium"). There were other gaps below boron and below silicon.

**Second, he predicted.** From its neighbours in the table, he forecast what each missing element would be like: its atomic weight, how dense it would be, what it would combine with.

:::fig s_mendeleev_gaps() | Part of Mendeleev's idea. Each missing element was surrounded by known neighbours, so he could predict its properties from theirs, the way you might guess a missing puzzle piece from the pieces around it.

#### Nature answers

In 1875 a French chemist, Paul-Émile Lecoq de Boisbaudran, spotted a new violet line in the light from a zinc ore and tracked down a new metal. He named it gallium, after France (Gallia in Latin). It was Mendeleev's eka-aluminium. There was one disagreement: Lecoq measured its density as 4.7, and Mendeleev had predicted about 5.9. Mendeleev wrote to him suggesting that his sample was impure. Lecoq purified it, measured again, and got 5.9. A man in St Petersburg had corrected a measurement on a metal he had never seen.

Then came scandium in 1879, matching eka-boron, and germanium in 1886, matching eka-silicon almost perfectly: Mendeleev had predicted an atomic weight of about 72 and a density of about 5.5; germanium turned out to be 72.6 and about 5.3. After that, chemists everywhere took the table seriously.

#### A whole family nobody expected

Even Mendeleev's table had a surprise coming. In 1894, Lord Rayleigh and William Ramsay found that nitrogen taken from the air was slightly heavier than nitrogen made by chemical reactions. The difference was tiny, but they did not ignore it. Hidden in the air was a new gas that reacted with nothing at all: argon, from the Greek for "lazy". Within four years Ramsay had also found helium on Earth, and then, with Morris Travers, neon, krypton and xenon. They formed a completely new column, the noble gases, and slotted neatly onto the end of every row. The table bent to fit them without breaking: the sign of a good model.

#### The last puzzle: the wrong order

There was one awkward thing. In a few places Mendeleev had to put elements slightly *out* of order of weight to keep the families together. Tellurium (127.6) is heavier than iodine (126.9), but tellurium clearly belongs with oxygen and sulfur, and iodine with chlorine and bromine. So he swapped them and guessed that tellurium's weight had been measured wrongly. It hadn't. The real explanation waited until 1913, when the young physicist Henry Moseley showed that the true order is not by weight at all, but by the number of protons in the nucleus: the atomic number. Tellurium has 52 protons, iodine 53. The swap was right all along.

#### Reading the table, step by step

Here is how to read the chart that grew out of all this.

1. **Each box is one element**, in order of atomic number: hydrogen 1, helium 2, lithium 3, and so on.
2. **A row is a period.** Each new row starts when the atoms begin filling a new shell of electrons. So the rows get longer as you go down.
3. **A column is a family**, called a group. Atoms in the same column have the same number of outer-shell electrons, so they behave alike: lithium, sodium and potassium in the first column; fluorine, chlorine and bromine in the next-to-last; the noble gases at the far right.
4. **Metals are on the left and in the middle; non-metals on the right.** A staircase line separates them.

#### The table today

The table now holds 118 elements. The last four were named in 2016: nihonium, moscovium, tennessine and oganesson. In 1955, scientists at Berkeley named element 101 *mendelevium*, in honour of the man who laid out the cards.

:::home A patience game of your own
Take twelve cards or slips of paper. On each write one of these, with its atomic weight: lithium 6.9, sodium 23.0, potassium 39.1 (soft metals that fizz in water); fluorine 19.0, chlorine 35.5, bromine 79.9 (sharp-smelling, poisonous non-metals); beryllium 9.0, magnesium 24.3, calcium 40.1 (harder metals); oxygen 16.0, sulfur 32.1, selenium 79.0. Shuffle them, then lay them out in order of weight in rows so that each family forms a column. You have just rebuilt a corner of Mendeleev's table.
:::

Every time you look at the periodic table in this book, remember that it began as a set of cards on a professor's desk, and that its greatest strength was the blank spaces where the professor admitted what he did not know.
