:::scene Paris and Stockholm, 1823

Imagine two young chemists, both in their early twenties, working in two different cities. In Paris, Justus von Liebig is studying a silver compound called silver fulminate. It is dangerous stuff: a pinch of it explodes with a sharp crack if you tap it or warm it. (Tiny amounts are what make the bang in snap toys and party crackers.) In Stockholm, in the laboratory of the great Berzelius, Friedrich Wöhler is studying a different silver compound, silver cyanate. It is calm and well behaved: it does not explode at all.

Both chemists did what chemists of the time did with a new substance: they broke it down and weighed what came out, to find exactly which elements it contained and in what proportions. And when their results were published, there was a problem. The two answers were the same. Silver, carbon, nitrogen and oxygen, one atom of each, in exactly the same proportions. Yet one substance exploded and the other did not.

#### Step 1: somebody must be wrong

In the 1820s nearly every chemist believed that a substance's composition decided what it was. If two samples had the same elements in the same proportions, they had to be the same substance. So when Liebig saw Wöhler's result, he assumed Wöhler had made a mistake in his analysis, and he said so. He even repeated the cyanate analysis himself, expecting to find the error.

He found that Wöhler was right. Both analyses were correct. Two different substances, with completely different behaviour, really did contain exactly the same atoms in the same numbers.

#### Step 2: from rivals to friends

The story could have turned into a bitter feud. Instead, when the two young men met a few years later, they discovered they liked each other enormously. They became lifelong friends and worked together for decades, writing hundreds of letters and publishing famous research side by side. It is one of the warmest friendships in the history of science, and it began with each of them thinking the other had got his sums wrong.

There were more puzzles like theirs. Wöhler's urea, made in 1828 from ammonium cyanate, had the same formula as the ammonium cyanate he started with. Two acids from wine-making, tartaric acid and "racemic acid", had the same composition but behaved differently. Something new was going on.

#### Step 3: Berzelius gives it a name

In 1830 Berzelius, who had a gift for naming things, proposed a word for substances with the same composition but different properties: ==isomers==, from the Greek *isos*, "equal", and *meros*, "part". Equal parts, different substances.

But *why* could equal parts make different substances? Nobody yet knew how atoms were arranged inside molecules. The full answer had to wait for the 1850s and 1860s, when chemists worked out that atoms are joined by bonds in a definite order, and then for 1874, when van ’t Hoff and Le Bel showed that molecules have shapes in space.

The answer, when it came, was simple: **same atoms, joined in a different order**. Both contain a little group of three atoms, one carbon, one nitrogen and one oxygen. In cyanate the carbon sits in the middle: O–C–N. In fulminate the nitrogen sits in the middle: C–N–O. That small change in who is holding hands with whom changes everything about how the substance behaves.

:::careful
Silver fulminate is a genuinely dangerous explosive. It is part of chemistry's history, not something to make. Chemists who handle compounds like it work with tiny amounts behind blast shields.
:::

#### Step 4: isomers you can understand with your own eyes

Here are two isomers you met in Chapter 10. Both have the formula C₂H₆O: two carbons, six hydrogens, one oxygen.

- In **ethanol**, the oxygen sits at the end of the chain with a hydrogen attached to it, an –OH group. Ethanol boils at 78.4 °C and mixes with water.
- In **dimethyl ether**, the oxygen sits in the middle, between the two carbons, and every hydrogen is attached to a carbon. It boils at −24.8 °C: it is a gas on any day in Mumbai.

Same atoms, a difference in boiling point of more than 100 °C, just because of where one oxygen sits.

:::fig c10_isomers() | Two pairs of isomers. Butane and isobutane share the formula C₄H₁₀; ethanol and dimethyl ether share C₂H₆O. Different arrangements, different substances.

#### Step 5: how many ways can you arrange the atoms?

Small formulas allow only a few arrangements, but the number grows astonishingly fast as molecules get bigger. Here is how many different alkanes (chains and branches of carbon with hydrogens) exist for each number of carbon atoms:

| Formula | Different isomers |
|---|---|
| CH₄, C₂H₆, C₃H₈ | 1 each |
| C₄H₁₀ | 2 |
| C₅H₁₂ | 3 |
| C₆H₁₄ | 5 |
| C₁₀H₂₂ | 75 |
| C₂₀H₄₂ | 366,319 |

That explosion of possibilities is one reason there are so many millions of carbon compounds. Carbon atoms can be arranged in more ways than anyone could ever list by hand.

:::work
**Finding the isomers of C₄H₁₀ step by step**
Start with the four carbons in a straight row: C–C–C–C. Fill in hydrogens so every carbon has four bonds: that is butane.
Now try a branch: three carbons in a row, with the fourth attached to the middle one. Fill in hydrogens: that is isobutane (2-methylpropane).
Any other drawing you try, such as bending the straight chain, turns out to be one of these two, just turned round. So C₄H₁₀ has exactly 2 isomers.
:::

:::home Build isomers from peas and toothpicks
Use soaked dried peas (or small balls of play dough) for atoms and toothpicks for bonds; ask an adult, as toothpicks are sharp. Make 4 big "carbon" balls and 10 small "hydrogen" balls. Every carbon must end up with exactly four toothpicks, every hydrogen with exactly one. How many genuinely different molecules can you build? (Turning a model round, or bending it, does not count as different: only changing which atom is joined to which does.) You should find exactly two: butane and isobutane. Then try five carbons and twelve hydrogens and see if you can find all three.
:::

Liebig and Wöhler's quarrel that never happened taught chemistry a lesson it has never forgotten: a formula tells you which atoms are present, but only the arrangement tells you what the substance really is.
