:::scene London, summer 1827

The Scottish botanist Robert Brown was studying how plants make seeds. He put pollen grains from a wildflower into a drop of water and looked at them under his microscope. Inside the pollen were much smaller particles, and when they spilled out into the water, they would not keep still. They jittered and wandered, this way and that, never stopping, never settling.

The obvious explanation was that they were alive: tiny swimming creatures, perhaps, or some "vital force" in the pollen. Brown was a careful scientist, so he tested it. He tried pollen that had been dried for a century in a museum. He tried powdered glass, ground-up rock, and even dust from a fragment of the ancient Egyptian Sphinx. Everything that was small enough jiggled in exactly the same restless way. So it could not be life. But then what was it? Brown had no idea. Nobody did, for almost eighty years. The jiggling was named after him: ==Brownian motion==.

(People sometimes say that the Roman poet Lucretius described the same thing two thousand years ago, in dust dancing in a sunbeam. He did describe the dancing dust, and he guessed it was caused by invisible atoms, but the dust in a sunbeam is mostly tossed about by air currents. It is a lovely passage, but it is not quite the same effect.)

:::scene Bern, Switzerland, 1905

In 1905 a 26-year-old clerk in the Swiss patent office, Albert Einstein, published several papers that changed physics. One of them was about Brownian motion, although at first he was not even sure that was the right name for what he was describing.

His idea was this. A grain floating in water is being struck by water molecules from every side, all the time. If molecules were infinitely small and infinitely many, the hits from every side would balance perfectly and the grain would sit still. But if water really is made of separate molecules, then at any instant, just by chance, a few more will hit one side than the other. The grain gets a tiny shove. A moment later, a shove in a different direction. Added up, these random shoves make the grain wander, exactly as Brown saw.

Einstein did more than explain it. He worked out *how far* a grain should wander in a given time, and showed that the answer depends on how big and how numerous the molecules are. In other words: watch the grains carefully enough, and you can count the molecules. A year later the Polish physicist Marian Smoluchowski reached the same conclusion independently.

#### Step 1: why a big grain is pushed around by tiny molecules

Imagine a huge beach ball being pushed about by a crowd of people who cannot see it. Each person who bumps it gives a tiny nudge. If the crowd is enormous and evenly spread, the nudges cancel and the ball barely moves. If the crowd is made of separate people, there will always be moments when more of them happen to push from the left than from the right, and the ball rolls a little to the right.

A grain of about a thousandth of a millimetre is struck by water molecules more than ten million million million times every second. That number is so big that the pushes almost cancel. *Almost.* The tiny leftover imbalance, changing every instant, is what makes the grain dance.

:::scene Paris, 1908 to 1909

Einstein's prediction needed testing, and the French physicist Jean Perrin set out to do it. He made thousands of tiny, identical spheres of gamboge, a yellow resin used in paint, which took months of patient work separating them by size in a centrifuge. Then he watched individual grains under his microscope, marking their positions every 30 seconds on squared paper and joining the dots. The pictures he published look like the scribbles of a tired child: zig-zag after zig-zag, never repeating.

Perrin measured how far the grains wandered, used Einstein's mathematics, and worked out how many molecules there are in a mole. Then he did it again in completely different ways: by how the grains settled into layers in a tall tube, by how they rotated, and more. All the methods gave nearly the same number. It was close to the value we know today, about 6 × 10²³, which Perrin suggested naming after Avogadro.

That agreement settled one of the longest arguments in science. Even the chemist Wilhelm Ostwald, who had spent years insisting atoms were just a convenient idea, admitted around 1909 that the evidence had convinced him. Perrin won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1926.

#### Step 2: what this means for you

Every time you see a box of jiggling particles in this book, remember that it is not just a guess. A botanist saw the effect, a patent clerk explained it, and a physicist measured it so carefully that it revealed the number of molecules in a gulp of water.

:::careful
> "The grains move because they are alive, or because the water is flowing."

Brown ruled out life by trying dead and non-living specks; the motion was the same. And it is not a current: neighbouring grains move in completely different directions at the same moment, which a flowing current could never do.
:::

:::home Watch the dance yourself
You will need help for this one, and a microscope if your school has one. Add a tiny drop of whole milk to a drop of water on a glass slide and look at it at high magnification. The fat droplets in milk are small enough to show Brownian motion: they shiver and wander without stopping. (Without a microscope, you can see a related effect: shine a torch beam across a dark room and watch the dust. That dancing is mostly air currents, as with Lucretius, but it gives you the feeling.)
:::

So the dance that puzzled Brown for the rest of his life turned out to be the most direct evidence there is that the world is made of molecules. You cannot see them, but you can watch them push.
