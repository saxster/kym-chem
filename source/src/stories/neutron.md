:::scene Glasgow, Scotland, 1913

By 1913, chemists studying radioactive substances had a problem. They had found dozens of "new" radioactive substances, far more than there were spaces for in the periodic table. Stranger still, some of them could not be separated from each other by any chemical method at all. One substance from thorium behaved exactly like lead: same reactions, same compounds, same everything. Yet it had a different mass from ordinary lead.

Frederick Soddy, a chemist in Glasgow, decided to stop fighting the facts. Perhaps, he said, these really are the same element. They sit in the *same place* in the periodic table, but their atoms have different masses. He needed a name for atoms like that. At a dinner party at his father-in-law's house, he explained the idea to a family friend, Margaret Todd, a Scottish doctor and writer. She suggested a word from Greek: *isos*, "equal", and *topos*, "place". Same place: **isotope**.

#### Step 1: the puzzle in a simple form

If two atoms are the same element, they have the same number of protons (you will meet that idea properly in Chapter 1). But in 1913 nobody knew about protons as separate particles yet, let alone what else might be in a nucleus. So the question was: what can make two atoms of the same element weigh different amounts?

#### Step 2: sorting atoms by weight

At about the same time, J. J. Thomson in Cambridge sent a beam of charged neon atoms through electric and magnetic fields, and saw *two* traces on his photographic plate instead of one: most of the neon landed at mass 20, but a little landed at 22. Neon, it seemed, was a mixture.

His assistant Francis Aston took up the idea after the First World War and built a much more precise instrument, the first **mass spectrometer** (he called it a mass spectrograph), in 1919. It sorts atoms by mass the way a strong wind sorts a handful of thrown objects: light ones are blown further off course than heavy ones. Aston found isotopes in one element after another. Chlorine, for example, turned out to be about three-quarters chlorine-35 and one-quarter chlorine-37. He won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1922.

#### Step 3: the missing particle

There was still a gap in the explanation. A helium nucleus has twice the charge of a hydrogen nucleus, but about four times the mass. Carbon has six times the charge but twelve times the mass. Something in the nucleus was adding mass without adding charge.

As early as 1920, Rutherford guessed that the nucleus might contain a neutral particle, about as heavy as a proton. But how do you catch something with no charge? Charged particles leave tracks, bend in magnets, and make flashes on screens. A neutral particle does none of that. It slips through everything without a trace.

#### Step 4: the race of 1932

In 1930 two German physicists, Walther Bothe and Herbert Becker, found that the metal beryllium, when hit by alpha particles, gave off a strange, very penetrating radiation. In Paris, Irène Joliot-Curie (Marie Curie's daughter) and her husband Frédéric Joliot showed in January 1932 that this radiation could knock protons out of paraffin wax at high speed. They thought it was a powerful kind of light, gamma rays.

In Cambridge, Rutherford's colleague James Chadwick read their paper and did not believe the explanation. Light hitting a proton should not be able to knock it out that fast. But a *particle* as heavy as the proton could, just as one snooker ball can send another flying. Chadwick worked almost day and night for about two weeks, measuring how the mystery radiation knocked atoms of different masses. In February 1932 he announced it: the radiation was a stream of neutral particles with almost exactly the mass of a proton. The **neutron** had been found. Chadwick won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1935.

#### Step 5: everything fits

Now the puzzle of isotopes has a simple answer:

1. The number of protons decides the element. Chlorine always has 17.
2. The number of neutrons can vary. Chlorine-35 has 17 protons and 18 neutrons; chlorine-37 has 17 protons and 20 neutrons.
3. Neutrons have no charge, so they do not change how the atom behaves chemically (that is mostly about electrons). They only change its mass, and sometimes how stable its nucleus is.

That is why Soddy could not separate his "lead" from ordinary lead: chemically they were the same element, just with different numbers of neutrons.

:::careful
> "Heavier isotopes are a different element."

No: an isotope is the same element with a different number of neutrons. Heavy water, made with the hydrogen isotope deuterium (one proton and one neutron), is still water: it looks the same and freezes and boils at almost the same temperatures. It is about 11% denser, which is why an ice cube of heavy water sinks in ordinary water.
:::

:::home A mixture you can average
Put 3 large spoons and 1 small spoon into a bag. Weigh each kind on a kitchen scale, then weigh all four together and divide by 4. That average is like chlorine's 35.45: a real, useful number, even though not one spoon in the bag has that mass.
:::

Isotopes turned out to be useful everywhere: carbon-14 for dating ancient bones, uranium-235 for nuclear power, and harmless heavy isotopes for tracing where atoms go inside living things.
