:::scene Manchester, 1913

Henry Moseley was 25 when he set up his strangest experiment. He was working in Ernest Rutherford's laboratory in Manchester, the same place where, a few years earlier, alpha particles had bounced back off gold foil and revealed the nucleus. Rutherford's team now knew that every atom had a tiny positive centre. But how much positive charge did each nucleus carry, and did it have anything to do with the periodic table?

A Dutch lawyer who did physics as a hobby, Antonius van den Broek, had made a bold guess: perhaps an element's position in the table, its place number counting from hydrogen, was simply the charge on its nucleus. Hydrogen 1, helium 2, lithium 3. It was a beautiful idea, but nobody had any way to test it. Moseley found one.

#### A toy railway of elements

Moseley built a long glass tube with the air pumped out. Inside it sat a little trolley that could carry samples of different metals, one after another, into the path of a beam of fast electrons. When electrons slam into a metal, the metal gives off X-rays, invisible light with very short waves. Each metal gives off its own particular X-rays, like a bell ringing its own note.

He measured those notes, one element at a time: calcium, titanium, vanadium, chromium, manganese, iron, cobalt, nickel, copper, zinc, and on through dozens more. And when he plotted them in the right way (taking the square root of each X-ray's frequency) the points did not scatter. They climbed in a perfectly even staircase: one equal step from each element to the next.

:::fig s_moseley_staircase() | Moseley's staircase, drawn from the modern values of the strongest X-ray line. Each element's point is one equal step above the one before. The steps are counting something inside the atom: one extra unit of positive charge in the nucleus each time.

#### What the steps were counting

Atomic weights do not go up in equal steps; they jump about. But Moseley's steps were perfectly regular. Something inside every atom increased by exactly one from each element to the next, and the X-rays came from electrons being pulled on by the nucleus. The only sensible conclusion was that the nucleus's positive charge goes up by one each time. Van den Broek's guess was right.

That whole number, the charge on the nucleus, is the **atomic number**. Today we know it is simply the number of protons. It is what makes an element the element it is.

#### Step by step: why this settled everything

1. **The true order.** Mendeleev had arranged elements by weight, but had been forced to put a few elements out of weight order to keep families together, most famously tellurium before iodine. (Argon and potassium, found later, turned out to be the same kind of puzzle.) Moseley's staircase showed these choices were right. Tellurium is 52 and iodine 53, even though tellurium is slightly heavier (127.6 against 126.9). Order by protons, and the puzzles vanish.
2. **Counting the missing.** Because the steps were even, a missing step meant a missing element. Moseley could see exactly where gaps were between aluminium (13) and gold (79): elements 43, 61, 72 and 75 had not been found. All four were found later (hafnium, 72, in 1923; rhenium, 75, in 1925; technetium, 43, and promethium, 61, only after they were made in laboratories).
3. **No room for impostors.** Before Moseley, people sometimes announced "new elements" that were really mixtures. After him, any claim could be tested: what is its atomic number? If there is no empty step for it, it is not new.

:::careful
> "Atomic number and mass number are the same thing."

They are different counts. The atomic number counts protons only, and it decides the element: every carbon atom has 6. The mass number counts protons and neutrons together, so it can be different for different atoms of the same element: carbon-12 and carbon-14 both have atomic number 6.
:::

#### The war

In August 1914, the First World War began. Moseley, by then working in Oxford, could have stayed in his laboratory. He chose to join the army, as a signals officer. On 10 August 1915, at Gallipoli in Turkey, he was killed by a sniper's bullet. He was 27.

Many scientists believed he would soon have won a Nobel Prize. It is often said that the loss of Moseley helped persuade Britain to keep its scientists out of front-line fighting in later wars, though historians debate how much difference it made.

#### Today

Every periodic table in the world, including the one on the cover of this book, is arranged by Moseley's numbers. When you read that an element "is" its number of protons, you are reading the conclusion of an experiment done with a toy-sized railway inside a glass tube, by a young man who had only two more years to live.
