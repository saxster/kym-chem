:::scene Abdera, a port town in northern Greece, about 420 BCE

Our adventure begins nearly two and a half thousand years ago, with a man and a question. The man was Democritus, and he is said to have laughed so much at the foolishness of the world that people called him the laughing philosopher. The question was one you can ask about anything around you: *what happens if you keep cutting it?*

Take a raindrop. Cut it in half (in your imagination) and you have two smaller drops. Cut those, and again, and again. Is there an end to the cutting? Democritus, and his teacher Leucippus before him, thought there must be. Somewhere, they said, you reach pieces so small they cannot be cut any further. They called them *atomos*, Greek for "uncuttable". Nobody could see these pieces, and nobody could test the idea. Aristotle, the most admired thinker of the ancient world, rejected it, and for nearly two thousand years most people agreed with him.

:::story atoms

:::scene Trinity College, Cambridge, the 1690s

Instead, for most of those two thousand years, the people who worked hardest on what things are made of were the alchemists. In workshops from Cairo to Prague they heated, dissolved, distilled and mixed, chasing one great dream: to turn "base" metals such as lead or mercury into gold. They never managed it, but they invented much of the equipment chemists still use, and they kept careful notes on how substances behave.

One of the most devoted alchemists of all is a surprise. Isaac Newton, the Cambridge professor who worked out the laws of motion and gravity, spent years at the furnace of a small laboratory in the garden beside his rooms at Trinity College, and secretly wrote about a million words about alchemy. He published almost none of it. In 1936 a large collection of his private papers was sold at auction at Sotheby's in London, and the economist John Maynard Keynes bought many of them. After reading them, Keynes wrote that Newton was not the first of the age of reason but "the last of the magicians".

:::story alchemy

The alchemists' dream will come back, with a twist, in Chapter 1. It took until the twentieth century to settle the argument beyond doubt, and the people who settled it had to learn two habits first. The first habit is keeping what you see separate from what you think is going on underneath. The second is learning to think with numbers far bigger and far smaller than anything in daily life. This short chapter is about those two habits. You will use them on every page that follows.

## 0.1 What we see and what we think

Look at a glass of water. It is clear and colourless, and it takes the shape of the glass. Those are ==observations==: things you can check. What else could you find out with a balance or a thermometer?

A 100-millilitre glassful has a mass of about 100 grams. Sufficiently pure water freezes near 0 °C and boils near 100 °C at ordinary sea-level pressure (about 1 atm). These numbers describe measurements under stated conditions; they are not results measured by this book.

To compare measurements fairly, record the substance, pressure, method and how precisely the instrument reads. Repeating the test under comparable conditions should give compatible results, allowing for measurement uncertainty.

:::note Observation
Something you can see or measure directly, such as a colour, a mass, a temperature, or a change of state.
:::

Now here is a very different kind of sentence: *water is made of tiny molecules, each one an oxygen atom joined to two hydrogen atoms, H₂O.* Democritus would have loved it. But notice: nobody has ever looked into a glass of water and *seen* that. It is an ==inference==: an explanation worked out from many observations and tested against many more. It is an extremely good inference, supported by dozens of completely different kinds of experiment, which you will meet in this book. But it belongs in a different column from "boils at 100 °C".

:::note Inference
A conclusion reached by reasoning from evidence. In chemistry, our ideas about particles we cannot see are inferences, built into **models**.
:::

:::fig see_vs_infer() | Two columns that a chemist keeps separate. On the left, things anyone can observe about water. On the right, the particle model that explains them. The model is drawn far larger than life and coloured for clarity; the display colours identify atoms rather than represent their appearance.
:::

This is what makes chemistry such a detective game. A detective sees footprints, a broken window and an empty jewellery box: those are observations. "The thief came in through the window" is an inference. A good detective, like a good chemist, keeps the two apart and is always ready to change the inference when a new clue turns up.

Chemists move back and forth between three ways of describing the same thing, all the time. There is the **bench** view: what you see and measure in the lab or the kitchen. There is the **particle** view: the atoms and molecules that we think are doing the work. And there is the **symbol** view: the shorthand we write down, such as H₂O. A big part of learning chemistry is learning to move smoothly between the three, and to remember which one you are in.

:::careful
> "If you looked at water through a powerful enough magnifying glass, you would see the bent shape of the H₂O molecules, like in the diagrams."

You would not, and not only because magnifying glasses are not strong enough. Light itself is the problem. Visible light is a wave, and its waves are between about 400 and 700 nanometres long. A water molecule is about 0.3 nanometres across: more than a thousand times smaller than the shortest wave of visible light. Light simply cannot pick out details that small. We know the shape of a water molecule from other kinds of evidence, which you will meet later. The diagrams are models drawn from that evidence, not photographs.
:::

:::five Observation and inference
? What is it?
An observation is something anyone can see or measure for themselves, such as "water boils at 100 °C". An inference is an explanation reasoned out from observations, such as "water is made of H₂O molecules".
? Why is it called this?
"Observe" comes from Latin *observare*, to watch over. "Infer" comes from Latin *inferre*, to carry in: you carry a conclusion in from the evidence.
? What problem did it solve?
For centuries people argued about things nobody could see, with no way to settle the argument. Keeping the two columns apart shows which claims anyone can check and which still need more evidence.
? Why does it work?
Other observers can repeat a measurement under comparable conditions and check its uncertainty, so it is harder to fool yourself. An inference is kept only while it explains every observation and survives new tests; one stubborn new fact can overturn it.
? How does it help?
It lets you build confident knowledge about things far too small to see, such as atoms, and it tells you what to test next when a clue does not fit.
?? How do we know?
We trust the H₂O inference because many completely different kinds of experiment, from splitting water with electricity to watching specks jiggle under a microscope, all point the same way.
?? What it isn't
An inference is not a wild guess or "just an opinion". The best inferences, like atoms, rest on so much evidence that scientists rely on them every day.
?? Where does it stop working?
The line can blur: even reading a thermometer relies on ideas about how thermometers work. Scientists still keep the two as separate as they can.
:::

:::home One drop, two glasses
You need two clear glasses of still water, one warm (from a hot tap, or mixed by an adult with a little water from the kettle: warm, not scalding) and one with ice water, and food colouring. **Predict first:** if you let one drop fall gently into each glass and do not stir, which will spread faster, or will they be the same? Write your prediction down. Then drop them in together and watch for ten minutes. Write down what you *observe* (colours, shapes, how long it took in each glass). Then write one *inference* about what might be happening, too small to see, that lets the colour spread out without anyone stirring it, and faster in one glass than the other. Keep the two lists separate: that is exactly what scientists do. Visible colour can be carried by circulating water as well as by molecular diffusion. Ask what other process could explain the pattern and what comparison would distinguish them; this home experiment does not isolate diffusion.
:::

:::home The mystery box
Scientists spend their lives working out what is inside things they cannot open. Practise on a box. Ask someone to tape one or two objects to the inside of a shoebox (a cork, a small cup, a ruler laid across: anything, without telling you), drop in a marble, and tape the lid shut. Now tilt the box, roll the marble, listen to it and feel it stop. Draw your best map of what is inside, and write down which observation told you each thing. Only when you have made your best inference may you open the box. You have just done, with a marble, what the scientists in this book did with atoms. One of them, in Chapter 1, will do it by firing tiny particles at a sheet of gold.
:::

## 0.2 Powers of ten: from a flask to an atom

If atoms exist, they must be unimaginably small, and there must be unimaginably many of them. To talk about numbers like that without filling the page with zeros, scientists use ==powers of ten==. 10³ means 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000. 10⁻³ means one thousandth, 0.001. A millimetre is 10⁻³ metres; a nanometre is 10⁻⁹ metres, a billionth of a metre.

:::note Powers of ten
A short way of writing big and small numbers. 10⁶ is a million; 10⁻⁶ is a millionth. Each step of 1 in the little number is a factor of ten.
:::

Take a journey down Figure 0.2. Each step is ten times smaller than the one before. Ten steps take you from something you can hold, past a grain of salt, a red blood cell and a virus, all the way to a single molecule.

:::figwide scale_ladder() | Ten powers of ten. Each mark is ten times smaller than the one to its left. A water molecule, about 3 × 10⁻¹⁰ m across, is as much smaller than a grain of salt as a grain of salt is smaller than a skyscraper 300 metres tall.
:::

Masses are just as extreme. One water molecule has a mass of about 3 × 10⁻²³ grams. Even an extremely sensitive laboratory balance can only detect about a ten-millionth of a gram, 10⁻⁷ g, which is still more than a thousand million million times the mass of one molecule. An ordinary laboratory balance therefore cannot weigh a single water molecule. Mass spectrometry and other specialised methods can determine molecular masses. Chemists solved that problem the way a rice seller does: they count by weighing, using a counting unit made for the job, called the ==mole==. One mole contains about 6.022 × 10²³ specified things. The exact count is 6.022 140 76 × 10²³; ==Avogadro's constant== expresses this count per mole, with the unit mol⁻¹. Relative particle masses give approximately the corresponding molar masses in grams per mole: about 12 g/mol for carbon-12 and 18 g/mol for water. This is a count-to-mass bridge, not a change in molecular size.

:::note Mole
A counting unit, like a dozen, but much bigger: one mole contains about 6.022 × 10²³ things (atoms, molecules, or whatever you say you are counting).
:::

:::note Avogadro's constant
The number of specified things per mole: exactly 6.022 140 76 × 10²³ mol⁻¹. The unit mol⁻¹ means “per mole”. This value has been fixed by definition since 2019.
:::

:::story mole

:::work
**A drop of water.** A typical raindrop-sized drop has a mass of about 0.05 g.

Number of moles = 0.05 g ÷ 18 g per mole ≈ 0.0028 moles.

Number of molecules ≈ 0.0028 × 6.022 × 10²³ ≈ 1.7 × 10²¹.

That is 1,700,000,000,000,000,000,000 molecules in one drop.
:::

When you have twice as many moles, you have twice the mass and twice the number of molecules. What you do *not* get is bigger molecules. A spoonful and a bucketful of water are made of exactly the same molecules; the bucket simply has more of them.

:::home How many molecules in one drop?
You need a medicine dropper (or a straw you can pinch at the top), a 5 mL measuring spoon and a little water. Count how many drops it takes to fill the spoon. A spoonful of 5 mL of water has a mass of about 5 g, so divide 5 g by your number of drops to get the mass of one drop. Then follow the steps in the box above to work out how many molecules are in one of *your* drops. (With a typical dropper it takes about 100 drops, which gives about 0.05 g a drop.)
:::

:::scene Paris, 1908

Back to Democritus. By 1900 most chemists used atoms every day, but some famous scientists in Europe, such as the German chemist Wilhelm Ostwald, still argued that atoms were only a handy way of talking, not real things. The person who changed their minds was Jean Perrin, a French physicist at the Sorbonne in Paris, patient and stubborn in equal measure. He looked through a microscope at tiny grains of resin, each about a thousandth of a millimetre across, floating in water. The grains never kept still. They jiggled and wandered, this way and that, for as long as he watched. The Scottish botanist Robert Brown, peering through his microscope at tiny specks released from pollen grains in water, had noticed this restless motion in 1827, and it is called Brownian motion after him.

:::fig c0_brownian() | Brownian motion. A grain of resin about a thousandth of a millimetre across, floating in water, never stops wandering. Perrin tracked grains like this and recorded their zig-zag paths. The cause is invisible: water molecules striking the grain from every side, a few more on one side than the other at each instant.
:::live brownian

In 1905 a young Albert Einstein, then working as a clerk in the patent office in Bern, Switzerland, had explained the jiggling: each grain is being battered by invisible water molecules from every side, and at each instant a few more hit one side than the other. He even worked out how far a grain should wander, depending on how many molecules there are. Perrin measured the wandering with enormous patience, marking each grain's position again and again, and from it worked out the number of molecules in a mole. Then he measured that number in several other, completely different ways, and the answers agreed. Ostwald and most of the other doubters gave in. Perrin won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1926.

A century later, scientists measured the number again, far more precisely, by counting the atoms in an almost perfect sphere of pure silicon, using X-rays to measure the spacing between them. The results were so good that in 2019 the number was fixed exactly: 6.022 140 76 × 10²³. Democritus's uncuttable pieces had been counted.

:::five Atom
? What is it?
The smallest piece of an element that is still that element. Everything you can touch is built from atoms, joined together in different ways.
? Why is it called this?
From the Greek *atomos*, "uncuttable", the name Leucippus and Democritus gave their imagined smallest pieces. The name turned out to be wrong: in Chapter 1 you will see atoms opened up.
? What problem did it solve?
It explained why substances combine in fixed amounts, and why melting, boiling and dissolving never make matter vanish: the same pieces are only being rearranged.
? Why does it work?
Matter really is made of separate particles, far too small to see, and chemical changes rearrange them instead of creating or destroying them.
? How does it help?
It lets chemists count by weighing, using the mole, and predict how much of each substance a change will need and make.
?? How do we know?
Perrin's measurements of Brownian motion, and several other completely different methods, all gave the same number of molecules in a mole. That agreement convinced even the doubters.
?? What it isn't
Not a tiny coloured ball like the drawings. Atoms have no colour, and as Chapter 1 shows, an atom is mostly empty space.
?? Where does it stop working?
"Uncuttable" fails: atoms have a nucleus and electrons inside, and nuclear processes can even turn one element into another.
:::

## The case of the vanishing water

:::case The case of the vanishing water
**A thought experiment, not a home activity. Do not heat a sealed container.** Imagine a purpose-built laboratory vessel: a student puts 0.90 g of water into a strong, empty 2-litre glass flask, seals it, and weighs it: 250.90 g. She heats the flask to 120 °C until every drop has disappeared. Her friend says: "The water has broken up into hydrogen and oxygen gas, and the flask must be lighter now that the liquid has gone."

1. Weighed while still hot and sealed, the flask is 250.90 g.
2. As the flask cools, droplets of colourless liquid form on the inside of the glass. The liquid boils at 100 °C, just like water.
3. 0.90 g of water is 0.050 mol, which is about 3.0 × 10²² molecules.

What happened to the water? Which clue rules out each half of the friend's claim?
???
The water turned to invisible water vapour: the same H₂O molecules, now spread through the whole flask instead of sitting in a puddle at the bottom. Clue 1 rules out "lighter": nothing left the sealed flask, so the mass did not change. Clue 2 rules out "broken up": the liquid that came back behaves exactly like water, because it is water. A mixture of hydrogen and oxygen would not condense on cooling. The molecules never changed; only their spacing did. Clue 3 reminds you how many molecules were wandering around the flask while you couldn't see them.
:::

So matter is made of separate particles, and we can even count them. Democritus’s idea of literally uncuttable pieces still needs checking. But picturing water as a crowd of tiny balls treats each atom as featureless. It cannot tell us what makes an oxygen atom different from a hydrogen atom, or where electric charge comes from.
