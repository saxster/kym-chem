At the end of Chapter 0, Democritus's uncuttable pieces had been counted. But the people who counted them already knew a secret that would have astonished him: atoms *can* be cut. They have insides.

Here is a puzzle to carry through this chapter. Gold and mercury sit side by side in the periodic table. Gold is a heavy yellow metal that stays bright for thousands of years. Mercury is a silvery liquid at room temperature that runs about like quicksilver, which is its old name. And yet, deep inside, a mercury nucleus has exactly one more proton than a gold nucleus: 80 instead of 79. One tiny particle separates a wedding ring from a puddle of liquid metal. This chapter opens the atom up, counts what is inside, and finds out which of those counts decides what an atom *is*.

## 1.1 Inside an atom

:::scene Cambridge, 1897

The first crack in the uncuttable atom appeared in a glass tube. At the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge, J. J. Thomson, a Manchester bookseller's son who had become head of the laboratory at just 28, pumped nearly all the air out of a tube, sealed a metal plate into each end, and connected them to a high voltage. A ghostly beam streamed from one plate, making the glass glow where it struck. Thomson bent the beam with magnets and with electric charges, and from how much it bent he worked out that it was made of tiny particles with negative charge, far lighter than any atom. It did not matter which metal he used for the plates or which gas was left in the tube: the particles were always the same. So every atom must contain them. These were ==electrons==, and the atom had just been shown to have parts.

Today we describe atoms using three kinds of smaller particle. Ordinary hydrogen-1 has a proton and an electron but no neutron; most other nuclei contain both protons and neutrons. ==Protons== carry a positive electric charge. ==Neutrons== carry no charge at all. Electrons carry a negative charge, exactly as strong as a proton's positive one but opposite in sign. Protons and neutrons are packed together in a tiny, dense centre called the ==nucleus==. Electrons occupy the much larger space around it.

:::note Proton, neutron, electron
The three particle kinds used in this atomic model; a hydrogen-1 nucleus has no neutron. Charges: proton +1, neutron 0, electron −1. A proton or neutron has nearly 2,000 times the mass of an electron.
:::

:::note Nucleus
The tiny centre of an atom, holding its protons and neutrons, and almost all of its mass.
:::

But Thomson did not know about the nucleus. He imagined the electrons dotted through a soft ball of positive charge, like currants in a pudding. Finding out how wrong that picture was took a darkened room in Manchester.

:::scene Manchester, 1909

Picture a small room with the blinds down. Hans Geiger, a German physicist, and Ernest Marsden, a twenty-year-old student, sit in the dark for half an hour until their eyes adjust. In front of them is a source of alpha particles: small, fast, positively charged bullets shot out by radioactive radium. The bullets are aimed at a sheet of gold foil only about a thousand atoms thick. Beyond the foil is a screen that gives off a tiny flash of light each time an alpha particle hits it, and the two men count the flashes through a microscope, one by one.

**Predict first.** Remember the mystery box from Chapter 0: this is the same game, with gold atoms in the box and alpha particles for marbles. If each gold atom really were a soft pudding, with its positive charge spread thinly all through it, what would you expect the fast alpha particles to do when they hit the foil? Decide before you read on.

Their professor, Ernest Rutherford, a farmer's son from New Zealand with a booming voice and a habit of singing hymns, loudly and off-key, as he walked round the lab, expected the bullets to pass through the soft "pudding" atoms of gold with hardly a wobble, and almost all of them did. But about one in several thousand was turned aside by a large angle, and a very few came almost straight back towards the source. Rutherford said it was as surprising as firing a heavy shell at a sheet of tissue paper and having it bounce back at you. He thought about it for nearly two years. In 1911 he gave the only explanation that worked: each gold atom's positive charge and almost all its mass are crammed into a minute centre, with mostly empty space around it. The pudding was gone. The nucleus had arrived. (If you predicted that nearly all would sail straight through, you were right about the many, as Rutherford was. The surprise was in the few: a soft pudding has nothing hard enough to send anything back.)

:::fig gold_foil() | The gold-foil experiment, in outline. Most alpha particles pass straight through the foil. A few are deflected sharply, and a very few bounce back. Only a tiny, heavy, positively charged nucleus can explain those rebounds.
:::live goldfoil

:::story nucleus

So almost all of an atom's mass is in its nucleus, because a proton or a neutron has nearly 2,000 times the mass of an electron. But almost all of an atom's *size* comes from its electrons. If an atom were blown up to the size of a cricket stadium, its nucleus would be about the size of a pea on the pitch, and the rest of the stadium would be the region where its electrons are found.

:::home A grain of salt and a very big atom
Ask an adult to come outside with you to a park or a long, safe pavement. Put one grain of salt on the ground: that is your nucleus. Now walk away from it, counting your steps. For a carbon atom built to this scale, the edge of the atom would be somewhere between about 5 and 15 metres away (roughly seven to twenty big steps), depending on how you measure an atom's edge. Turn round and look back at the grain you can hardly see. Almost all of the atom's mass is in that grain. Everything between you and it is the space where the atom's six electrons are found. That is why most of Geiger and Marsden's alpha particles flew straight through.
:::

Drawing an atom on a page is a problem, then: draw the nucleus big enough to see and the atom would not fit in the room. So chemists use a different kind of picture.

:::fig atom_counting(6, 6, 6, 'carbon-12') | A counting model of a carbon-12 atom: six protons (+) and six neutrons packed into the nucleus, and six electrons (−) in the region around it. The drawing is good for counting. It is badly wrong about size: drawn to scale, the nucleus would be far too small to see on this page.

The electrons in that drawing are dots scattered around the nucleus. They are not on tracks, and the dots are not where real electrons "are" at some moment. Electrons do not behave like tiny planets, and we will improve this part of the picture as the book goes on. For now, this is a ==counting model==: it is drawn to help you count how many of each particle there are, and nothing more.

:::note Counting model
A diagram that shows how many particles of each kind there are. It is not a picture of where they are or how big they are.
:::

:::five The nucleus
? What is it?
The tiny centre of an atom, made of protons and neutrons. It holds almost all of the atom's mass but takes up almost none of its space.
? Why is it called this?
From Latin *nucleus*, "kernel", which comes from a word for a little nut: the hard centre inside a fruit. Rutherford was using the word for the atom's core by 1912.
? What problem did it solve?
Thomson's "plum pudding" atom could not explain why a few alpha particles bounced back from gold foil. Something small, heavy and positively charged had to be there.
? Why does it work?
All of the atom's positive charge is packed into that minute centre, so an alpha particle that comes very close to it feels a fierce push, strong enough to turn it right round. The rest pass through the empty space.
? How does it help?
It explains why atoms are mostly empty space and where their mass comes from, and it gives the picture used for the rest of this book: a nucleus, with electrons around it.
?? How do we know?
Geiger and Marsden counted flashes from alpha particles fired at gold foil in Manchester in 1909. About one in several thousand was turned aside sharply, which only a tiny, heavy, charged centre can explain.
?? What it isn't
Not a sun with electrons going round it like planets. Electrons do not follow tracks at all.
?? Where does it stop working?
A small solid ball is itself only a model. The nucleus has its own inner structure, which takes nuclear physics to describe.
:::

## 1.2 What identifies an element?

An ==element== is a substance made of only one kind of atom. Hydrogen, carbon, oxygen, gold and mercury are all elements, and there are 118 known so far. But what makes two atoms "the same kind"? For a long time nobody could say. Chemists arranged the elements in order of how heavy their atoms were, and a few of them stubbornly seemed to sit in the wrong places.

:::note Element
A substance made of atoms that all have the same number of protons.
:::

:::scene Manchester, 1913

The answer came from one of Rutherford's young researchers, Henry Moseley, who was twenty-five. He fired electrons at targets made of different elements, one after another, and measured the X-rays that came off. Each element gave out its own X-ray "note". When Moseley plotted the square root of each note's frequency, the points followed a nearly even staircase within the same X-ray spectral series, one step per element. The relationship was approximately linear, not perfectly exact. The steps were counting something inside the atom: the positive charge of its nucleus, in other words the number of protons. Moseley's staircase put every element in its correct place, and where a step was missing, it showed exactly where an undiscovered element must fit.

That number is called the ==atomic number==, and it has the symbol *Z*. It is the answer to the question, and it is simple and absolute: two atoms are the same element if, and only if, they have the same number of protons.

:::note Atomic number (*Z*)
The number of protons in an atom's nucleus. It decides which element the atom is: 1 is hydrogen, 6 is carbon, 8 is oxygen, 79 is gold.
:::

Moseley did not live to see what his staircase became. In 1915, during the First World War, he was killed by a sniper at Gallipoli, aged 27. Many scientists have said that his death was one of the war's great losses to science.

:::story atomic-number

Every atom with 6 protons is carbon, whether it is in a pencil, a diamond, a sugar crystal or the carbon dioxide you breathe out. Every atom with 7 protons is nitrogen. Change the number of neutrons, and you still have the same element. Change the number of electrons, and you still have the same element. Change the number of protons, and you have a different element. Add one proton to gold, and you have mercury.

:::fig identity_strip() | Three changes to a carbon atom. Taking away an electron leaves it carbon (with a charge). Adding a neutron leaves it carbon (a heavier version). Adding a proton makes it nitrogen.

So could you turn mercury into gold, the dream of the alchemists and of Isaac Newton at his furnace, by taking one proton away? In the lab app you can do it with one tap. In the real world, that is not something chemistry can do, and this is why every alchemist failed. Chemical changes, like burning or rusting or cooking, move electrons around and rearrange atoms, but they never change the number of protons in a nucleus. However hot the furnace, however clever the recipe, the nucleus is untouched. Changing that number takes a nuclear process, such as the reactions in the core of a star or in a nuclear reactor. So when you tap that button, you are editing a model, not doing an experiment.

Nuclear physics, though, can do it, and in our own time it has. At CERN, the giant laboratory near Geneva, the Large Hadron Collider smashes lead nuclei together at nearly the speed of light. In May 2025 the team running its ALICE experiment reported that when two lead nuclei only *just* miss each other, the electric jolt can knock three protons out of one of them. Lead has 82 protons; take away three and you have 79, which is gold. Over the years 2015 to 2018 the collider made gold nuclei this way, about 29 picograms in all (a picogram is a millionth of a millionth of a gram), and each one lasted only a split second before hitting the machine's walls and breaking apart. Then, in October 2026, a US start-up called Marathon Fusion announced that it had made gold from mercury, using neutrons from fusion reactions: a neutron knocks a neutron out of mercury-198, making mercury-197, which then decays by itself into gold-197. That is a company's claim, and when this book was written its data had not yet been published or checked by other scientists. Either way, notice what did the work. Not a chemical recipe, but a change to the nucleus.

:::story transmutation

:::story newton

:::careful
> "Na⁺ has 10 electrons, the same as neon. So it has turned into neon."

Count the protons instead. A sodium ion still has 11 protons, so it is still sodium, just with a positive charge. Neon has 10 protons. Having the same number of electrons as another element makes two particles similar in some ways, but it never makes them the same element. That is the answer to this chapter's big question.
:::

:::five Element
? What is it?
A substance made of only one kind of atom: atoms that all have the same number of protons. Carbon, oxygen and gold are elements, and 118 are known so far.
? Why is it called this?
From Latin *elementum*, a first principle or basic part. Where the Latin word itself came from, nobody knows.
? What problem did it solve?
For two thousand years people argued about what the basic ingredients of everything were. Elements are the ingredients chemistry cannot split any further, and Moseley's proton count finally gave an exact test of which is which.
? Why does it work?
The number of protons fixes the charge of the nucleus. That fixes how many electrons a neutral atom holds, and the electrons decide how the atom behaves.
? How does it help?
Every substance in the world is made of elements joined in different ways, so a list of 118 ingredients describes everything you will ever touch.
?? How do we know?
Within a given X-ray spectral series, Moseley's 1913 measurements followed nearly equal steps in square-root frequency. They supported ordering by nuclear charge; today we identify that charge with the proton count.
?? What it isn't
Not "an atom with a certain mass" or "an atom with a certain number of electrons". Isotopes and ions change those, and the element stays the same. Only the protons count.
?? Where does it stop working?
Chemistry can never turn one element into another, but nuclear processes can, in stars, in reactors, and in machines like the Large Hadron Collider.
:::

## 1.3 Same element, different isotope

:::scene Cambridge, 1919

Back in Cambridge, J. J. Thomson had noticed something odd as early as 1913. When he sent a beam of charged neon atoms through magnetic and electric fields, it split into two faint streaks instead of one, as if neon came in two weights. His former assistant Francis Aston returned from war work and built a much sharper machine to settle it, one of the first ==mass spectrometers==. It gives atoms an electric charge, then sends them flying through a magnetic field. The field bends heavier particles less than lighter ones, so the beam splits into separate streams, one for each mass. In 1919 Aston confirmed that neon really is a mixture of two kinds of atom, and went on to show the same for chlorine and many other elements.

How can one element come in different weights? The protons cannot differ, or it would be a different element. The extra weight belongs to the neutrons. Atoms of the same element can have different numbers of neutrons. Carbon atoms, for example, can have 6, 7 or 8 neutrons, and all of them are carbon. These versions are called ==isotopes==. We name an isotope by its ==mass number==, the total number of protons and neutrons: carbon-12 has 6 + 6 = 12, carbon-13 has 6 + 7 = 13, and carbon-14 has 6 + 8 = 14.

:::note Isotopes
Atoms of the same element (same number of protons) with different numbers of neutrons.
:::

:::note Mass number (*A*)
The number of protons plus the number of neutrons in one nucleus. It is always a whole number.
:::

Isotopes behave almost identically in chemistry, because chemistry is mostly about electrons, and isotopes of an element have the same number of electrons. But their nuclei can be very different. Carbon-12 and carbon-13 last forever. Carbon-14 is radioactive: its nuclei slowly break down, so that half of any sample is gone after about 5,700 years. That is how archaeologists date ancient wood and bone. Adding a neutron in the app does not tell you whether the new nucleus would be stable; that depends on nuclear physics, not on counting.

Most elements found in nature are a mixture of isotopes. Chlorine, for example, is about three-quarters chlorine-35 and one-quarter chlorine-37. So the ==average atomic mass== of chlorine is somewhere between 35 and 37. Here is the arithmetic, using the real measured fractions:

:::note Average atomic mass
The average mass of an element's atoms in a natural sample, weighted by how common each isotope is. This is the decimal number printed on most periodic tables.
:::

:::work
0.758 × 35.0 + 0.242 × 37.0 ≈ 26.5 + 9.0 = 35.5

(With the isotopes' exact masses, 34.969 and 36.966, the answer is 35.45.)
:::

That is why periodic tables give chlorine as 35.45. But notice something odd: no single chlorine atom has a mass of 35.45. Every chlorine atom is either about 35 or about 37. The average describes the *crowd*, not any member of it, in the same way that the average number of children in a family can be 2.4 even though no family has 0.4 of a child.

:::fig mass_spectrum_cl() | The mass spectrum of chlorine. Each peak is a group of atoms with the same mass; its height shows how common that isotope is. The average, 35.45, falls between the peaks, where there are no atoms at all.

This is exactly the kind of picture Aston's machine drew: one peak for each isotope, with the height of each peak showing how common it is.

:::home An element made of beans
Make your own "element" with two isotopes: mix 75 rajma beans and 25 small brown chickpeas, kala chana (any two clearly different sizes of bean or dal will do; big white chickpeas can weigh about as much as rajma) in a bowl. **Predict first:** will the average mass of a bean in the bowl be nearer a rajma's mass or a chickpea's? A kitchen scale only reads to about a gram, so weigh beans in twenties: weigh 20 rajma and divide by 20; weigh 20 chickpeas and divide by 20. Work out the average just as for chlorine: 0.75 × (one rajma) + 0.25 × (one chickpea). Check it by weighing all 100 and dividing by 100. Then look in the bowl: is any bean an "average bean"? Each one is a rajma or a chickpea, just as each chlorine atom is chlorine-35 or chlorine-37.
:::

:::five Isotope
? What is it?
One of the different versions of the same element. Isotopes have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons, so their atoms have different masses.
? Why is it called this?
From Greek *isos*, "equal", and *topos*, "place": isotopes of an element sit in the same place in the periodic table. The word was suggested to Frederick Soddy in 1913 by Margaret Todd, a Scottish doctor and writer and a friend of his family.
? What problem did it solve?
Thomson's neon beam split in two, and many elements had average masses that were not whole numbers. Isotopes explained both: an element can be a mixture of atoms of different masses.
? Why does it work?
Neutrons add mass but no charge, so they change the weight of the nucleus without changing the electrons. Chemistry is mostly about electrons, so isotopes of an element behave almost the same.
? How does it help?
It explains averages such as chlorine's 35.45, and gives tools such as carbon-14 dating of ancient wood and bone.
?? How do we know?
Aston's mass spectrometer of 1919 split neon, and then chlorine and many other elements, into one stream for each mass.
?? What it isn't
Not a different element, and not necessarily radioactive. Carbon-12 and carbon-13 last forever; only some isotopes, such as carbon-14, break down.
?? Where does it stop working?
"Almost the same" is only almost. Hydrogen is the big exception: its heavy isotope, deuterium, doubles the atom's mass, and "heavy water" made from it freezes at about 3.8 °C instead of 0 °C.
:::

## 1.4 Where does charge come from?

Try a little magic before the explanation.

:::home The balloon that sorts salt from pepper
On a dry day, sprinkle a teaspoon of salt and a pinch of ground black pepper on a plate and mix them. Blow up a balloon, tie it, and rub it hard on your hair or a wool jumper for ten seconds. Now hold the balloon a couple of centimetres above the plate and slowly lower it. The pepper jumps up and sticks to the balloon first; bring it lower and some salt follows. Rubbing moved electrons from your hair onto the balloon, giving it a negative charge, and that charge pulls on the specks below. The lighter pepper flakes are lifted first. Now rub a second balloon, hang both from threads so that they dangle side by side, and let them go: they swing *apart*. Both carry the same kind of charge, and like charges push each other away. Only electrons moved in all this. Your hair, having lost some, is left with a positive charge, which is why it may lift towards the balloon.
:::

Nothing was created when you rubbed that balloon. Electrons were simply moved from one place to another. Each proton has a charge of +1 and each electron a charge of −1, measured in a unit called the ==elementary charge==. Neutrons have no charge. So the overall charge of any particle is just a subtraction:

:::eq
**charge = number of protons − number of electrons**
:::

:::note Elementary charge
The size of the charge on one proton (+1) or one electron (−1). It is the smallest amount of charge any free particle has.
:::

A sodium atom has 11 protons and 11 electrons, so its charge is 11 − 11 = 0: it is neutral. If it loses one electron, it has 11 protons and 10 electrons, and its charge is +1. A particle with an overall charge is called an ==ion==. This one is written Na⁺, "sodium plus". An oxygen atom with 8 protons that has gained two extra electrons has 8 − 10 = −2, and is written O²⁻.

:::note Ion
An atom or group of atoms with an overall electric charge, because its numbers of protons and electrons are not equal.
:::

:::fig charge_tally() | Charge is a count. A sodium atom (left) has 11 positive and 11 negative charges, which cancel. A sodium ion (right) has one electron fewer, so one positive charge is left over. The nucleus has not changed at all.

Notice what does not change when an ion forms: the nucleus. The protons and neutrons stay exactly where they were. That is why an ion is still the same element.

:::scene London, the 1830s

The word "ion" is older than the electron. At the Royal Institution in London, Michael Faraday, who had started life as a bookbinder's apprentice, was passing electric currents through all sorts of substances. Solid salt would not carry a current. But when salt was melted, or dissolved in water, a current flowed through it, and new substances appeared at the metal plates where the current went in and out. Something in the liquid was carrying charge from one plate to the other. In 1834 Faraday named these charge-carriers *ions*, from the Greek for "going". Sixty years before anyone knew about electrons, he had found their footprints. We now know that in salt water they are Na⁺ ions drifting one way and Cl⁻ ions drifting the other.

Arithmetic lets you write down any charge you like: carbon with 5 electrons, oxygen with 12. But being able to write a particle down does not mean it can exist for long in the real world. Whether an ion survives depends on its surroundings. Na⁺ ions last indefinitely in salt crystals and in seawater; a C⁺ ion can be made in a vacuum chamber but would not last a moment in water. The subtraction tells you the charge. It does not tell you whether the particle is real.

:::careful
> "The charge of an atom is the number of protons minus the number of neutrons."

Neutrons have no charge, so they cannot affect the subtraction at all. They change the mass of an atom, not its charge. Charge is always protons minus *electrons*.
:::

:::five Ion
? What is it?
An atom, or a group of atoms, with an electric charge, because it has more or fewer electrons than protons. Na⁺ has lost one electron; O²⁻ has gained two.
? Why is it called this?
From the Greek for "going". Michael Faraday named ions in 1834, on the suggestion of his friend William Whewell, because they travel through a liquid towards the plates where a current goes in and out.
? What problem did it solve?
Faraday could not explain how melted or dissolved salt carried an electric current when solid salt could not. Charged particles moving through the liquid were the answer.
? Why does it work?
Charge is a simple count: protons minus electrons. Move electrons on or off and the count changes, while the nucleus, and so the element, stays exactly the same.
? How does it help?
It explains why salt water carries electricity, why salt is safe to eat although sodium and chlorine are dangerous, and much of what happens inside batteries.
?? How do we know?
Pass a current through melted or dissolved salt and new substances appear at each plate, just as moving charged particles would make them.
?? What it isn't
Not a different element. Na⁺ still has 11 protons, so it is still sodium, even though it has the same number of electrons as neon.
?? Where does it stop working?
The arithmetic can describe ions that cannot last. A C⁺ ion can be made in a vacuum chamber, but it would not survive a moment in water.
:::

## 1.5 Reading an atom's identity

You have now met everything the explorers of this chapter found: Thomson's electrons, Rutherford's nucleus, Moseley's proton count, Aston's isotopes, Faraday's ions. Chemists have a compact way of writing all of it down at once. Take the element's symbol, for example O for oxygen, and put three numbers around it, each in its own corner.

:::fig nuclide_notation() | The three corners of a nuclear symbol. Bottom left: protons. Top left: protons plus neutrons. Top right: the charge. This particle has 8 protons, 9 neutrons and 10 electrons.

The bottom-left number is the atomic number, *Z*. It is the number of protons, and it is really the same information as the symbol: an O always has 8. The top-left number is the mass number, *A*, protons plus neutrons. The top-right number is the charge, protons minus electrons, with its sign written after the number. Leave it off when the charge is zero.

So [[nuc:17:8:O:2−]] means: 8 protons; 17 − 8 = 9 neutrons; and a charge of −2, so 8 + 2 = 10 electrons.

To find the neutrons, subtract the bottom number from the top: *N* = *A* − *Z*. To find the electrons, start from the protons and use the charge: a charge of 2− means two more electrons than protons; a charge of 3+ means three fewer.

The positions of these numbers are an international agreement, kept up to date by the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC), so that a chemist in Mumbai, Moscow or Montreal reads the symbol in exactly the same way. Getting the corners mixed up is one of the commonest mistakes in chemistry exams, so the labels in Figure 1.5 are worth learning.

:::careful
> "The periodic table says oxygen is 15.999, so the top-left number for oxygen should be 15.999."

These are two different kinds of number. The top-left number in a nuclear symbol describes *one* nucleus, and it is always a whole number because you cannot have part of a proton or neutron. The 15.999 on the periodic table is the average atomic mass for natural oxygen: an average over a mixture of isotopes, measured in units in which the individual masses are not quite whole numbers either.
:::

:::

An atom has *Z* = 7 and *A* = 15. How many neutrons does it have, and which element is it?
???
15 − 7 = 8 neutrons. With 7 protons it is nitrogen: this is nitrogen-15, a rare but stable isotope.

## 1.6 What might explain patterns?

:::scene London, 1807

Go back a century, to the basement laboratory of the Royal Institution, where Faraday would later work. Humphry Davy, a woodcarver's son from Penzance in Cornwall who had become the most famous lecturer in London, passed a powerful electric current through a lump of slightly damp potash. Tiny silvery globules appeared, like drops of mercury; some burst into flame as they broke through the crust. Davy had discovered potassium. His cousin Edmund, who was helping him, later recalled that Davy danced about the room with delight. A few days later he made sodium the same way.

Lithium, sodium and potassium are soft, shiny metals that react with water (sodium and potassium fiercely) and are stored under oil. Chemists noticed long ago that they belong together as a family, and that the same family pattern repeats across the elements. That repeating pattern is what Dmitri Mendeleev, a chemist born in Siberia and teaching in St Petersburg, built his periodic table around in 1869. Something about these atoms must be alike. Their nuclei are very different (3, 11 and 19 protons), so it must be something about their electrons.

:::story periodic-table

The clue comes from measuring how much energy it takes to pull one electron off an atom. This is called the ==ionisation energy==. Figure 1.6 shows it for the first 18 elements.

:::note Ionisation energy
The energy needed to pull one electron completely away from an atom. A high value means the electron is held tightly.
:::

:::figwide ionisation_plot() | First ionisation energies of elements 1 to 18. The tallest peaks are helium, neon and argon, whose electrons are held most tightly. Straight after each peak comes a deep drop: lithium, then sodium, which lose one electron easily. The pattern repeats.

The pattern is not a smooth climb. It rises, crashes, rises again, crashes again. The best explanation is that electrons are arranged in groups, called ==shells==, at different energies. The first shell holds up to 2 electrons; the next holds up to 8; for the first 18 elements, the third also fills at 8. Lithium has 2 electrons in the first shell and 1 in the second (written 2, 1). Sodium has 2, 8, 1. Both have a single electron in their outermost shell, farther from the nucleus and more shielded from its pull by the electrons inside it. That lone outer electron is easy to remove, and it explains why the two metals behave so much alike.

:::note Shell
A group of electrons with similar energy in an atom. Shells are energy levels, not circular tracks.
:::

:::story table-shape

:::fig shells_li_na() | Shell counts for lithium (2, 1) and sodium (2, 8, 1). Both have one electron in their outermost shell. The rings show groupings by energy, not paths that electrons travel along.

**Predict first.** The rings in the figure above look like tracks, with electrons running round them like trains on a circle line. If you could photograph an electron in a neon atom again and again, would the dots all land on a neat circle?

They would not. Electrons don't follow paths at all. What we *can* know is where an electron is likely to be found, and that likelihood makes a cloud: thick in some places, thin in others, fading away with no edge. Each shell is made of one or more of these clouds, and **each cloud holds at most two electrons**. The first shell is a single round cloud (called 1s). The second shell is a bigger round cloud (2s) plus three dumbbell-shaped clouds (2p) at right angles to each other: 1 + 3 = 4 clouds × 2 electrons = 8, the second shell's eight. Turn the atom below and see for yourself.

:::fig i_orbitals() | Two ways to picture an atom's electrons. Left: shells as rings, a handy way to count. Right: hydrogen-like mathematical probability-density models: 1s, 2s (with its hollow layer) and one of the three 2p clouds. Each cloud holds at most two electrons. Dots sample mathematical densities; they are not electrons or measured positions. These one-electron shapes are not a calculation of neon.
:::live orbitals {}

The rings are not wrong: they are a **model** that keeps the counting right and leaves out the shapes. You will meet the clouds again in the story of why the periodic table has its strange shape: the 2p clouds are why the second row has six columns on the right, and the five d clouds are why the middle of the table is ten columns wide.


What, then, makes a full shell special? You will often hear the answer "atoms want a full shell". It is time to look at that word properly.

:::careful
> "Sodium wants to lose its electron to get a full shell, and chlorine wants to gain one."

**The word.** "Want" is shorthand. Scientists use it when talking quickly, and your school book may say that atoms gain, lose or share electrons "to get a full outer shell". It is a handy way to predict what happens. But an atom has no wishes, so the word hides the real reason. Here is the reason.

**The real principle.** Put a marble in a bowl and it ends up at the bottom and stays there. Not because it wants to be at the bottom, but because getting it out again costs energy that something has to supply. On its way down it gave energy away, as a little heat, which spread out into the bowl and the air. Lower-energy arrangements can be favoured in a specified comparison, but whether a change happens also depends on conditions and an accessible pathway. A material can last because rearrangement is slow, as we will revisit with diamond. The marble picture illustrates an energy well; it does not prove that everything that persists is the lowest-energy possibility. And once the energy has gone out as heat, it spreads through the surroundings and does not come back by itself, for the same reason that a shuffled pack of cards never sorts itself back into order: there are overwhelmingly more spread-out ways than gathered-up ones.

**The sodium paradox.** If sodium really "wanted" to lose its electron, taking it off would give out energy. It does the opposite. Look at the graph: removing sodium's outer electron *costs* about 496 kJ per mole. When chlorine catches that electron it gives back only about 349 kJ. So the swap on its own is uphill: the pair ends up 147 kJ worse off, and if nothing else happened, salt would never form. What makes the whole journey run downhill is what comes next: the new ions pull together into a crystal and release about 787 kJ per mole. You will see that energy account in full in Section 2.5. (In Chapter 2 you can follow this energy account step by step in a moving figure.)

**Why full shells look "stable".** A full shell is not magic; it is where the next step gets expensive. Take one electron from sodium (2, 8, 1) and you remove the lonely outer one, which is far out and shielded. Try to take a second, and it must come out of the full shell underneath, much closer to the nucleus: that costs about 4,560 kJ per mole, more than nine times as much. Neon (2, 8) is the mirror image. An extra electron would have to go into a brand-new shell much farther out, where the 10 electrons inside shield it from almost all of the nucleus's pull, so it would not be held at all: a neon atom cannot keep an extra electron. Full shells mark the places where adding or removing one more electron suddenly costs a great deal of energy. That is all "a full shell is stable" really means.
:::

:::careful
> "Shells are the circles that electrons travel around on, like planets around the Sun."

Shell diagrams are a way of counting electrons by energy, and they are useful. But electrons do not follow tracks at all. The truth, which needs quantum physics to describe properly, is that we can only say how likely an electron is to be found in different places. Shell diagrams are a simplified model, and later chapters will show where it needs improving.
:::

## The three mystery beams

:::case The three mystery beams
A particle detector picks up three kinds of particle, called X, Y and Z.

- X has 8 protons, 8 neutrons and 10 electrons.
- Y has 8 protons, 10 neutrons and 8 electrons.
- Z has 10 protons, 10 neutrons and 10 electrons.

A lab assistant notices that X and Z have the same number of electrons, and claims that Y and Z have the same mass number. She concludes that at least two of them must be the same element.

Which of X, Y and Z are the same element? Write a full nuclear symbol for each, and explain where the assistant went wrong.
???
Only the protons decide the element. X and Y both have 8 protons, so both are oxygen. Z has 10 protons, so it is neon.

- X is [[nuc:16:8:O:2−]]: oxygen-16 with two extra electrons, an oxide ion.
- Y is [[nuc:18:8:O:]]: oxygen-18, a neutral atom of a heavier isotope.
- Z is [[nuc:20:10:Ne:]]: neon-20, a neutral atom.

The assistant matched the wrong counts. Sharing an electron count (X and Z) does not make two particles the same element. The assistant also miscounted: Y has A = 8 + 10 = 18, whereas Z has A = 10 + 10 = 20. Sharing a proton count (X and Y) establishes the element. These inventories alone do not establish species stability.
:::
