Open a cold bottle of soda water and listen. That hiss is carbon dioxide escaping: the gas was squeezed into the water at the factory, and the moment you release the pressure, some of it bubbles out. Taste the water and it is faintly sharp, a little sour, even though nobody added lemon. Something in that water is a weak acid, and it came from the gas.

Hold on to that bottle, because it is a small model of something enormous. The whole ocean is slowly doing what your soda water did at the factory: taking in carbon dioxide from the air above it.

All through this adventure, ideas have lived in separate episodes: atoms in one, acids in another, batteries in a third. The real world doesn't sort itself like that. A single flame on a gas stove involves counting atoms, breaking and making bonds, energy barriers, moles, the air and, years later, the sea. This last episode follows two of the biggest pieces of chemistry on our planet, the carbon dioxide we add to the air and the batteries we hope will replace much of our burning, and shows that you already have every idea you need to understand them.

## 11.1 The ocean and the carbon dioxide we add

:::scene Mauna Loa, Hawaii, March 1958

High on the side of a volcano in the middle of the Pacific Ocean, more than three thousand metres up and thousands of kilometres from the nearest continent, a young American scientist, not yet thirty, named Charles David Keeling set up an instrument to measure one thing: how much carbon dioxide there is in the air. Other people had measured it before, but their numbers jumped about wildly, because air near towns, fields and forests is always being changed by whatever is nearby. Keeling was a keen hiker and camper who had first taken up measuring carbon dioxide almost by accident, as a young researcher in California, carrying glass flasks into forests and up mountains. He was fussy, in the best way. He built careful instruments, checked them against standard gases again and again, and chose a place where the air had blown across thousands of kilometres of open sea.

His first readings were about 315 parts per million: out of every million molecules of air, about 315 were carbon dioxide. Within a couple of years he noticed two things. The number went up and down with the seasons, a few parts per million each year, as the forests of the northern half of the world drew carbon dioxide in each summer and let it out again each winter: the planet breathing. And underneath the breathing, the number climbed, every single year. The measurements have continued ever since, and the line, now called the Keeling curve, has passed 420 parts per million.

:::story keeling

Where is all that carbon dioxide coming from? When methane, petrol or coal burns, its carbon atoms join with oxygen from the air to make carbon dioxide. None of those atoms disappears (Chapter 4), so all of that carbon ends up somewhere. Some stays in the air, where carbon dioxide traps heat and warms the planet. (The warm ground gives off invisible infrared light. Carbon dioxide molecules absorb some of it and send it off again in every direction, some of it back down, so less heat escapes to space.) Some is taken up by plants. And a large share dissolves into the sea: over the past two centuries, the oceans have absorbed roughly a quarter to a third of the carbon dioxide released by burning fuel.

That sounds like good news, and in one way it is: without the oceans, the air would hold even more carbon dioxide. But dissolving carbon dioxide changes the sea, exactly as it changed your soda water. When CO₂ dissolves, a little of it reacts with water to make ==carbonic acid==, H₂CO₃, a weak acid that can pass an H⁺ to water:

:::eq
CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻
:::

:::note Carbonic acid
H₂CO₃, a weak acid formed when carbon dioxide reacts with water. It gives the slight sharpness to fizzy drinks.
:::

More H⁺ means a lower pH. But seawater has a defence, and you met it in Chapter 7: a ==buffer==. Seawater contains carbonate ions, CO₃²⁻, and most of the new H⁺ ions are picked up by them, turning them into bicarbonate ions, HCO₃⁻:

:::eq
H⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → HCO₃⁻
:::

Put the two steps together and count everything. For every molecule of carbon dioxide that dissolves and reacts, one carbonate ion is used up and two bicarbonate ions are made:

:::eq
CO₂ + H₂O + CO₃²⁻ → 2 HCO₃⁻
:::

:::note Buffer
A solution containing particles that take up added H⁺ (or OH⁻), so its pH changes much less than it otherwise would.
:::

:::home The buffer race
Ask an adult to help you make red cabbage indicator: chop a few leaves of red cabbage, cover them with warm tap water, leave them for half an hour, then strain off the purple liquid. Pour equal amounts into two glasses and top each up with the same amount of water (filtered RO drinking water, if you have it, gives the clearest result). Into one glass only, stir the tiniest pinch of baking soda, just enough to turn it blue-green: this is your pretend sea, slightly basic and buffered. The other glass is plain water with no buffer.

**Predict first.** You are going to blow ten slow breaths through a straw into each glass (blow out only; never suck up the liquid). Which glass will change colour first?

Your breath carries carbon dioxide, made when your body "burns" its food, and in water it makes carbonic acid. The plain glass shifts towards pinkish purple quite quickly; the change is gentle, so judge it against white paper. The pretend sea holds its colour much longer, because its bicarbonate soaks up the H⁺. Keep blowing into it, ten breaths at a time, and slowly it drifts towards purple too: the buffer is being worn down, breath by breath, just as the sea's carbonate is. For one more link in the chain, an adult can hold an upturned glass a hand's width above a lit tealight for ten seconds, slide a saucer over its mouth while turning it the right way up, then pour in a little indicator and swirl: burning, too, makes carbon dioxide. That change is small, so look against white paper. (Drink none of it.)
:::

:::figwide c11_carbonate() | What happens to carbon dioxide in the sea. Most of the H⁺ it releases is taken up by carbonate ions. The pH drops only a little, but the carbonate that shell-building animals need is used up. (The proportions in the bar are rough values for surface seawater today.)

So the buffer works: the sea's pH has not plunged towards the pH of lemon juice. But a buffer is not free. Every H⁺ it soaks up uses up a carbonate ion, and corals, oysters, sea snails and many tiny plankton build their shells and skeletons out of exactly those ions:

:::eq
Ca²⁺ + CO₃²⁻ ⇌ CaCO₃ (shell)
:::

With fewer carbonate ions around, it becomes harder for them to build shells, and in places where the water is already low in carbonate, existing shells can start to dissolve. This whole process is called ==ocean acidification==.

:::note Ocean acidification
The slow fall in the pH of seawater as it absorbs carbon dioxide from the air.
:::

:::home A shell in vinegar
Put a clean piece of eggshell (or a small seashell) in a glass and cover it with white vinegar. Watch closely: tiny bubbles form on the shell. Leave it overnight and see how much of the shell is left. Eggshell and seashell are both mostly calcium carbonate, CaCO₃; the acid in vinegar takes away its carbonate as carbon dioxide gas. Vinegar is far, far more acidic than seawater, so this is a dramatic, sped-up picture, not what is happening in the ocean. But the chemistry pulling on the carbonate is the same.
:::

The pH of the surface ocean has fallen from about 8.2 before industry began to about 8.1 today. A tenth of a pH unit sounds tiny. But remember that each step on the pH scale is a factor of ten (Chapter 7):

:::work
Change of 0.1 in pH → H⁺ concentration multiplied by 10⁰·¹
10⁰·¹ = 1.26
So there are about 26% more hydrogen ions in surface seawater than before industry. (You will often see "about 30%" quoted, because estimates of the starting pH vary a little.)
:::

:::careful
> "Ocean acidification means the sea is turning into acid."

Seawater is still slightly basic: its pH of about 8.1 is above 7, the neutral point. "Acidification" means *moving towards* acid, not becoming acidic. The worry is not that the sea will burn your skin. It is that the carbonate balance that marine life depends on is shifting faster than it has for a very long time. Nor is it caused by factory waste or acid rain: it is the same carbon dioxide that is in your soda water, simply far more of it.
:::

How do we know the sea is really changing, and not just the air? Keeling's curve tells us about the air. For the water, scientists needed a second patient watcher. Since 1988, researchers have sailed regularly to a spot in the open ocean north of Hawaii, a station called ALOHA, and sampled the seawater there, month after month. As the carbon dioxide in the air has risen, the dissolved carbon dioxide in the water has risen with it, and the pH has fallen steadily: by a few hundredths of a unit in thirty years. Two long, careful records, one of the air and one of the sea, tell the same story.

## 11.2 A battery you can refill

:::scene Oxford, England, 1980

In a laboratory at the University of Oxford, a professor named John Goodenough was hunting for a better material to hold lithium. He was already in his late fifties, an age at which many scientists slow down. As a boy he had struggled badly to learn to read (he was probably dyslexic), and he had served as a weather forecaster in the Second World War before coming to science. He never stopped working: he was still going to his laboratory in his nineties. A few years earlier, Stanley Whittingham, working for the oil company Exxon in the United States, had shown that lithium ions could slip in and out between the layers of a solid and make a working rechargeable cell. But Whittingham's cells used pure lithium metal, which could catch fire, and they gave only a little over 2 volts. Goodenough reasoned that a metal *oxide*, built in layers, should hold onto incoming electrons more strongly and so give a higher voltage. His team tried lithium cobalt oxide. It gave about 4 volts.

Five years later in Japan, Akira Yoshino, working for the chemical company Asahi Kasei, took the last dangerous piece away. Instead of lithium metal on the other side, he used a form of carbon that could hold lithium atoms tucked between its layers. The cell no longer contained any bare lithium metal at all, and it was far safer. The first lithium-ion batteries went on sale, made by Sony, in 1991. In 2019 the three men shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry; Goodenough was 97, the oldest person ever to win a Nobel Prize.

:::story lithium-ion

What did they build? Burning releases energy by moving electrons into lower-energy arrangements, as fuel and oxygen turn into carbon dioxide and water. A battery does something similar with electrons, but without fire and without making carbon dioxide, and a rechargeable battery can do it backwards too. The phone in your hand most likely runs on a ==lithium-ion cell==.

:::note Lithium-ion cell
A rechargeable cell in which lithium ions move back and forth between layers of graphite and layers of a metal oxide, while electrons travel through the outside circuit.
:::

Inside, there are two layered materials, separated by a liquid or gel that lets ions through but not electrons (Chapter 8). On one side is graphite, the same flat sheets of carbon as in a pencil, the very pencil this adventure began with. When the cell is charged, lithium atoms are tucked between those sheets. On the other side is a metal oxide, for example Goodenough's cobalt oxide, also built in layers with gaps between them.

Picture two books. The graphite is one book, and its sheets are the pages; the lithium atoms are slips of paper tucked between the pages. The metal oxide is a second book. Using the battery slides the slips out of the first book, across, and in between the pages of the second. Charging pushes them back. No page is torn and no slip is lost, which is why it can be done hundreds of times.

When you switch on the phone, the cell **discharges**. Each lithium atom between the graphite sheets gives up one electron and becomes a lithium ion, Li⁺. The electron cannot cross the separator, so it takes the long way round: out through the wire, through the phone, doing useful work, and in at the other side. Meanwhile the Li⁺ ion travels the short way, straight through the electrolyte, and slips in between the layers of the metal oxide, where it meets the electron again.

:::fig c11_li_cell() | A lithium-ion cell discharging. Lithium ions take the short path through the electrolyte; electrons take the long path through the phone. To charge the cell, a charger pushes both back to the graphite.
:::live licell {}

Count the charges and you will see why the two paths must go together. Every time an electron leaves one side through the wire, a positive ion leaves the same side through the electrolyte, so neither side builds up a charge. Stop either flow (cut the wire, or block the ions) and the other stops too.

When you **charge** the phone, the charger does work: it pushes electrons back through the wire to the graphite, and the lithium ions follow them back across the electrolyte into the graphite layers. This is the uphill direction (an electrolytic process, Chapter 8), which is why it needs a power supply. No atoms are made or lost in either direction. The same lithium atoms shuttle back and forth, which is why these cells are sometimes called "rocking-chair" batteries.

Why lithium? Three reasons, all from earlier episodes. It is the lightest metal: a mole of lithium has a mass of only 6.94 g. Each atom gives up exactly one electron, so each 6.94 g of lithium can deliver a whole mole of electrons. And lithium gives up that electron very readily, which makes the voltage of a lithium cell high, about 3.6 to 3.7 volts, compared with about 1.1 volts for the zinc–copper cell in Chapter 8.

:::work
Moles of electrons per kilogram of metal:
Lithium: 1000 g ÷ 6.94 g per mole × 1 electron = about 144 moles of electrons
Lead (in a car battery, each atom gives 2 electrons): 1000 g ÷ 207.2 g per mole × 2 = about 9.7 moles of electrons
Lithium can deliver about 15 times as many electrons per kilogram.
:::

A whole cell is much heavier than the lithium inside it (there is graphite, metal oxide, electrolyte, casing and wiring too), so a real lithium-ion battery stores several times more energy per kilogram than a lead–acid battery, not fifteen times. That is still why phones and electric cars use lithium.

:::careful
> "Charging fills a battery up with electrons from the wall socket, so a full battery is heavier."

The number of electrons in the cell never changes. For every electron the charger pushes into the graphite side, it pulls one out of the metal oxide side. What charging stores is *energy*: the lithium and its electrons are pushed back into a higher-energy arrangement, ready to run downhill again. (Batteries do slowly wear out, but not because anything is used up by electrons leaving. Side reactions slowly trap some of the lithium in a thin crust on the graphite, so there is less to shuttle.)
:::

:::

:::check
Why must lithium ions move through the electrolyte at the same time as electrons move through the wire? What would happen if they could not?
???
Each electron that leaves the graphite side through the wire takes away a negative charge; the Li⁺ ion leaving the same side through the electrolyte takes away a matching positive charge. Together they keep both sides neutral. If the ions could not move, negative charge would pile up on one side and positive on the other, the build-up would push back against the electrons, and the current would stop almost at once.
:::

## The kilogram of gas

:::case The kilogram of gas
The piped gas in many Mumbai kitchens is mostly methane, CH₄. Suppose a family's stove burns exactly 1.00 kg of it over a few weeks. This last case needs everything you have learned on the whole journey. Here is what the clues say.

1. One mole of methane has a mass of 16.04 g. Burning it: CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O.
2. Careful measurement shows that the flames produced 2.74 kg of carbon dioxide and 2.25 kg of water vapour: almost 5 kg of products from 1 kg of fuel.
3. Average bond energies, in kilojoules per mole of bonds: C–H 413, O=O 498, C=O 799, O–H 463.
4. A sealed bottle of methane and air can sit on a shelf for years without burning, yet a single spark lights it.
5. Over the following years, a share of that carbon dioxide dissolves in the sea.

Answer these, and for each one say which of the book's four big ideas (count everything; opposite charges attract; jiggling against sticking; huge numbers make steady averages) you used.

(a) Where did the extra mass of the products come from?
(b) Burning breaks every bond in the methane and oxygen, and breaking bonds always costs energy. So why does the flame give out heat? How much, for the whole kilogram?
(c) Why doesn't the gas burn on its own, and why does one spark set it going?
(d) What happens to the sea's carbonate when the CO₂ dissolves?
???
**(a) From the air.** 1.00 kg of methane is 1000 ÷ 16.04 = 62.3 moles. The equation needs 2 moles of O₂ for each mole of CH₄, so the flame took 124.7 moles of oxygen, 3.99 kg, out of the kitchen air. Mass in: 1.00 + 3.99 = 4.99 kg. Mass out: 2.74 + 2.25 = 4.99 kg. Every atom is accounted for. *Count everything*, using moles: *huge numbers*.

**(b) Making bonds pays back more than breaking them costs.** For one mole of methane:

- Break 4 C–H and 2 O=O: 4 × 413 + 2 × 498 = 2,648 kJ taken in.
- Make 2 C=O and 4 O–H: 2 × 799 + 4 × 463 = 3,450 kJ given out.
- Net: 2,648 − 3,450 = −802 kJ per mole, which matches the measured value for methane burning to carbon dioxide and water vapour.

For the whole kilogram: 62.3 × 802 ≈ 50,000 kJ, or 50 megajoules. The new bonds hold their atoms more tightly than the old ones did: *opposite charges attract*, and the electrons end up in lower-energy positions, closer to the nuclei that pull on them.

**(c) An energy barrier.** At room temperature, only a tiny fraction of collisions between methane and oxygen molecules are energetic enough to get over the barrier and start breaking bonds. A spark makes a small spot so hot that many collisions there do get over it; the heat they release then keeps the next layer of gas hot enough, and the flame keeps going. *Jiggling against sticking*.

**(d) It gets used up.** The CO₂ forms carbonic acid, and most of the H⁺ it releases is taken up by carbonate ions, turning them into bicarbonate. The pH falls only a little, but there is less carbonate left for shell-building animals. Equilibria and buffers: *huge numbers make steady averages*, together with *count everything*, since every carbon atom from the gas ends up somewhere.
:::

One last story before the end. The alchemists of the Middle Ages, the people who first set up furnaces and flasks and began the long road to chemistry, dreamed above all of turning lead into gold. Chemistry could never do it: as you now know, a reaction only rearranges electrons, and an element is fixed by the number of protons in its nucleus. Only nuclear physics can change that number. In 2025 scientists at CERN's ALICE experiment reported that lead nuclei, smashed past each other at almost the speed of light, had sometimes lost three protons and become gold: billions of nuclei in all, adding up to only a tiny fraction of a billionth of a gram. In October 2026 a start-up company, Marathon Fusion, claimed to have made gold from mercury using neutrons from fusion; at the time of writing, no one else has yet checked that claim. The old dream came true, in a way nobody in the Middle Ages could have imagined, and it turned out not to matter much. Chemistry's real treasure was never gold. It was understanding.

:::next Where you go from here
You started this adventure with a pencil and a diamond, and a question about how anyone can know what is inside things too small to see. Look how far you have travelled since. You stood with Greek thinkers arguing about whether a pebble could be cut forever. You watched a Manchester schoolteacher weigh things nobody could see, and a young team fire particles at gold foil in a darkened room and find almost all of an atom is empty. You counted protons, weighed molecules by the mole, watched energy run downhill and be pushed uphill, saw Wöhler's crystals and van 't Hoff's tetrahedra, and followed carbon from a gas flame in a Mumbai kitchen, through Keeling's air, into the sea. And the graphite in that first pencil turned out to be the same carbon that holds the lithium in the phone in your pocket.

Every person you met in this book began exactly where you are now: with a question, some careful measurements, and a willingness to be wrong for a while. None of them knew all the answers. Many got things wrong before they got them right. What they had was the habit you now have too: asking *how do we know?*, and not stopping until the evidence answers.

There is so much more still to find. Beyond this book, electrons stop looking like dots and become clouds of probability, described by quantum mechanics. Chemists design new molecules step by step, from medicines to materials for better batteries. And some of the most important open questions in the world, how to capture carbon dioxide, how to store sunlight, how life began, are chemistry questions that nobody has fully answered yet. The adventure does not end here. It just stops being a book and starts being yours.
:::
