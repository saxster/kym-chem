:::scene Berlin, February 1828

A 27-year-old chemist named Friedrich Wöhler is heating a white salt called ammonium cyanate, made from nothing more mysterious than minerals and the gases of a laboratory. When he looks at the crystals that form, he finds something that should not be there. They are urea: the same substance that living bodies make and get rid of in urine. Wöhler had trained as a doctor before chemistry pulled him away, and he had spent a year in Stockholm learning from the most famous chemist in Europe. Now he writes, delighted, to that old teacher in Stockholm, Jöns Jacob Berzelius, that he can make urea without needing a kidney, "either of man or dog".

Why was that exciting? For centuries people had believed that the substances of living things, sugar, oil, urea, the stuff of flesh and wood, could only be made by life itself, by some special "vital force" that ordinary chemistry could never copy. Wöhler's crystals did not end that belief overnight (the story is slower and messier than that, and you can read it in full), but they were one of the first cracks. Slowly chemists came to see that the molecules of life obey exactly the same rules as everything else in this book. They are special for a different reason: almost all of them are built on a skeleton of carbon.

:::story organic

Look at the label on almost anything in a kitchen: sugar, cooking oil, ghee, vinegar, rice, the plastic of the bottle, the soap by the sink. Different as they are, nearly all of them are made of molecules built around carbon. So are the fibres of your clothes, the DNA in your cells and the fuel in a scooter. Of the many millions of compounds that chemists have described, the great majority contain carbon. This episode of our adventure is about why one element can build so much, and about the small groups of atoms that decide what each carbon molecule actually does.

## 10.1 Chains, branches and rings

:::scene Utrecht, the Netherlands, 1874

In 1874 a 22-year-old chemist called Jacobus van 't Hoff, not yet even a doctor of science, published a little pamphlet with a bold idea. He was a doctor's son from Rotterdam who read poetry and philosophy as eagerly as chemistry, and he believed that a scientist needed imagination as much as a good balance. Some carbon compounds were known to come in two forms that were mirror images of each other, like your left and right hands. That made no sense if a carbon atom's bonds lay flat on a page, as chemists usually drew them. But it made perfect sense, van 't Hoff argued, if the four bonds of a carbon atom pointed to the four corners of a ==tetrahedron==, a little pyramid with a triangle for a base. In Paris, Joseph Le Bel had reached the same idea independently, within months. A famous German chemist, Hermann Kolbe, mocked van 't Hoff in print for his flights of fancy. The young man turned out to be right. Decades later X-ray measurements showed the angle between the bonds of such a carbon atom: about 109.5°, exactly a tetrahedron's angle. And in 1931 the American chemist Linus Pauling, a pharmacist's son from Oregon who had lost his father at nine and worked his way through college, chopping wood and teaching younger students, used the new quantum physics to explain *why* carbon's four bonds point that way. In 1901 van 't Hoff received the very first Nobel Prize in Chemistry (for different work), and molecules have been three-dimensional ever since.

:::note Tetrahedron
A pyramid with four triangular faces. The four bonds of a carbon atom with only single bonds point to its four corners, about 109.5° apart.
:::

:::story tetrahedron

Why four bonds? A carbon atom has 6 electrons: 2 in its inner shell and 4 in its outer one. Those 4 outer electrons let it form four covalent bonds, four shared pairs of electrons (Chapter 2). Count the lines meeting at any carbon atom in a structure, and you will almost always find four.

What makes carbon special is that its bonds *to other carbon atoms* are strong, roughly as strong as its bonds to hydrogen. So carbon atoms can join to each other again and again, forming long ==chains==, chains with branches, and closed rings, with hydrogen atoms (and sometimes oxygen or nitrogen) filling the remaining bonds. Silicon, the element just below carbon in the periodic table, also forms four bonds, but a silicon–silicon bond is only about two-thirds as strong as a carbon–carbon bond, and long silicon chains fall apart easily in air and water. Carbon's chains don't.

:::note Organic compound
A compound built around carbon atoms, usually bonded to hydrogen. The name is old: people once thought only living things could make them.
:::

The simplest family is the ==alkanes==: chains of carbon with only hydrogen attached and only single bonds. Methane, CH₄, has one carbon; ethane, C₂H₆, has two; propane has three; butane has four. Each extra carbon brings two more hydrogens, so an alkane with *n* carbons has 2*n* + 2 hydrogens.

:::note Alkane
A chain of carbon atoms joined by single bonds, with hydrogen everywhere else. Formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂: methane, ethane, propane, butane...
:::

:::fig c10_frameworks() | Three ways to arrange carbon. A straight chain, a branched chain and a ring. The chains are drawn as zig-zags because real chains are zig-zag shaped: the four bonds around each carbon point to the corners of a tetrahedron.
:::live organic3d {"set":"frameworks"}

Turn the 3D model and you will see van 't Hoff's tetrahedra everywhere: at every carbon, the four bonds splay out into space, which is why a "straight" chain is really a zig-zag.

Drawing every hydrogen gets tiring, so chemists often use a shorthand called a ==skeletal formula==: just the zig-zag lines of the carbon skeleton, with a carbon understood at every corner and every end, and enough hydrogens understood to give each carbon four bonds. Butane becomes a little zig-zag of three lines. You will see these drawings in any chemistry book; now you know how to read them.

:::note Skeletal formula
A shorthand drawing of a molecule: lines for the carbon skeleton, a carbon at each corner and end, hydrogens on carbon left out.
:::

:::five Organic chemistry
? What is it?
The chemistry of compounds built on a skeleton of carbon atoms, usually with hydrogen attached: sugars, oils, plastics, fuels, medicines and the molecules of life.
? Why is it called this?
Berzelius named it around 1807 for substances that came from living *organisms*, because people then believed only life could make them. The name outlived the belief. In chemistry, "organic" does not mean natural or pesticide-free: petrol and plastic are organic compounds.
? What problem did it solve?
Millions of different substances seemed to follow no shared rules. Seeing them as carbon skeletons with groups attached turned a jungle into a handful of patterns.
? Why does it work?
A carbon atom has four outer electrons, so it forms four bonds, and its bonds to other carbon atoms are strong. So carbon can link to itself again and again in chains, branches and rings, with bonds left over for hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen.
? How does it help?
Know a molecule's skeleton and its functional groups, and you can predict how it behaves: whether it mixes with water, how high it boils, whether it is an acid or a base.
?? How do we know?
In 1828 Wöhler made urea, a substance of living bodies, from lifeless starting materials, and in 1901 Fischer joined the building blocks of proteins by hand. The molecules of life obey the same rules as everything else.
?? What it isn't
Not "made only by living things" any more, and not "healthy" or "natural". The word describes carbon chemistry, nothing more.
?? Where does it stop working?
The border is blurry. Carbon dioxide, carbonates and diamond all contain carbon but are usually counted as inorganic, by tradition rather than by any deep rule.
:::

## 10.2 The part that does the chemistry

:::scene Your kitchen, with an orange and a balloon

Here is a trick that looks like magic. Blow up a balloon and tie it. Peel an orange, then hold a piece of peel close to the balloon and squeeze it hard, so that a fine spray of oil from the peel lands on the rubber. Very often, within a few seconds, the balloon bursts. Nothing sharp touched it.

The trick works because of a rule chemists sum up as *like dissolves like*. The oil in orange peel is mostly a molecule called limonene: a carbon skeleton covered in hydrogen, with no charged ends at all. Balloon rubber is also a long carbon-and-hydrogen molecule. The limonene soaks into the stretched rubber, softens and weakens it, and the balloon pops. Water, whose molecules have slightly charged ends, would do nothing to the balloon at all.

:::home The orange-peel balloon
You need a balloon and an orange (or a lemon). Blow up the balloon, then squeeze a piece of peel, coloured side towards the balloon, so its oil sprays onto the rubber. Try it on a few spots. Then try dripping plain water on another balloon. What does the difference tell you about which molecules dissolve which? (Keep the peel away from your eyes.)
:::

Alkanes and molecules like limonene are rather dull chemically. Their C–C and C–H bonds share electrons almost equally, so the molecules have no positive or negative ends, and they don't mix with water. That is the answer to half of this chapter's big question: petrol is a mixture of hydrocarbons, mostly alkanes, and water molecules stick to each other far more strongly than they stick to petrol, so the two stay apart and the lighter petrol floats on top. (The rainbow colours on a monsoon puddle come from light bouncing off the top and bottom of that very thin film of petrol.)

Now swap one hydrogen on ethane for an oxygen with a hydrogen attached, an –OH group. You get ethanol. The two molecules are almost the same size, yet ethane is a gas that boils at −88.6 °C and ethanol is a liquid that boils at 78.4 °C and mixes with water in any amount.

:::fig c10_functional() | Ethane and ethanol. Replacing one hydrogen with an –OH group turns a gas that ignores water into a liquid that mixes with it completely.

The difference comes from the O–H bond. Oxygen attracts the shared electrons much more strongly than hydrogen does, so the oxygen end carries a slight negative charge and the hydrogen a slight positive one. That hydrogen can be attracted to an oxygen on a *neighbouring* molecule: a hydrogen bond, the same kind of attraction that holds water molecules together (Chapter 3). It was Linus Pauling, again, who gave chemists a number scale for how strongly each element pulls on shared electrons, and who made hydrogen bonds a central idea in chemistry; later he used them to work out how protein chains coil and fold. Ethanol molecules hold on to each other, which raises the boiling point, and they hold on to water molecules too, which lets them mix.

:::story pauling

A small group of atoms like –OH, which gives a molecule its particular behaviour, is called a ==functional group==. It is the part that does the chemistry. The carbon skeleton is the frame; the functional groups are the handles, hooks and switches fixed onto it.

:::note Functional group
A small group of atoms on a carbon molecule, such as –OH or –COOH, that gives it its typical behaviour.
:::

| Group | Name | Example | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| –OH | hydroxyl (alcohols) | ethanol | hydrogen bonds; mixes with water |
| –COOH | carboxyl (carboxylic acids) | ethanoic acid, in vinegar | gives away H⁺: a weak acid |
| –NH₂ | amino (amines) | in almost every amino acid | picks up H⁺: a weak base |
| C=C | double bond (alkenes) | ethene | holds the molecule flat; can open up to join other molecules |

Sugar answers the other half of the big question. A sugar molecule has a carbon skeleton studded with –OH groups, five of them on a glucose molecule, so it forms hydrogen bonds with water all over its surface and dissolves easily into your chai.

Soap is a molecule with a split personality, and that is exactly why it works. One end is a long carbon tail with no charged ends, which mixes with grease; the other end is charged and loves water. The tails bury themselves in a spot of oil, the heads face outwards into the water, and the oil is carried away in tiny wrapped-up droplets.

:::home Oil, water and soap
Half-fill a clear glass jar with water and add a spoonful of cooking oil. Put the lid on and shake hard. Watch: within a minute the oil gathers back into a layer on top. Now add a few drops of washing-up liquid, shake again, and compare. Which molecules are holding on to which?

Then a second test. Drop one sugar-coated sweet (a Gems or similar) into a glass of water and another into a glass of cooking oil. Predict first: which one will lose its colour? The sugar coat, studded with –OH groups, dissolves in the water and the colour runs; in the oil, it hardly changes. (The sweet from the oil glass is still fine to eat once wiped.)
:::

:::careful
> "Adding an –OH group makes the molecule boil higher just because it makes it heavier."

Check the masses. Ethane (30 grams per mole) and methanol, CH₃OH (32 grams per mole), weigh almost the same, yet ethane boils at −88.6 °C and methanol at 64.7 °C. The extra mass is tiny; the hydrogen bonding between methanol molecules is what holds them together.
:::

:::five Functional group
? What is it?
A small group of atoms on a carbon skeleton, such as –OH or –COOH, that gives the molecule its typical behaviour. The skeleton is the frame; the functional group is the part that does the chemistry.
? Why is it called this?
Because it decides the molecule's *function*: how it reacts and what it mixes with. Molecules with the same group behave alike, whatever the length of their skeleton.
? What problem did it solve?
With millions of carbon compounds, learning each one separately is impossible. Learn a dozen groups and you can predict the behaviour of most of them.
? Why does it work?
Carbon–carbon and carbon–hydrogen bonds share electrons almost evenly and do little. Groups containing oxygen or nitrogen share them unevenly, making slightly charged ends that form hydrogen bonds, give away H⁺ or pick it up.
? How does it help?
It explains why sugar dissolves in chai but petrol floats on a puddle, why vinegar is sour, why soap lifts grease, and how amino acids link into proteins.
?? How do we know?
Swap one hydrogen on ethane for –OH: a gas that boils at −88.6 °C becomes ethanol, a liquid that boils at 78.4 °C and mixes with water. Almost the same size; one group changed everything.
?? What it isn't
Not just extra weight. Methanol is only slightly heavier than ethane, yet it boils more than 150 °C higher, because of the hydrogen bonds its –OH group makes.
?? Where does it stop working?
Groups affect their neighbours. The –OH inside vinegar's –COOH gives up H⁺, while the –OH in ethanol essentially doesn't, because the C=O next door changes it. A group's behaviour depends on what it is attached to.
:::

:::

Methanol's –OH group has a slightly negative oxygen and a slightly positive hydrogen, so it forms hydrogen bonds with water molecules, and the two mix.

## 10.3 Longer chains, higher boiling points

:::scene Titusville, Pennsylvania, August 1859

On a farm in western Pennsylvania, a man named Edwin Drake and a hired well-driller had spent months boring a hole into the ground, while the locals laughed at "Drake's folly". At about 21 metres down, the drill hit oil. It was not the first oil anyone had ever seen, but Drake's well started an industry. Within a few years refineries were heating crude oil to sort it into its parts, at first for one thing above all: kerosene, a clean-burning fuel for lamps that could replace whale oil.

What the refiners were exploiting is one of the neatest patterns in chemistry. Crude oil is a jumble of alkanes, from tiny ones to long ones. They have no –OH groups, no charged ends, nothing to grab with. You might expect them all to behave alike. Yet the gas in a camping stove is a gas, the petrol in a car is a liquid, and a candle is a solid, and all three are alkanes. What changes is the length of the chain.

:::figwide c10_chain_bp() | Boiling points of the first eight straight-chain alkanes (measured values at normal air pressure). Each extra carbon raises the boiling point. Methane to butane are gases in a warm room; pentane onwards are liquids.

Even molecules with no permanent charged ends attract each other a little. The electrons in a molecule are always moving, and at any instant they may be slightly bunched on one side, making a fleeting charged end that tugs on the electrons of the next molecule. These flickering attractions are called ==dispersion forces==. Each one is weak, but they add up along the whole length of the molecule. A long chain has more electrons and more surface touching its neighbours than a short one, so long chains cling together more, and it takes a higher temperature to pull them apart into a gas. These are the same flickering attractions, added up over millions of tiny hairs on its toes, that let a gecko hang upside down from your ceiling.

:::note Dispersion forces
Weak attractions between all molecules, caused by electrons bunching up for an instant. They are stronger for bigger molecules with more surface in contact.
:::

That is how a refinery sorts crude oil. The oil is heated until most of it boils, and the vapour rises up a tall tower that gets cooler towards the top. Each kind of molecule condenses back into liquid at the height where the temperature falls below its boiling point: the long, heavy chains low down, and the short ones higher up. Short chains become gas for cooking, middle ones petrol and kerosene, long ones diesel, lubricating oil and wax. If boiling points did not climb steadily with chain length, as Figure 10.3 shows, the mixture could not be sorted this way.

:::careful
> "When butane boils, its molecules break apart into carbon and hydrogen."

No C–C or C–H bond breaks when an alkane boils. Boiling only pulls whole molecules away from their *neighbours*, overcoming the weak dispersion forces between them; the strong bonds *inside* each molecule are untouched. The molecules in butane gas are exactly the molecules that were in the liquid, and if you cool the gas they become liquid butane again. Breaking the bonds inside the molecule is a different and far bigger job: that is burning, or what a refinery's "crackers" do at much higher temperatures with a catalyst.
:::

:::five Dispersion forces
? What are they?
Weak attractions between all molecules, even ones with no charged ends, caused by electrons bunching up for an instant on one side of a molecule and tugging on the electrons of the next.
? Why are they called this?
The physicist Fritz London named them in 1930, because his quantum theory of them closely resembled the theory of the *dispersion* of light, the way light spreads into colours. Chemists often call them London forces after him. The name comes from the mathematics, not from anything spreading out.
? What problem did they solve?
Why should molecules with no charged ends, like the alkanes or the noble gases, stick together at all? Without some attraction, they could never become liquids or solids.
? Why do they work?
Electrons are always moving. At any instant one side of a molecule may have a few more, making a fleeting charged end that pulls the electrons of its neighbour into step. Each tug is tiny, but they add up along the whole length of a molecule.
? How do they help?
They explain why longer chains boil higher (so a refinery can sort crude oil), why branched molecules boil lower, why straight-chained ghee is solid while kinked olive oil is runny, and how a gecko walks on a ceiling.
?? How do we know?
Boiling points climb steadily as each carbon is added to an alkane chain, from methane, a gas, to octane, a liquid, with nothing changing except the length of the chain.
?? What they aren't
Not bonds inside the molecule. When butane boils, no bond breaks; whole molecules pull away from their neighbours.
?? Where do they stop working?
They are the weakest attractions between molecules. Where molecules have permanent charged ends, and above all where they can hydrogen-bond, those stronger forces usually decide the boiling point instead.
:::

:::home The alkane ladder
This one is for looking, not for heating. Many homes hold a ladder of alkanes of different lengths. The LPG cylinder in the kitchen holds mostly propane and butane, 3 and 4 carbons long (just look at it; don't touch the valve). Baby oil (liquid paraffin) is chains of roughly 15 to 30 or more carbons, many of them branched or bent into rings, so they pack together badly. A paraffin candle is mostly straight chains of roughly 20 to 40. Predict first: which is a gas, which a runny liquid, which a solid, and why in that order? Petroleum jelly (Vaseline) sits in between, because it is a mixture: long waxy chains with oily ones trapped among them. Rub a drop of baby oil and a dab of Vaseline between your fingers and feel the difference that chain length makes.
:::

## 10.4 Same formula, different molecule

:::scene Germany, the 1820s

Two young chemists who had never met were puzzling over two silver compounds. In Paris and then Giessen, Justus von Liebig studied silver fulminate, a substance so dangerous that it exploded at a touch. Friedrich Wöhler, the man who would later make urea, studied silver cyanate, which was perfectly calm. When their analyses were published side by side, something looked badly wrong: both compounds were made of exactly the same elements, in exactly the same proportions. Each chemist suspected the other of a mistake. They checked, and checked again, and found that both were right. Instead of becoming enemies, they became lifelong friends and partners. In 1830 Berzelius gave the puzzle its name: these were ==isomers==, from Greek words meaning "equal parts".

:::note Isomers
Different compounds with the same molecular formula but atoms joined in a different order.
:::

:::story isomers

The answer to the puzzle is that a formula lists the parts, but not how they are put together, any more than the list "four wheels, two seats, one engine" tells you whether you have a car or a rickshaw. Here are two substances with exactly the same formula, C₄H₁₀. Each molecule contains 4 carbons and 10 hydrogens, the same atoms, the same mass. One boils at −0.5 °C, the other at −11.7 °C. In butane the four carbons make a straight chain. In isobutane three carbons hang off a central one.

:::fig c10_isomers() | Two pairs of isomers. Branching makes isobutane more compact than butane, so less of each molecule touches its neighbours and it boils lower. Moving one oxygen turns ethanol, which can hydrogen-bond, into dimethyl ether, which cannot.
:::live organic3d {"set":"isomers"}

The butane pair differ for the reason you met in 10.3: a compact, branched molecule has less surface in contact with its neighbours than a stretched-out chain, so its dispersion forces are weaker and it boils lower.

The second pair differ far more. C₂H₆O can be ethanol, with its –OH group, or dimethyl ether, with the oxygen in the middle of the chain and every hydrogen attached to carbon. Ethanol boils at 78.4 °C; dimethyl ether at −24.8 °C, more than 100 °C lower. Without a hydrogen attached to the oxygen, dimethyl ether molecules can't form hydrogen bonds with each other. Same atoms, different arrangement: a liquid you could pour versus a gas.

:::careful
> "Butane and isobutane boil at different temperatures, so one of them must be heavier."

They contain exactly the same atoms, so their molecules have exactly the same mass. Any difference in how they behave must come from their shape and their connections, not their mass.
:::

:::five Isomers
? What are they?
Different substances whose molecules contain exactly the same atoms, joined in a different order or arrangement. Same formula, different molecule.
? Why are they called this?
Berzelius named them in 1830 from the Greek *isos*, "equal", and *meros*, "part": made of equal parts.
? What problem did they solve?
Liebig's explosive silver fulminate and Wöhler's calm silver cyanate had exactly the same composition, and each chemist assumed the other had made a mistake. Isomers showed both were right: what a molecule is made of is not the whole story.
? Why does it work?
A molecule's behaviour depends on how its atoms are connected and on its shape. Rearranging the same atoms can hide or expose a group that forms hydrogen bonds, or make a molecule more compact so its neighbours grip it less.
? How do they help?
They warn you that a formula names only the parts, and they explain why ethanol is a liquid while dimethyl ether, with the same atoms, is a gas. Shape matters just as much in medicines and inside your body.
?? How do we know?
Butane and isobutane, both C₄H₁₀, boil at −0.5 °C and −11.7 °C, and can be separated and bottled as two pure substances.
?? What they aren't
Not one molecule heavier than the other. Isomers have exactly the same mass; the differences come from shape and connections.
?? Where does the idea stop working?
Some arrangements turn into each other all the time, for example by spinning around a single bond, so they can't be bottled separately, and chemists don't usually count them as different substances. Only when an arrangement is locked, for example by different connections or by a double bond, do you get two separate substances.
:::

## 10.5 Double bonds and locked shapes

:::scene A kitchen shelf on a winter morning

Look along a kitchen shelf on a cool morning and you will find a puzzle. The ghee in its jar is solid. The coconut oil may be solid too, if the room is cold enough. But the olive oil and the sunflower oil are runny liquids. All of them are fats, made of the same kinds of atoms in the same kinds of long chains. Why are some solid and some liquid at the same temperature? The answer is a tiny, rigid kink.

Carbon atoms can share *two* pairs of electrons with each other: a ==double bond==, written C=C. Ethene, C₂H₄, is the simplest example. It has two fewer hydrogens than ethane, because each carbon is now using two of its four bonds on its partner. Molecules with C=C bonds are said to be *unsaturated*: they hold fewer hydrogens than they could.

:::note Double bond
Two shared pairs of electrons between the same two atoms. A C=C double bond is shorter and stronger than a single bond, and cannot twist.
:::

:::fig c10_single_double() | Single and double bonds compared. The ends of ethane spin around the single bond all the time. Ethene's double bond is shorter and keeps the whole molecule flat; twisting it would mean breaking part of the bond.
:::live ethane3d {}

A single bond is like an axle: the two ends of ethane spin round it freely, billions of times a second at room temperature. A double bond is different. Its second shared pair sits above and below the line between the two carbons, and it only works while the two ends stay lined up, so twisting one end would break part of the bond. At ordinary temperatures, that doesn't happen. All six atoms of ethene stay in one flat plane, with the bonds around each carbon about 120° apart.

How do we know a double bond really can't twist? Because some molecules come in two versions that would turn into each other if it could. But-2-ene is a chain of four carbons with a double bond in the middle. It comes as two separate substances: one with the two end groups on the same side of the double bond, which boils at about 4 °C, and one with them on opposite sides, which boils at about 1 °C. Chemists can bottle them separately. If the double bond could spin, there would be only one substance.

Now back to the shelf. The long chains in fats are mostly alkane-like. In ghee and butter, many of the chains are fully saturated and straight, so they pack together neatly, like pencils in a box, and the fat is solid at room temperature. In olive oil, many chains have a double bond with the two halves of the chain on the same side, which puts a fixed kink in the middle. Kinked chains can't pack neatly, their dispersion forces are weaker, and the oil stays liquid. Stearic acid, a straight 18-carbon chain, melts at about 69 °C; oleic acid, the same length with one kinked double bond, melts at about 13 °C.

:::home The fridge test
Put a spoonful each of ghee or butter, coconut oil (if you have it) and olive or sunflower oil into three small clean bowls and leave them in the fridge for an hour. Which ones turn hard, which go cloudy, and which stay runny? The more straight, saturated chains a fat has, the more easily its molecules pack together into a solid.
:::

:::try Investigation 10.5

:::check
Why is a fat whose chains are all straight and saturated more likely to be solid at room temperature than an oil whose chains each have a kinked double bond?
???
Straight chains pack closely side by side, so a lot of surface touches and the dispersion forces add up; it takes a higher temperature to separate them, so the fat is solid. Kinked chains can't pack closely, so they attract each other less and the oil melts at a lower temperature.

## 10.6 Acids, amines and the links of life

:::scene Berlin, 1901

Seventy-three years after Wöhler's urea, in the same city, the great German chemist Emil Fischer and his colleague Ernest Fourneau did something that would have astonished the believers in a vital force. They took two molecules of glycine, the simplest of the building blocks of proteins, and joined them by hand into a single molecule. It was the first time anyone had deliberately made the kind of link that holds every protein in every living thing together. Fischer went on to build chains of up to eighteen of these building blocks, and showed that they behaved like the fragments you get by breaking real proteins apart. Fischer was a timber merchant's son whose father, the family story goes, gave up trying to make a businessman of him and sent him off to study instead. He became the most famous organic chemist of his age: he had already worked out the shapes of the sugars, and won the Nobel Prize in 1902. To understand what he joined, and how, we need two more functional groups.

Vinegar is sour because of ethanoic acid (also called acetic acid), CH₃COOH. Its working part is the ==carboxyl group==, –COOH: a carbon with a double-bonded oxygen and an –OH. That hydrogen on the end can be passed to a water molecule as H⁺, so ethanoic acid is an acid, though a weak one: in vinegar, only a small fraction of its molecules give up their H⁺ at any moment (Chapter 7). The neighbouring C=O pulls on the electrons nearby, and once the H⁺ has gone, the leftover negative charge is spread over both oxygens rather than sitting on one. That is why the –OH in a carboxyl group gives up H⁺ while the –OH in ethanol essentially does not.

:::note Carboxyl group
–COOH. The functional group of carboxylic acids such as ethanoic acid. It can give an H⁺ to water.
:::

The ==amino group==, –NH₂, does the opposite. Its nitrogen has a pair of electrons not used in bonding, which can pick up an H⁺. So amines are weak bases. You can smell this one. The fishy smell of fish is mostly a small amine, trimethylamine, drifting into the air. Squeeze lemon on the fish and the smell fades: the lemon's acid hands H⁺ to the amine's spare pair of electrons, turning it into a charged ion that stays dissolved in the juice instead of floating up your nose.

:::note Amino group
–NH₂. A nitrogen with two hydrogens. Its spare pair of electrons can pick up H⁺, so it acts as a weak base.
:::

When a carboxyl group meets an –OH or an –NH₂ on another molecule, the two can join together, and in doing so they release a molecule of water. This is called a ==condensation reaction==. An acid joined to an alcohol makes an *ester*; many esters smell of fruit, and they are part of what makes a ripe banana or pineapple smell the way it does.

:::note Condensation reaction
A reaction in which two molecules join and a small molecule, usually water, is released.
:::

:::fig c10_condensation() | Making an ester. The –OH from the acid and the H from the alcohol leave together as water, and the two molecules join through the oxygen that is left. The atom count is the same on both sides.

Now the big one, the link Fischer made. An ==amino acid== has a carboxyl group and an amino group on the same molecule. So amino acids can join head to tail: the –COOH of one with the –NH₂ of the next, releasing water each time. The new link, –CO–NH–, is called a *peptide bond*, and the result can join to another amino acid, and another, and another. A protein is a chain of hundreds or thousands of amino acids made exactly this way, folded into a particular shape. Every enzyme in your body, the keratin in your hair and the haemoglobin in your blood were built like this, one water molecule at a time.

:::note Amino acid
A molecule with both an amino group and a carboxyl group. Twenty kinds are used to build the proteins in living things.
:::

Joining amino acids is one thing; seeing the shape of the finished molecule is another. In Oxford, Dorothy Hodgkin, who had fallen in love with crystals as a ten-year-old growing them at home, spent decades photographing how X-rays scatter off crystals of the molecules of life. She worked out the structure of penicillin in 1945, of vitamin B₁₂ in the 1950s, and in 1969, after thirty-four years of trying, of the protein insulin, much of it while living with painful arthritis in her hands. She won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1964.

The chemists who followed Wöhler and Fischer turned these ideas into medicines. In Kolkata in the mid-twentieth century, Asima Chatterjee, one of the first women in India to earn a doctorate in science, who had chosen chemistry against the expectations of her time, spent her working life finding out the structures of molecules from plants used in traditional medicine, and helped develop medicines from them. Every one of those molecules was a carbon skeleton with functional groups attached, obeying exactly the rules in this chapter.

:::careful
> "When two molecules join to make an ester, some atoms are destroyed, which is why the ester is lighter."

Every atom is accounted for: C₂H₄O₂ + CH₄O → C₃H₆O₂ + H₂O. In grams per mole, 60.05 + 32.04 = 74.08 + 18.02. The ester is lighter than the two starting molecules combined only because one water molecule's worth of atoms left as water.
:::

Glycine, C₂H₅NO₂, has a mass of 75.07 grams per mole. Two glycine molecules join by a peptide bond. What is the formula of the new molecule, and what is its mass per mole? (Water is 18.02 grams per mole.)
???
Two glycines contain C₄H₁₀N₂O₄. One water, H₂O, leaves, so the new molecule is C₄H₈N₂O₃. Its mass is 2 × 75.07 − 18.02 = 132.12 grams per mole.

## The case of the two clear liquids

:::case The case of the two clear liquids
Two unlabelled bottles each hold a few millilitres of a colourless liquid. The label that fell off says only "C₂H₆O". A chemist runs three tests.

1. Bottle A boils at 78 °C. Bottle B is barely liquid: it is in a sealed, chilled pressure bottle, and when it is opened at room temperature it hisses away as a gas.
2. Each sample has exactly the same mass per molecule: 46 grams per mole.
3. A drop of A mixes completely into water. A drop of liquid B, chilled first, mixes with water only partly.

A friend says the labels must be wrong, because one substance can't have two boiling points. Who is right? Draw both molecules, and explain all three clues.
???
Both bottles are correctly labelled C₂H₆O. They are isomers: the same atoms joined differently, just as Liebig's and Wöhler's silver salts were.

- **Bottle A is ethanol**, CH₃–CH₂–OH. Its oxygen carries a hydrogen, so ethanol molecules hydrogen-bond to each other (high boiling point, 78.4 °C) and to water (mixes completely).
- **Bottle B is dimethyl ether**, CH₃–O–CH₃. All six hydrogens are on carbon, so its molecules cannot hydrogen-bond to each other; only weaker attractions hold them, and it boils at −24.8 °C. Its oxygen can still accept a hydrogen bond from water, which is why it mixes partly.

Clue 2 rules out the idea that one is heavier: same formula, same mass. The friend's mistake is assuming a formula names a single substance. It names only the parts.
:::
