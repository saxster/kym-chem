## TOP 5 CHANGES (priority order)

1. **4.3: Let her rebuild Lavoisier's sealed vessel in her own kitchen.** The current home box only shows the open-system mass loss. It then *tells* her that a sealed version would show no change. Add a second step: do the same reaction inside a capped fizzy-drink PET bottle on the kitchen scale. The mass does not change. Then open the cap slowly, hear the hiss, and watch the reading drop. Ask her to predict before each step. Use a rigid bottle, not a balloon, because an inflating balloon pushes air aside and shows a false mass "loss" through buoyancy. This targets the most common misconceptions in the chapter: atoms are created or destroyed (36–40% of US students, AAAS), and gases have no mass (only about 25% of 13–16-year-olds accepted that the gas from a fizzing tablet has mass, per Kind/RSC). Sources: [ChemEdX sealed-bottle demo](https://chemedx.org/node/4983), [RSC Teaching conservation of mass](https://edu.rsc.org/cpd/teaching-conservation-of-mass-at-14-16/4020546.article), [NSTA buoyancy note](https://www.nsta.org/resources/science-sampler-conservation-mass-and-unsuspected-buoyancy-effect), [AAAS testing results](https://www.aaas.org/news/us-students-and-science-aaas-testing-gives-new-insight-what-they-know-and-their-misconceptions), [RSC/Kind closed systems](https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-ideas-about-specific-chemical-events-closed-system-reactions-beyond-appearances/4017790.article).

2. **5.5: Add a "two glasses, two straws" dynamic-equilibrium demo with a prediction.** This is a kitchen version of an activity in NCERT Class 11. Fill one glass with coloured water and leave a second glass empty. Use one wide straw and one thin straw, closing the top with a thumb to carry water. Move water from A to B with the wide straw and from B to A with the thin straw, one scoop each per turn. First ask her: "Will the levels end up equal?" They do not. They settle where the wide-straw scoop out of A equals the thin-straw scoop out of B, and keep changing hands forever. That is "equal rates, not equal amounts" made physical. In a UK survey, 48% of GCSE students believed concentrations are equal at equilibrium and 40% believed a catalyst raises yield. The live figure could animate the two flows as arrows sized by rate. Sources: [NCERT Class 11 Equilibrium (kech106)](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kech106.pdf), [Riddle & Lo-Fan-Hin, Univ. of Leicester *New Directions*](https://journals.le.ac.uk/index.php/new-directions/article/download/3900/3677/13531).

3. **7.3: Fix one sentence that teaches a misconception, and make the tenfold steps visible.**
   - (a) Section 7.3 says cabbage turns "red-pink for strong acids, pink-purple for weak ones". The colour shows the pH, not the strength. Vinegar is a weak acid at about pH 2.4 and turns cabbage red-pink. The current wording reinforces the confusion of strength with pH that 7.2 tries to undo. In Sheppard's study, 6 of 16 students thought pH measures acid strength, and PhET flags the same difficulty. Reword it by pH: "red for very acidic (pH ~2), pink-purple for mildly acidic…".
   - (b) The `c7_ph_ladder` figure is an evenly spaced ruler, so it *looks* linear. PhET found that bar graphs led students to call 100-fold differences "twice as large", and most of Sheppard's students treated pH as linear. Add a staircase of countable dot-blocks under pH 7, 6, 5 and 4 (1, 10, 100 and 1,000 dots), or nested squares, so that each step is visibly ten times bigger. Also add the cabbage colour band under the scale to tie the figure to her home experiment.

   Sources: [Sheppard 2006, CERP](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2006/rp/b5rp90014j), [PhET pH Scale teacher guide](https://phet.colorado.edu/files/teachers-guide/ph-scale-html-guide_en.pdf), [PhET Acid-Base Solutions guide](https://phet.colorado.edu/files/teachers-guide/acid-base-solutions-html-guide_en.pdf), [Science Buddies cabbage colour chart](https://www.sciencebuddies.org/stem-activities/cabbage-pH-indicator).

4. **5.4: Give the catalyst section something she can see. It currently has no hands-on activity.** Add a home box: a pinch of dried yeast, or a slice of raw potato, in pharmacy hydrogen peroxide diluted to roughly 1.5%. It froths with oxygen and gets slightly warm. Then add fresh peroxide to the *same* yeast: it froths again, so the catalyst was not used up. For a prediction-before-reveal, try a slice of *boiled* potato, whose enzyme is destroyed by cooking. An adult must be present, eye protection is advised, and nothing is tasted. Sources: [ACS Middle School Chemistry 6.5 (yeast catalyst)](https://www.acs.org/content/dam/acsorg/msc/downloads/chapter-6/lesson-5/ch6-l5-lesson-plan.pdf), [RSC enzymes in food + H₂O₂ (dilute "5 volume", safety)](https://edu.rsc.org/425.article), [Science Buddies potato catalase](https://www.sciencebuddies.org/science-fair-projects/project_ideas/BioChem_p019.shtml).

5. **7.4: Confront "nothing is happening yet" in titrations.** About half of Sheppard's students thought the flat first part of a titration curve meant "no reaction has started". Most predicted a straight-line curve, and only 2 of 16 predicted the S-shape. Two fixes:
   - (a) Before the reveal of Figure 7.4, ask her to sketch the curve she expects.
   - (b) Reword the kitchen titration's "At first nothing seems to happen". Point at the evidence instead: it is fizzing the whole time, so the reaction is happening, and the baking soda is mopping up each spoon of vinegar before the colour can change.

   Optionally add a breath-into-cabbage demo, adapted from MIT's *Colorful Indicators*, where CO₂ makes water acidic. Blow gently through a straw into blue cabbage juice (cabbage juice plus a pinch of baking soda) and it drifts toward purple. Her own breath is an acid-making gas, which bridges directly to Henderson's blood buffer. Sources: [Sheppard 2006](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2006/rp/b5rp90014j), [MIT RES.HS-002 Colorful Indicators transcript](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-hs-002-chemistry-behind-the-magic-chemical-demonstrations-for-the-classroom/nAhIrh9vM4A_transcript.pdf).

## Chapter 4: What changes in a reaction?

### 4.1 What counts as a chemical change?

**Misconceptions:**
- **Dissolving, diluting and changes of state are "chemical".** Schollum found about 70% of 14-year-olds thought diluting fruit juice was a chemical change, and 48% thought dissolving sugar was one. Source: [Kind/RSC, Introducing chemical reactions](https://edu.rsc.org/resources/introducing-chemical-reactions-beyond-appearances/4017789.article).
- **Judging by drama.** Students rely on colour, fizz or heat, and often think the original substances persist inside the product. These are Andersson's "displacement" and "modification" ideas. Sources: [same Kind page](https://edu.rsc.org/resources/introducing-chemical-reactions-beyond-appearances/4017789.article), [IOP Spark: Andersson](https://spark.iop.org/andersson-1990).
- **Gas from a fizzing tablet was "air trapped in it".** Source: [Kind/RSC closed systems](https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-ideas-about-specific-chemical-events-closed-system-reactions-beyond-appearances/4017790.article).

**Best sequence and demos:**
- de Vos & Verdonk's route, summarised by Kind: make a new substance appear (cognitive conflict). Then *test* it against the starting materials, and only then explain with atoms. Source: [Kind/RSC](https://edu.rsc.org/resources/introducing-chemical-reactions-beyond-appearances/4017789.article).
- ACS 6.3 has students compare whether a precipitate and the reactants dissolve: "behaves differently, so it's new". Source: [ACS 6.3](https://www.acs.org/content/dam/acsorg/msc/downloads/chapter-6/lesson-3/ch6-l3-lesson-plan.pdf).
- NCERT Class 7 Activities 5.3 and 5.4: breathe into lime water, and pass the gas from vinegar plus baking soda into lime water. Source: [gecu105](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/gecu105.pdf).

**Recommendation:** Already strong. "Test the stuff, not the drama", the sugar-in-chai example and the fizzy-tablet check all hit these misconceptions. One addition would make the check answer *doable*:
- Add a short home box "Make your own gas detector". Stir a little *chuna* (slaked lime from a paan shop) into water, let it settle, and pour off the clear limewater. Blow gently through a straw into one glass. Bubble the gas from vinegar plus baking soda into another. Both go milky; plain air pumped from a squeezed empty bottle does not.
- Adult needed: chuna is caustic, and she should rinse her hands.
- This mirrors NCERT Class 7 Activity 5.3 she may have done at school, and turns the "trapped air?" question into a test she can run.

**NCERT:** Class 7 Curiosity Ch 5 ([gecu105](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/gecu105.pdf)) introduces physical and chemical change with word equations only. Class 10 Ch 1 ([jesc101](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/jesc101.pdf)) adds formal treatment and corrosion.

### 4.2 Reading a chemical formula

**Misconceptions:**
- **Coefficient and subscript are confused.** About 40% of 16–17-year-olds in BouJaoude & Barakat multiplied or divided molar mass by the equation coefficient. Sources: [Kind/RSC stoichiometry](https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-difficulties-with-stoichiometry-beyond-appearances/4017805.article), [PhET Balancing Equations guide](https://phet.colorado.edu/files/teachers-guide/balancing-chemical-equations-html-guide_en.pdf).
- **A formula is just a label, not a count of particles.** Students struggle to translate between symbols and particle pictures. Source: [PhET guide](https://phet.colorado.edu/files/teachers-guide/balancing-chemical-equations-html-guide_en.pdf).

**Best sequence and demos:**
- PhET's "hide the molecules" move: show only the symbols, have her draw the particles, then reveal. Source: [PhET guide](https://phet.colorado.edu/files/teachers-guide/balancing-chemical-equations-html-guide_en.pdf).
- Kind suggests physical formula cards before any maths. Source: [Kind stoichiometry](https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-difficulties-with-stoichiometry-beyond-appearances/4017805.article).

**Recommendation:** Already strong. The H₂O versus H₂O₂ contrast and the "2 H₂O is not H₄O₂" careful box are exactly right.
- Small upgrade: reverse Investigation 4.2 so it starts with a symbol (say 2 H₂O₂) and asks her to sketch the particles *before* turning the app's molecule view on, following PhET's hide-then-reveal pattern.
- Keep the ion-charge reasoning (MgCl₂, Al₂O₃).

**NCERT:** Formulae and formula-unit mass are in Class 9 (new) "Atomic Foundations of Matter" ([iesc109](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/iesc109.pdf)). Formula writing in equations is in Class 10 Ch 1 ([jesc101](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/jesc101.pdf)).

### 4.3 Where did every atom go?

**Misconceptions:**
- **Atoms are created, destroyed or turned into other atoms in reactions.** Of US students tested, 40% thought atoms are created, 36% thought they are destroyed, and 40% thought reactant atoms become different atoms. Source: [AAAS](https://www.aaas.org/news/us-students-and-science-aaas-testing-gives-new-insight-what-they-know-and-their-misconceptions).
- **Gases weigh nothing; smoke is weightless; solids are "heavier" than liquids.** Only about 25% of 13–16-year-olds accepted that the gas from a fizzing tablet has mass. Sources: [Kind/RSC closed systems](https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-ideas-about-specific-chemical-events-closed-system-reactions-beyond-appearances/4017790.article), [Özmen & Ayas abstract](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2003/rp/b3rp90017g).
- **Balancing by changing subscripts.** Source: [PhET guide](https://phet.colorado.edu/files/teachers-guide/balancing-chemical-equations-html-guide_en.pdf).

**Best sequence and demos:**
- The RSC sequence is open flask (mass falls), then closed system (mass constant), then atom-model rearrangement, then symbols. Source: [RSC CPD](https://edu.rsc.org/cpd/teaching-conservation-of-mass-at-14-16/4020546.article).
- ChemEdX shows why the balloon version misleads (the inflating balloon displaces air), and that a sealed soda bottle works. Source: [ChemEdX](https://chemedx.org/node/4983); see also [NSTA](https://www.nsta.org/resources/science-sampler-conservation-mass-and-unsuspected-buoyancy-effect).
- ACS 6.1 has students cut out atoms and rebuild CH₄ + 2O₂ into products. Source: [ACS 6.1](https://www.acs.org/content/dam/acsorg/msc/downloads/chapter-6/lesson-1/ch6-l1-lesson-plan.pdf).

**Recommendation: TOP-1.** Add a second part to "Weigh a gas escaping" so the Lavoisier story becomes something she does:
- **Predict first.** "Will the sealed bottle get lighter, heavier, or stay the same when it fizzes?"
- **Method.** Use a 1–2 L fizzy-drink PET bottle (built for pressure; not a water bottle and never glass). Add about 50 mL of vinegar. Put half a teaspoon of baking soda in a twist of tissue, drop it in, and cap the bottle tightly at once. Weigh it on the scale before and after the fizzing: the reading is unchanged. Then, pointing the bottle away from faces, open the cap slowly: hiss. Reweigh: lighter by about a gram or more.
- Use small amounts and an adult.
- A caption line could say: "Lavoisier's trick in a Thums Up bottle."
- Add a careful box: "Gases have no weight." Answer it with the bottle result, plus the fact that a litre of air has a mass of about 1.2 g. That figure is the air-density value used in [ChemEdX's buoyancy explanation](https://chemedx.org/node/4983).

**NCERT:** Class 9 (new) "Atomic Foundations of Matter", §9.1 law of conservation of mass ([iesc109](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/iesc109.pdf)). Balancing equations is in Class 10 Ch 1 ([jesc101](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/jesc101.pdf)).

### 4.4 What a balanced equation cannot tell us

**Misconceptions:**
- **A reaction keeps going until all reactants are used up**, and a balanced equation means the reaction happens. Source: [RSC CPD](https://edu.rsc.org/cpd/teaching-conservation-of-mass-at-14-16/4020546.article).
- **A time-based view of reactions**: reactions "start" when something visible happens. Source: [Sheppard 2006](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2006/rp/b5rp90014j).

**Best demo:** The APS "Rusted Out" activity seals vinegar-soaked steel wool in a zip bag with a thermometer and graphs temperature against time. Rusting is visibly exothermic and fast once the coating is stripped. Source: [APS](https://www.aps.org/learning-resources/students/rusted-out).

**Recommendation:** Already strong. The spark contrast and the "steel wool eats air" home box are excellent, and the box ends on exactly the right moral.
- Two small additions:
  - Add a prediction line *before* the method: "How high do you think the water will climb: not at all, halfway, a fifth?"
  - Add the tip that a brief dip in vinegar first strips the protective coating on steel wool, so the rusting is faster and more reliable (the APS activity uses the vinegar soak for this).
- Save the "it gets warm" observation (APS) for 5.1 as a cross-link (see below).

**NCERT:** Class 10 Ch 1 ([jesc101](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/jesc101.pdf)) covers corrosion and the 2:1 electrolysis (Activity 1.8). Rate ideas come later, in Class 12 Chemical Kinetics.

## Chapter 5: Energy, speed and balance

### Opening (glowsticks)

Already strong. ACS 6.4 opens its lesson with the same hot-versus-cold glowstick demo. Source: [ACS 6.4](https://www.acs.org/content/dam/acsorg/msc/downloads/chapter-6/lesson-4/ch6-l4-lesson-plan.pdf).

### 5.1 Where does the energy go?

**Misconceptions:**
- **Energy is "used up" or created.** About two-thirds of students in Brook & Driver said energy is used up or lost. Source: [Kind/RSC thermodynamics](https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-ideas-about-thermodynamics-beyond-appearances/4017807.article).
- **Every reaction gives out heat, and fizz means warmth.** Kind warns teachers not to let students over-generalise that all reactions release heat. Source: [Kind/RSC chemical reactions](https://edu.rsc.org/resources/introducing-chemical-reactions-beyond-appearances/4017789.article).
- **A local clash.** NCERT Class 7 writes neutralisation as "Acid + Base → Salt + Water + Heat" ([gecu102](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/gecu102.pdf)). Yet vinegar plus baking soda, an acid-base reaction, gets *colder* ([ACS 6.7](https://www.acs.org/content/dam/acsorg/msc/downloads/chapter-6/lesson-7/ch6-l7-lesson-plan.pdf)).

**Best demos:**
- ACS 6.7 pairs an endothermic reaction (vinegar plus baking soda) with an exothermic one, then uses energy-arrow diagrams. It also has students feel a hand warmer. Source: [ACS 6.7](https://www.acs.org/content/dam/acsorg/msc/downloads/chapter-6/lesson-7/ch6-l7-lesson-plan.pdf).
- APS "Rusted Out" gives a kitchen-safe exothermic partner. Source: [APS](https://www.aps.org/learning-resources/students/rusted-out).

**Recommendation:** The home box is good, but make it a prediction.
- Open the box with: "Most people expect a fizzing reaction to get warm. Write down your guess before you feel the glass."
- Pair it with an exothermic twin she can feel: vinegar-soaked steel wool sealed in a zip bag (from 4.4) warms up over a few minutes. One fizzes and cools; one sits quietly and warms. That breaks "drama equals heat".
- Add one sentence for an Indian reader: "Your Class 7 book says neutralisation gives out heat. Many acid-base reactions do, but this one takes heat in, because the fizz (the bicarbonate breaking up into CO₂) costs energy."

**NCERT:** Exothermic and endothermic appear in Class 10 Ch 1 ([jesc101](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/jesc101.pdf)). System and surroundings appear in Class 11 Unit 5 ([kech105](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kech105.pdf)).

### 5.2 Why is there an energy barrier?

**Misconceptions:**
- **A reaction that releases a lot of energy must be fast** (the book addresses this).
- **Heating speeds up only endothermic reactions, or slows exothermic ones.** Of pre-service teachers, 30.9% held the second idea. Source: [Çam et al. 2015](https://www.eduhk.hk/apfslt/v16_issue2/cam/page4.htm).

**Best sequence:**
- OpenStax gives the collision-energy-plus-orientation picture, with the share of molecules above Ea growing with temperature. Source: [OpenStax 12.5](https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/12-5-collision-theory).
- ACS 6.4's extension: a match lights a candle, and the heat released keeps the reaction going. Source: [ACS 6.4](https://www.acs.org/content/dam/acsorg/msc/downloads/chapter-6/lesson-4/ch6-l4-lesson-plan.pdf).
- The PhET Reactions & Rates tips note that glancing collisions may fail even with enough energy. Source: [PhET](https://phet.colorado.edu/files/teachers-guide/reactions-and-rates-guide.pdf).

**Recommendation:** Already strong (ball in a dip, struck match, spread of energies).
- Two additions:
  - Build from her school experience. NCERT Class 7 Activity 5.6 (a magnifying glass on paper) teaches "ignition temperature" ([gecu105](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/gecu105.pdf)). Add a sentence saying that ignition temperature is what activation energy looks like from outside.
  - Add a careful line, or extend the existing one: the glowstick reaction gives energy *out*, yet still speeds up when warmed. Warming speeds up exothermic and endothermic reactions alike.
- **Figure:** In the live barrier widget, let her drag a "temperature" slider. It should show the tail of a particle-energy histogram crossing the Ea line (the share above Ea grows sharply), rather than the whole crowd moving.

**NCERT:** Class 7 (ignition temperature). Activation energy and Arrhenius are in Class 12 Chemical Kinetics (NCERT PDF robots-blocked; see [Teachoo listing](https://www.teachoo.com/subjects/chemistry/class-12/chapter-3/)).

### 5.3 How quickly does change happen?

**Misconceptions:**
- **Rate is confused with how much product forms, or with the time taken.** 49.4% of responses on this item showed the confusion. Source: [Çam et al.](https://www.eduhk.hk/apfslt/v16_issue2/cam/page4.htm).
- **Higher concentration means a longer reaction time** (27.2%). Source: [same](https://www.eduhk.hk/apfslt/v16_issue2/cam/page4.htm).

**Best demo:** ACS 6.4 warms one pair of solutions and cools another, then compares how quickly gas and solid form. Source: [ACS 6.4](https://www.acs.org/content/dam/acsorg/msc/downloads/chapter-6/lesson-4/ch6-l4-lesson-plan.pdf).

**Recommendation:** Already strong. The glowstick "brighter but runs out sooner, same total light" line separates rate from amount beautifully.
- Missing piece: surface area is stated but not *seen*. Add a two-minute kitchen race with effervescent tablets (a vitamin-C or antacid fizzy tablet). Drop one whole tablet and one crushed tablet into equal glasses of water, then a whole tablet into hot water versus cold. Ask her to predict the order of finishing.
- Keep the made-up A ⇌ B model; it does the conceptual heavy lifting.

**NCERT:** Class 12 Chemical Kinetics (as above).

### 5.4 A different pathway

**Misconceptions:**
- **A catalyst increases yield.** 40% of GCSE students thought so. Source: [Riddle & Lo-Fan-Hin](https://journals.le.ac.uk/index.php/new-directions/article/download/3900/3677/13531).
- **A catalyst is consumed, or becomes part of the product.** ACS stresses that it is not written among the products. Source: [ACS 6.5](https://www.acs.org/content/dam/acsorg/msc/downloads/chapter-6/lesson-5/ch6-l5-lesson-plan.pdf).
- **Catalyst is confused with intermediate** (37% of pre-service teachers). Source: [Çam et al.](https://www.eduhk.hk/apfslt/v16_issue2/cam/page4.htm).

**Best demo:** Yeast plus hydrogen peroxide plus a drop of detergent foams and warms slightly. Sources: [ACS 6.5](https://www.acs.org/content/dam/acsorg/msc/downloads/chapter-6/lesson-5/ch6-l5-lesson-plan.pdf); food catalase with dilute ("5 volume") peroxide and safety notes in [RSC](https://edu.rsc.org/425.article). OpenStax and MIT 5.111SC L34 confirm that a catalyst lowers Ea without changing K: [OpenStax 13.3](https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/13-3-shifting-equilibria-le-chateliers-principle), [MIT L34](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/5-111sc-principles-of-chemical-science-fall-2014/pages/unit-v-chemical-kinetics/lecture-34-kinetics-catalysts/).

**Recommendation: TOP-4.** The text and figure are accurate and the carbonic anhydrase story is lovely, but there is no hands-on activity. Add a home box "A helper that isn't used up":
- Dilute 3% pharmacy hydrogen peroxide about 1:1 with water.
- In glass 1, add a pinch of dried yeast, and in glass 2, a slice of raw potato. Watch the froth, and feel the glass warm slightly.
- When the froth dies, add fresh peroxide to the *same* yeast: it froths again, so the catalyst is still there.
- **Predict:** "What will a slice of boiled potato do?" Almost nothing, because cooking wrecks the enzyme (the Science Buddies variation, [BioChem_p019](https://www.sciencebuddies.org/science-fair-projects/project_ideas/BioChem_p019.shtml)).
- Adult present, eye protection, no tasting, and pour it away afterwards.

**NCERT:** Class 12 Chemical Kinetics. Catalyst effect on equilibrium: Class 11 Unit 6 §6.8 ([kech106](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kech106.pdf)).

### 5.5 A balance that keeps moving

**Misconceptions:**
- **Equal amounts at equilibrium** (48% of GCSE students; 25% at A-level).
- **The reaction stops** (6–13%).
- **A closed system is part of the definition** (83%).

Source for all three: [Riddle & Lo-Fan-Hin](https://journals.le.ac.uk/index.php/new-directions/article/download/3900/3677/13531).

**Best demo:** The NCERT Class 11 coloured-water transfer activity: two cylinders and two tubes of different bore, moving water both ways until the levels stop changing. Different bores give unequal final levels. NCERT also gives the radioactive-sugar and H/D-ammonia evidence. Source: [kech106](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kech106.pdf).

**Recommendation: TOP-2.** Add a kitchen version: two glasses, a wide straw and a thin straw. Run it as a prediction ("Will they end level?"), then reveal unequal but steady levels with water still moving.
- The Mumbai local-platform analogy is already strong; keep it.
- **Accuracy fix (deuterium paragraph).** As written, adding D₂ to an equilibrium mixture does change the amounts slightly, because it is extra hydrogen. NCERT's cleaner version is the one to use: make one equilibrium mixture with H₂ and an identical one with D₂, then mix them. The amounts stay the same, yet NH₂D, NHD₂ and HD appear ([kech106](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kech106.pdf)). An even simpler, kid-sized parallel: drop one sugar crystal of a different colour (or flavour) into a saturated sugar solution. This is the same logic as NCERT's radioactive-sugar evidence, but frame it as a thought experiment.

**NCERT:** Class 11 Unit 6 Equilibrium ([kech106](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kech106.pdf)).

### 5.6 Breaking bonds costs; making bonds pays

**Misconceptions:**
- **Bond breaking releases energy, and energy is "stored in" fuels.** Only 6% of 16-year-olds in Barker's study linked combustion energy to bond formation at first; about 50% did 15 months later, and some kept "stored in methane". Source: [Kind/RSC thermodynamics](https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-ideas-about-thermodynamics-beyond-appearances/4017807.article).
- **The same error persists in university biology ("high-energy bonds" in ATP).** Source: [arXiv 1209.0994](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1209.0994) (title seen in search, not read).

**Best sequence:**
- Kind/RSC suggest the "molecular murder" model-pulling activity, plus calling the fuel a "fuel–oxygen system", not just "fuel". Source: [Kind/RSC](https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-ideas-about-thermodynamics-beyond-appearances/4017807.article).
- ACS 6.7 uses "energy arrows" for break and make. Source: [ACS 6.7](https://www.acs.org/content/dam/acsorg/msc/downloads/chapter-6/lesson-7/ch6-l7-lesson-plan.pdf).
- OpenStax states that separating bonded atoms always requires energy. Source: [OpenStax 7.5](https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/7-5-strengths-of-ionic-and-covalent-bonds).

**Recommendation:** Already strong. It has the magnet and rubber-band analogies, two worked ledgers and the right careful box ("fuels together with oxygen").
- One structural point: exothermic and endothermic are introduced in 5.1, but the bond explanation waits until 5.6. Meanwhile 5.1's wording ("products sit lower") may let "energy stored in the fuel" set in.
- Add a one-line forward pointer in 5.1: "Where does that energy come from? Not from bonds breaking. Section 5.6 shows it is the other way round." This plants the conflict early.
- **Figure:** `c5_bond_ledger` could animate as a coin ledger. Coins go *in* to pull atoms apart, more coins come *out* as O–H bonds snap together, and the leftover pile drops into the "surroundings" as heat.

**NCERT:** Class 11 Unit 5 Thermodynamics, bond enthalpy ([kech105](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kech105.pdf)).

### 5.7 Disturbing an equilibrium

**Misconceptions:**
- **The mixture "fights back" on purpose, or fully cancels the stress.** MIT's lecture stresses that it is only partly offset. Source: [MIT 5.111SC L19 transcript](https://live.ocw.mit.edu/courses/5-111sc-principles-of-chemical-science-fall-2014/c94f44a29a7a952b86fe8fac40e8f708_AVL5AwJrrEU.pdf).
- **Changing amounts changes K.** The book addresses this.
- **Catalysts shift the position** (40%). Source: [Riddle & Lo-Fan-Hin](https://journals.le.ac.uk/index.php/new-directions/article/download/3900/3677/13531).

**Best examples:**
- OpenStax uses the carbonated soft drink as an equilibrium example. Source: [OpenStax 13.3](https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/13-3-shifting-equilibria-le-chateliers-principle).
- MIT L19 uses haemoglobin at altitude: low O₂ pushes oxyhaemoglobin backwards, and the body responds by making more haemoglobin. Source: [MIT L19](https://live.ocw.mit.edu/courses/5-111sc-principles-of-chemical-science-fall-2014/c94f44a29a7a952b86fe8fac40e8f708_AVL5AwJrrEU.pdf).
- NCERT gives the Fe³⁺/SCN⁻ colour demo. Source: [kech106](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kech106.pdf).

**Recommendation:** The rate-based explanation is already strong and honest ("not fighting back"). The Fe³⁺/SCN⁻ demo is lab-only, so add a kitchen one.
- **Soda bottle demo.** A sealed bottle of soda shows no bubbles, because dissolved and gaseous CO₂ are balanced. Open it and bubbles stream out: you removed the gas above, so the forward direction wins. Re-cap it and the bubbling soon stops. Cold soda keeps its fizz longer, which ties back to temperature.
- **Real-world hook.** For a Mumbai reader, add the altitude example: why trekkers in Ladakh feel breathless at first and acclimatise over days.

**NCERT:** Class 11 Unit 6 §6.8 ([kech106](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kech106.pdf)).

### 6.1 Ratios tell a story

**Misconceptions:**
- **Atom ratio is taken as mass ratio**, so water is thought to be "mostly hydrogen".

**Best demos:**

**Recommendation:** Already strong. The "almost nine-tenths of every glass is oxygen" punchline is the right confrontation.

### 6.2 Counting beyond what we can see

**Misconceptions:**
- **The mole is a mass, or a "portion"**, rather than a number. The word has historically carried three meanings (Dierks).
- **Avogadro's number is unimaginable.**
- **A heavier substance's mole contains more particles.** The book addresses this.

Source: [Kind/RSC stoichiometry](https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-difficulties-with-stoichiometry-beyond-appearances/4017805.article).

**Best demos:** Oklahoma State ChemSource:
- the "molar display" (one mole of several substances side by side: equal counts, different heaps)
- piles of ½, 1, 2 and 5 moles of salt to estimate, then weigh
- the paper-stack-to-Pluto scale image

Source: [okstate](https://intro.chem.okstate.edu/ChemSource/Moles/mole13.htm). AACT's "Bring me a mole" uses the same idea ([AACT](https://teachchemistry.org/classroom-resources/bring-me-a-mole); member page, summary only).

**Recommendation:** Already strong. The dozen analogy, the "always say what the things are" rule and the dal-counting home box ("counting by weighing") are excellent.
- Add a short "kitchen mole display" next to the dal box. Each heap holds the *same* number of particles:
  - one mole of water: 18 mL, about one and a quarter tablespoons
  - one mole of salt: about 58 g, roughly three tablespoons
  - one mole of sugar: 342 g, well over a cup
- These spoon equivalents are our own arithmetic from molar masses and typical kitchen densities.
- The molar display makes "same count, different heap" visible ([okstate](https://intro.chem.okstate.edu/ChemSource/Moles/mole13.htm)).

**NCERT:** The mole is **not** in the new Class 9 book ([iesc109](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/iesc109.pdf)). It first appears in Class 11 Unit 1 ([kech101](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kech101.pdf)). This chapter is therefore well ahead of her school syllabus, so extra concreteness pays off.

### 6.3 From moles to mass

**Misconceptions:**
- **Multiplying the molar mass by the equation coefficient** (about 40%).

Source: [Kind/RSC](https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-difficulties-with-stoichiometry-beyond-appearances/4017805.article).

**Recommendation:** Already strong. Units cancelling "like fractions" and the mass-number careful box are good.
- Add one careful box for the 40% error: "The molar mass of water is 18 g/mol whether the equation says 2 H₂O or 10 H₂O. It belongs to the substance, not to the equation. The coefficient tells you how many moles, not a new molar mass."
- `c6_unit_chain` could animate units striking through as they cancel.

**NCERT:** Class 11 Unit 1 ([kech101](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kech101.pdf)). Molecular mass appears in Class 9 ([iesc109](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/iesc109.pdf)).

### 6.4 Which reactant runs out first?

**Misconceptions:**
- **The reactant you have less of is always limiting.**
- **Reactions run until everything is used up.**
- **Students only try "perfect recipe" amounts.**

Sources: [RSC CPD](https://edu.rsc.org/cpd/teaching-conservation-of-mass-at-14-16/4020546.article), [PhET RPAL guide](https://phet.colorado.edu/files/teachers-guide/reactants-products-and-leftovers-html-guide_en.pdf), [Kind (BouJaoude & Barakat)](https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-difficulties-with-stoichiometry-beyond-appearances/4017805.article).

**Best sequence and demos:**
- PhET's sandwich screen comes first, then molecules. PhET found students progress faster when prompted to go *back* to the sandwiches. - ACS 6.2: with fixed vinegar, adding more baking soda eventually stops giving more gas. Source: [ACS 6.2](https://www.acs.org/content/dam/acsorg/msc/downloads/chapter-6/lesson-2/ch6-l2-lesson-plan.pdf).

  - Put ¼, ½, 1 and 2 teaspoons of baking soda into four balloons, stretch them over the necks, then tip the soda in.

- Live widget: let her set the recipe ratio as well as the amounts, following PhET's custom-recipe idea.

## Chapter 7: Acids, bases and passing protons

### Opening (cabbage, Boyle, acid and base notes)

**Misconceptions:**
- **Acids are the dangerous ones and bases are harmless.** Dilute alkalis are in fact more corrosive to many materials than dilute acids. Students also know far fewer bases. Source: [Kind/RSC acids and bases](https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-thinking-about-acids-bases-and-neutralisation-beyond-appearances/4017804.article).

**Recommendation:**
- **(a) Safety.** The opening describes bases by feel and taste ("feel slippery and taste bitter"). Add a safety line beside the "never taste" note: the most dangerous chemicals in a kitchen cupboard are bases (drain cleaner, caustic soda, and *chuna*). Following Kind, this also breaks "bases are safe".
- **(b) Build from her experience.** NCERT Class 7 uses turmeric as an indicator: it turns red in base ([gecu102](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/gecu102.pdf)). Most Indian children have seen a yellow haldi or dal stain turn red-brown when scrubbed with soap. One line, "that was chemistry", gives an instant personal anchor before the cabbage.
- The cabbage opening itself is already strong.

### 7.1 What makes a solution acidic?

**Best demos and sequences:**

- ACS 6.8 animates water passing protons to make H₃O⁺ and OH⁻. Source: [ACS 6.8](https://www.acs.org/content/dam/acsorg/msc/downloads/chapter-6/lesson-8/ch6-l8-lesson-plan.pdf).
- PhET Acid-Base Solutions: introduce real names (HCl) before generic HA. Source: [PhET guide](https://phet.colorado.edu/files/teachers-guide/acid-base-solutions-html-guide_en.pdf).

**Recommendation:** Already strong. The conductivity clue, the proton-transfer figure and live widget, the ammonia example and the "no nucleus is broken" careful box are all good.
- Add a careful box for Barker's 40%: "In hydrochloric acid, are there HCl molecules floating about?" Answer: essentially none; that is what "strong" will mean in 7.2. This also sets up the next section.

**NCERT:** Class 7 Ch 2 has no H⁺ ([gecu102](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/gecu102.pdf)). Class 10 Ch 2 covers H⁺ and H₃O⁺ ([jesc102](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/jesc102.pdf)). Brønsted–Lowry is in Class 11 Unit 6 ([kech106](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kech106.pdf)).

### 7.2 Strong or concentrated?

**Misconceptions:**
- **A stronger acid must be more concentrated.**
- **pH measures strength** (6 of 16 students).
- **Strength is confused with how fast an acid "eats" things.**

Sources: [PhET Acid-Base guide](https://phet.colorado.edu/files/teachers-guide/acid-base-solutions-html-guide_en.pdf), [Sheppard 2006](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2006/rp/b5rp90014j), [Kind/RSC](https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-thinking-about-acids-bases-and-neutralisation-beyond-appearances/4017804.article).

**Best sequence:** Kind suggests serial 1:10 dilutions of strong and weak acids, compared with concentrated *non*-acids such as salt or sugar. Source: [Kind/RSC](https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-thinking-about-acids-bases-and-neutralisation-beyond-appearances/4017804.article). PhET's challenge prompt: evaluate "Strong acids always have a lower pH than weak acids." Source: [PhET](https://phet.colorado.edu/files/teachers-guide/acid-base-solutions-html-guide_en.pdf).

**Recommendation:** Already strong. The classroom/hands-up analogy, the measured numbers and the dilute-not-weaker careful box are good. Two refinements:
- **(a) Accuracy nuance.** For a weak acid, the *fraction* ionised rises as you dilute it, while Ka (the true strength) stays fixed ([OpenStax 14.3](https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/14-3-relative-strengths-of-acids-and-bases)). Defining strength as "what fraction hand over their protons" therefore clashes slightly with "never weaker".
  - Suggested wording: "Strength is how readily an acid's molecules give up protons, which is fixed for each acid. At the same concentration, a stronger acid hands over a far bigger fraction."
- **(b) Add the PhET challenge as a check question**: "True or false: a strong acid always has a lower pH than a weak one." False; the chapter's own case proves it.
- **(c) Optional analogy that carries through to the case and buffers.** A weak acid keeps most of its protons "in the bank" (whole molecules). pH only counts the cash in hand (H₃O⁺). A titration counts the whole fortune, bank included. This is our own analogy; no source. It makes the "two bottles at pH 3" resolution and the buffer's "reserve" feel inevitable.

**NCERT:** Class 10 Ch 2 contrasts equal-molarity HCl and acetic acid ([jesc102](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/jesc102.pdf)). Class 11 Unit 6 covers strength ([kech106](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kech106.pdf)).

### 7.3 Every step on the pH scale is tenfold

**Misconceptions:**
- **pH is linear.** Most students thought so; only 6 of 16 recognised the log scale.
- **Indicators change colour at pH 7** (14 of 16).
- **Neutral means "no ions".**

Source: [Sheppard 2006](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2006/rp/b5rp90014j). PhET also found that bar graphs led students to call 100-fold gaps "twice as large", and that students think colour *is* pH ([PhET pH Scale guide](https://phet.colorado.edu/files/teachers-guide/ph-scale-html-guide_en.pdf)).

**Best ideas:**
- PhET's design choices: log/linear toggle, a sliding scale instead of bars, and hiding the pH so students predict before seeing it ([PhET guide](https://phet.colorado.edu/files/teachers-guide/ph-scale-html-guide_en.pdf)).
- MIT 5.111SC Lecture 21 opens with "would you drink water at pH 2 or pH 11?" ([MIT L21](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/5-111sc-principles-of-chemical-science-fall-2014/pages/unit-iii-thermodynamics-chemical-equilibrium/lecture-21)). This is a good prediction hook; for a child, the answer is neither.
- Cabbage colour chart: red at pH 2, purple at 4, violet at 6, blue at 8, blue-green at 10, greenish-yellow at 12 ([Science Buddies](https://www.sciencebuddies.org/stem-activities/cabbage-pH-indicator)).

**Recommendation: TOP-3.**
- **(a)** Reword "red-pink for strong acids, pink-purple for weak ones" by pH.
- **(b)** Give `c7_ph_ladder` a tenfold dot-block staircase and a cabbage colour band.
- **(c)** The careful box ("pH 2 is not twice pH 4") is already strong; keep it.
- **(d)** The rainbow home box is good. Add PhET's hide-then-predict move: "Before adding each one, guess its colour."

**NCERT:** Class 10 Ch 2, pH scale and universal indicator ([jesc102](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/jesc102.pdf)).

### 7.4 Titration curves and buffers

**Misconceptions:**
- **The flat early part of the curve means no reaction has started** (about half of students).
- **The curve should be linear** (8 of 16; only 2 predicted the S-shape).
- **Neutralisation is just mixing.**
- **"The acid overpowers the base."**

Source: [Sheppard 2006](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2006/rp/b5rp90014j). Neutralisation is seen as the acid "losing" its acidity rather than reacting ([Kind/RSC](https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-thinking-about-acids-bases-and-neutralisation-beyond-appearances/4017804.article)).

**Best demos:**
- ACS 6.9: count drops of base to neutralise. A more concentrated acid needs more drops ([ACS 6.9](https://www.acs.org/content/dam/acsorg/msc/downloads/chapter-6/lesson-9/ch6-l9-lesson-plan.pdf)).
- Kind: record the temperature during the titration; the maximum is at neutral ([Kind/RSC](https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-thinking-about-acids-bases-and-neutralisation-beyond-appearances/4017804.article)).
- MIT RES.HS-002: dissolving CO₂ (dry ice) turns basic indicator solutions acidic ([MIT transcript](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-hs-002-chemistry-behind-the-magic-chemical-demonstrations-for-the-classroom/nAhIrh9vM4A_transcript.pdf)).
- OpenStax confirms pH = pKa = 4.74 at half-equivalence ([OpenStax 14.7](https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/14-7-acid-base-titrations)).

**Recommendation: TOP-5.**
- **(a)** Sketch-your-prediction prompt before Figure 7.4.
- **(b)** In the kitchen titration, replace "At first nothing seems to happen" with an observation that shows the reaction *is* happening: the fizz on every spoonful. It is the colour that is being held steady.
- **(c)** A side-by-side kitchen buffer demo:
  - Glass A: cabbage juice in water. Glass B: cabbage juice with a pinch of baking soda.
  - Add lemon juice drop by drop to both. A flips pink almost at once; B holds its colour for many drops, then gives way.
  - This shows both buffering and capacity ("A buffer stops the pH from changing at all" is already well handled).
- **(d)** Breath-into-blue-cabbage (MIT's CO₂ idea) as the bridge to Henderson's blood buffer: "the acid your muscles pour into your blood is largely carbonic acid from CO₂, the same gas you just blew into the glass."
  - Our phrasing. Henderson's carbonic acid/hydrogencarbonate buffer is already in the text.
