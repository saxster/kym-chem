## TOP 5 CHANGES (priority order)

**1. Ch 3 (opening, 3.1 and 3.3): make "what is in the bubbles?" a full predict–test–explain episode, and add a "boil without a flame" home demo.**
- **The misconception.** It is one of the best-documented in school chemistry. Children aged 8–17 say the bubbles are air, oxygen, hydrogen or "heat" (Osborne & Cosgrove 1983; Mayer 2011, both cited in the [NSTA "What's in the Bubbles?" probe](https://www.nsta.org/lesson-plan/whats-bubbles)). In a 696-student survey, 25% said water molecules split into hydrogen and oxygen when water evaporates ([Özalp & Kahveci 2015, CERP](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2015/rp/c5rp00096c)).
- **What the book already does well.** The opener invites a guess, and 3.3 answers it with the cold-lid clue.
- **What is missing.** She never gets the decisive experience. Add an adult-run :::home: pull back the plunger of a sealed syringe of hot tap water, and it boils with no flame at all (procedure in [Earthlearningidea "Pressure reduction"](https://www.earthlearningidea.com/PDF/338_Pressure_reduction.pdf); see also [AlgebraLab syringe inquiry](https://algebralab.org/activities/science-boilingwatersyringe)).
  - Pushing the plunger back in makes the bubbles vanish into liquid again. That rules out "air got in" and "the water split up".
  - No heat was added, so it also rules out "heat breaks the molecules".
- **Figure.** Add a zoom-in "bubble" to c3_boil_vs_split or to the states widget. Show a pocket inside the liquid that holds a few whole, widely spaced H₂O molecules and nothing else between them.

**2. Ch 3.1–3.2: turn the `states` widget from three tabs into one heat slider, and make two invisible things visible: empty space and motion in the solid.**
- **Empty space.** About 25% of 13–14-year-olds fill the space between particles with air or "stuff" (Novick & Nussbaum, summarised in [RSC *Beyond Appearances*: particulate nature of matter](https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-ideas-about-the-particulate-nature-of-matter-beyond-appearances/4017763.article)).
- **Stillness.** About 32% think atoms in a solid do not move. About 31.6% think molecules stop moving when water freezes ([Özalp & Kahveci 2015](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2015/rp/c5rp00096c); [IOP Spark](https://spark.iop.org/many-students-think-atoms-do-not-move-solid)).
- **Size.** About half of 18-year-olds thought steam molecules are bigger than ice molecules (Griffiths & Preston, same RSC page).
- **What the book does now.** The text handles these ideas well in words (the 3.1 careful box). The live figure is a tab switch (live/w_states.js), so it implies three separate pictures rather than one continuous story.
- **The change.** Follow [PhET *States of Matter*](https://phet.colorado.edu/en/simulations/states-of-matter) (design notes: [teacher guide PDF](https://phet.colorado.edu/files/teachers-guide/states-of-matter-html-guide_en.pdf)):
  - Use one heat/cool control.
  - Add a small temperature–time trace. It should flatten at the melting and boiling points, which is Joseph Black's plateau, so the chapter opener pays off visually.
  - Make the solid visibly jiggle.
  - Add a toggle that labels the gaps "nothing here, not air".
  - Add a ruler or outline that proves one molecule is the same size in all three states.

**3. Ch 3.5: add the missing :::careful and :::home.**
- **The gap.** 3.5 is the only section in these chapters with neither box, and dissolving is where particle ideas break down most.
- **"Sugar melts" in water.** About 35% of students say this. About 23% call dissolving a chemical reaction ([Özalp & Kahveci 2015](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2015/rp/c5rp00096c)).
- **"The solute disappears and its mass goes."** Learners often believe the solute disappears and do not conserve mass ([RSC "Mass and dissolving" diagnostic](https://edu.rsc.org/resources/mass-and-dissolving/1084.article)).
- **The changes.**
  - Add a careful box contrasting *melting* (one substance, needs heat) with *dissolving* (two substances, happens in cold water).
  - Add a home activity: weigh a glass of water plus a separate spoonful of salt on a kitchen scale, then stir it in and weigh again. The mass is the same, matching NCERT Class 9 Activity 9.1 ([iesc109](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/iesc109.pdf)).
  - Add a second home activity: a full glass with sugar stirred in barely rises. That is NCERT Class 8 Activity 7.7 ([hecu107](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/hecu107.pdf)) and evidence for spaces between particles.
- **A Mumbai anchor for dilution.** Use nimbu-pani or Rooh Afza: the bottle concentrate versus the glass. The same amount of syrup is spread through more water.

**4. Ch 2.5: break the "salt molecule" / "electron-giving partners" idea explicitly.**
- **What students believe.** Taber's research shows they treat an Na⁺ and the Cl⁻ that received "its" electron as a bonded pair. All other neighbours are thought to be held by "just forces" (the *history conjecture* and the *molecular framework*; [Taber, "Misunderstanding the ionic bond"](https://science-education-research.com/publications/articles/misunderstanding-the-ionic-bond/); [ECLIPSE summary](https://science-education-research.com/ECLIPSE/molecular%20framework.html)).
- **What Taber advises.** Separate *ion formation* from *ionic bonding*. Show crystals that formed without any electron transfer.
- **What the book does now.** The text has the right lattice picture and the line "No ion belongs to any one partner". But its sequence opens with "each sodium atom passes one electron to a chlorine atom", which is exactly the pseudo-molecular story Taber warns about.
- **The changes.**
  - Add a :::careful: "Which chloride is 'its' partner?" The answer is none. All six neighbours pull equally.
  - Point back to the 2.1 "Get the salt back" home activity. The cubes that grew on the saucer formed from ions that were already ions. None of those Na⁺ ever gave an electron to the Cl⁻ beside it, yet the crystal holds together just as well.
  - In live/lattice3d, add a tap-an-ion mode that highlights all six equal neighbours.

**5. Ch 0.1 → 1.1: build one "inference engine" that runs from the first chapter to Rutherford.**
- **What students struggle with.** They find inference about the invisible hard, and they treat models as photographs. Many think particles can be seen under a microscope (Griffiths & Preston 1992, Lee et al. 1993, cited in [Özalp & Kahveci 2015](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2015/rp/c5rp00096c)). Textbook drawings mislead about size and spacing ([IOP Spark on Andersson 1990](https://spark.iop.org/andersson-1990)).
- **The activity.** Add a "mystery box" :::home to 0.1: a shoebox with something taped inside, which she explores by tilting it so a marble rolls and listening. Models: [Berkeley *Understanding Science* Mystery Tubes](https://undsci.berkeley.edu/lessons/mystery_tubes.html) and [CERN scattering boxes](https://educational-resources.web.cern.ch/node/85).
- **The payoff in 1.1.** First ask her to *predict* what the plum-pudding atom would do to alpha particles. Then reveal Geiger and Marsden's result, and call back: "you did this with a marble and a shoebox".
- **Precedent.** [MIT 5.111SC Lecture 2](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/5-111sc-principles-of-chemical-science-fall-2014/pages/unit-i-the-atom/lecture-2) recreates Rutherford with ping-pong balls fired at foam balls on strings. This is the same idea at kitchen scale.

## Chapter 0: The chemist's way of seeing

### 0.1 What we see and what we think
1. **Misconceptions.**
   - Particle diagrams are taken as photographs, so atoms "could be seen with a good enough microscope" (several studies cited in [Özalp & Kahveci 2015](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2015/rp/c5rp00096c)).
   - Particles are given bulk properties: water molecules are wet, copper atoms are shiny and orange. Nearly half of 15-year-olds in one study did this ([IOP Spark](https://spark.iop.org/some-students-think-particles-possess-same-properties-materials-they-compose); [RSC *Beyond Appearances*: elements, compounds, mixtures](https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-ideas-about-the-differences-between-elements-compounds-and-mixtures-beyond-appearances/4017771.article)).
   - The space between particles is filled with air ([RSC *Beyond Appearances*: particles](https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-ideas-about-the-particulate-nature-of-matter-beyond-appearances/4017763.article)).
2. **Best sequence and demos.**
   - ACS *Middle School Chemistry* Lesson 1.1, "Molecules Matter" ([lesson plan PDF](https://www.acs.org/content/dam/acsorg/msc/downloads/chapter-1/lesson-1/ch1-l1-lesson-plan.pdf)) uses exactly our demo: an unstirred food-colouring drop.
   - ACS Lesson 1.2, "Molecules in Motion" ([PDF](https://www.acs.org/content/dam/acsorg/msc/downloads/chapter-1/lesson-2/ch1-l2-ngss.pdf)), escalates to **hot versus cold water side by side**.
   - Mystery tubes and boxes teach inference directly ([Berkeley](https://undsci.berkeley.edu/lessons/mystery_tubes.html); [CERN](https://educational-resources.web.cern.ch/node/85)).
   - RSC suggests saying openly that invisibility makes "continuous" thinking natural, and that scientists themselves took about 2,000 years to accept atoms ([RSC](https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-ideas-about-the-particulate-nature-of-matter-beyond-appearances/4017763.article)). Our Democritus/Ostwald arc already does this.
3. **Recommendation.**
   - **Already strong:** the two-column figure (see_vs_infer, with "real molecules have no colour"), the detective analogy, the three views (bench, particle, symbol), and the light-wavelength careful box.
   - **Add:**
     - (a) Upgrade the :::home to two glasses, one hot tap water and one with ice water. She *predicts first* which spreads faster, then observes. The inference "something invisible is moving faster" becomes hers, and it sets up 3.2.
     - (b) Add the mystery-box activity (Top-5 #5).
     - (c) In the see_vs_infer figure, add one explicit "not air" label to the gap between molecules, so the very first particle picture she sees does not plant "air between particles".
4. **NCERT.**
   - Observation, inference and models: Class 9 Ch 1 ([iesc101](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/iesc101.pdf)).
   - "Diagrams are models, not exact pictures", plus KMnO₄ diffusion and the hot, room-temperature and ice-cold comparison: Class 8 Ch 7 ([hecu107](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/hecu107.pdf)).

### 0.2 Powers of ten: from a flask to an atom
1. **Misconceptions.**
   - Children's sense of size breaks down at the edge of what the eye can see. Below it, "very small" collapses into one category, so a cell, a virus and an atom seem about equally tiny. Relative comparisons work better than absolute sizes ([Tretter 2004, scale and scaling](https://istardb.org/aerpublications/conceptions-of-scale-and-scaling-the-expert-novice-continuum/)).
   - The mole is notoriously hard. "Mole" gets used to mean a mass, a portion of substance and a number interchangeably (Dierks, summarised in [RSC *Beyond Appearances*: stoichiometry](https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-difficulties-with-stoichiometry-beyond-appearances/4017805.article); review by [Furió et al. 2002, CERP](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2002/rp/b2rp90023h)).
2. **Best sequence and demos.**
   - Continuous zoom tools beat static ladders for crossing the visibility boundary. Examples are the Eames film [*Powers of Ten*](https://eamesdesigns.com/powers-of-ten) and the Utah Learn.Genetics "Cell Size and Scale" slider ([teacher page](https://teach.genetics.utah.edu/content/cells/); Indian mirror at [IISER Pune STEM-ready](https://stemready.acads.iiserpune.ac.in/modules/mathematics/scales-measurement/cell-size-and-scale-simulation)).
   - For the mole, Kind's sequence builds the idea before the number: ratios, then counting by mass, then Avogadro's number, then maths last ([RSC](https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-difficulties-with-stoichiometry-beyond-appearances/4017805.article)).
3. **Recommendation.**
   - **Already strong:** the rice-seller "count by weighing" analogy, the worked drop, the dropper home activity, and "bigger amount, not bigger molecules".
   - **Add:**
     - (a) Make scale_ladder zoomable, or give it a live version: a slider through ten steps with the frame staying fixed, so the visibility boundary is felt rather than read. Mark the step where "your eye gives up" and the step where "a light microscope gives up". That ties back to the 0.1 light-wavelength box.
     - (b) Add a five-minute "count by weighing" warm-up before the mole: weigh 100 grains of rice, then estimate how many grains are in a 1 kg packet. It is her own experience of what the mole is for.
     - (c) The Brownian figure (c0_brownian plus live brownian with "Show the water molecules") matches the NSTA design, where students hide the small particles to see the big one still jiggle ([NSTA, "Evidence of molecular motion"](https://www.nsta.org/science-teacher/science-teacher-novemberdecember-2019/evidence-molecular-motion)). **Already strong.** The same article's coin-flip random walk on floor tiles would make a lovely :::home. Real Brownian motion of milk-fat droplets needs about 400× magnification, so it is not feasible at home without a microscope.
4. **NCERT.**
   - Estimation: Class 9 Ch 1 ([iesc101](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/iesc101.pdf)).
   - Mole, Avogadro's number and molarity: Class 11 Unit 1 ([kech101](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kech101.pdf)). I did not find the mole in the parts of the new Class 9 Ch 9 I could read ([iesc109](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/iesc109.pdf)), so our 0.2 runs several years ahead of school. That is fine, but keep it conceptual.
   - Diffusion: Class 8 Ch 7.

## Chapter 1: What makes an atom?

### 1.1 Inside an atom
1. **Misconceptions.**
   - Electrons orbit like planets, and students mix planetary, wavy-path and cloud pictures into hybrids ([IOP Spark](https://spark.iop.org/many-students-hold-range-atom-models-including-hybrid-models-consisting-combinations-different)).
   - Textbook pictures give false ideas of scale, with the nucleus drawn huge ([IOP Spark, Andersson 1990](https://spark.iop.org/andersson-1990)).
   - Atoms carry the properties of the bulk material, for example gold atoms are shiny (Stepans 2003, in [Özalp & Kahveci 2015](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2015/rp/c5rp00096c)).
2. **Best sequence and demos.**
   - Rutherford by analogy: ping-pong balls at foam "nuclei" ([MIT 5.111SC L2](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/5-111sc-principles-of-chemical-science-fall-2014/pages/unit-i-the-atom/lecture-2)).
   - Marbles rolled at the base of a hidden wine glass ([Perimeter Institute](https://perimeterinstitute.ca/news/perimeter-classroom-activity-rutherford-scattering-with-marbles)).
   - CERN scattering boxes ([CERN](https://educational-resources.web.cern.ch/node/85)).
   - PhET's *Build an Atom* deliberately offers both an orbit view and a cloud view, and warns that neither is to scale ([teacher guide](https://phet.colorado.edu/files/teachers-guide/build-an-atom-html-guide_en.pdf)).
3. **Recommendation.**
   - **Already strong:** Thomson to Rutherford told as a story, the stadium-and-pea image, the salt-grain walk outdoors, and "counting model" named as a model with its limits stated.
   - **Add:**
     - (a) A prediction prompt *before* the Manchester reveal: "If the atom were a soft pudding, what would the alpha particles do?" The surprise only lands if she has committed to an answer.
     - (b) The goldfoil widget readout already says the nuclei are drawn far too big. **Already strong.** Consider adding a "predict, then fire 1,000" button that asks how many will bounce back before it runs.
     - (c) The salt-grain walk is excellent. Add one sentence telling her to *look back* at the grain and ask where the rest of the atom's mass is. Almost all of it is in that grain.
4. **NCERT.** Thomson, Rutherford, Bohr, Chadwick: Class 9 Ch 8 ([iesc108](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/iesc108.pdf)). It suggests the PhET Rutherford and isotope sims. Note that NCERT calls Rutherford's model "planetary", so our "not tracks" warning matters.

### 1.2 What identifies an element?
1. **Misconceptions.**
   - Students treat full-shell species as stable in all circumstances. That is what drives "Na⁺ has neon's electrons, so it is like neon" ([Taber 2003, ionisation energy conceptions, CERP](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2003/rp/b3rp90010j); [Taber, octet framework](https://science-education-research.com/?p=5029)).
   - Students cannot tell atoms of an element from the element itself, and some think an element can be split further ([RSC *Beyond Appearances*](https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-ideas-about-the-differences-between-elements-compounds-and-mixtures-beyond-appearances/4017771.article)).
   - Atoms are thought to be transformed or created in chemical reactions. About 40% of US middle and high-school students chose these options on AAAS items ([AAAS news](https://www.aaas.org/news/us-students-and-science-aaas-testing-gives-new-insight-what-they-know-and-their-misconceptions)).
2. **Best sequence.** PhET *Build an Atom* puts the element name, net charge and mass number on screen together as particles are dragged in. Its game drills "protons decide the element" ([teacher guide](https://phet.colorado.edu/files/teachers-guide/build-an-atom-html-guide_en.pdf)).
3. **Recommendation.**
   - **Already strong:** the gold/mercury hook, Moseley's staircase, the identity_strip figure (electron, neutron, proton changes), the alchemy payoff ("chemistry never touches the nucleus") and the Na⁺ careful box, which answers the chapter question.
   - **Accuracy check.** The Marathon Fusion claim checks out against reporting dated 21–22 October 2026 ([Business Today](https://www.businesstoday.in/technology/story/mercury-to-gold-marathon-fusion-claims-10x-cern-record-but-can-modern-alchemy-work-563022-2026-10-22)). The mechanism matches: Hg-198 (n,2n) Hg-197, then electron capture to Au-197. Data not peer-reviewed, as the book says.
   - **Add:** since 40% of students think reactions transform atoms, add one :::check here: "When a candle burns, do any carbon atoms become different atoms?"
4. **NCERT.**
   - Atomic number and mass number: Class 9 Ch 8.
   - Elements made of identical atoms: Class 8 Ch 8 ([hecu108](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/hecu108.pdf)).

### 1.3 Same element, different isotope
1. **Misconceptions.**
   - Average atomic mass is read as the mass of a real atom. The book itself flags this.
   - Isotopes are assumed to be radioactive, or to be different elements. Students also hold odd ideas about which nuclei can be radioactive ([IOP Spark](https://spark.iop.org/some-students-think-only-large-nuclei-can-be-radioactive)).
2. **Best sequence and demos.**
   - PhET *Isotopes and Atomic Mass*: build "My Mix", then compare it with "Nature's Mix" ([teacher guide](https://phet.colorado.edu/files/teachers-guide/isotopes-and-atomic-mass-html-guide_en.pdf)).
   - The classic "Beanium" lab: a bag of two or three bean types, where each type is an isotope, and you weigh to find the average ([AACT](https://teachchemistry.org/classroom-resources/beanium-isotopes)).
3. **Recommendation.**
   - **Already strong:** the Aston story, the 2.4-children analogy, the mass_spectrum_cl figure with the average falling *between* peaks, and the clear statement that "the app does not tell you stability".
   - **Add a kitchen "Beanium" :::home.** Mix 75 rajma and 25 chana (or two sizes of dal). Weigh 10 of each kind on a kitchen scale and work out the average bean mass. Then ask whether any single bean in the bowl weighs that much. Her hands do the same arithmetic as the chlorine example.
4. **NCERT.** Isotopes (C-12, C-13, C-14) and isobars: Class 9 Ch 8 ([iesc108](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/iesc108.pdf)). Note that NCERT also teaches **isobars**, which we do not. That is fine, but she may ask.

### 1.4 Where does charge come from?
1. **Misconceptions.**
   - Pupils think both positive and negative charges move when objects are rubbed. In fact only electrons move ([Oak National Academy KS3 lesson](https://thenational.academy/teachers/programmes/science-secondary-ks3/units/series-circuits/lessons/static-electricity-charge)).
   - "Charge = protons − neutrons" is already a careful box in the book.
2. **Best demos.**
   - ACS *Middle School Chemistry* Ch 4 static demos: a rubbed plastic strip is attracted to a finger, two rubbed strips repel, and a balloon picks up paper by induced charge ([ACS Ch 4 reading](https://www.acs.org/content/dam/acsorg/msc/downloads/chapter-4/ch4-student-reading.pdf)).
   - PhET *Balloons and Static Electricity* ([sim](https://phet.colorado.edu/en/simulations/balloons-and-static-electricity)) is the matching sim. I could not load its page text to check how it draws charge transfer, so check it before citing it.
3. **Recommendation.**
   - **Already strong:** the salt-and-pepper balloon *before* the explanation (phenomenon first), the subtraction rule, Faraday's ions, and "you can write it, but can it exist?".
   - **Add:**
     - (a) A second balloon so she sees *repulsion* too. Hang two rubbed balloons on threads and they push apart. Otherwise "charge" only ever means "sticks".
     - (b) One sentence in the home box: "only electrons moved; your hair is left with a positive charge because it has lost some". Optional proof: the balloon now pulls hair strands towards it.
4. **NCERT.**
   - Charge from friction (lightning): Class 8 Ch 6 ([hecu106](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/hecu106.pdf)).
   - Cations and anions: Class 9 Ch 9 ([iesc109](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/iesc109.pdf)). The atom chapter, Class 9 Ch 8, does not cover ions.

### 1.5 Reading an atom's identity
1. **Misconception.** The corners of the symbol get swapped. PhET's guide notes that some periodic tables reverse the positions of mass number and atomic number, so learners need scaffolding ([Build an Atom guide](https://phet.colorado.edu/files/teachers-guide/build-an-atom-html-guide_en.pdf)). Confusing the average mass (15.999) with the mass number is already a careful box.
2. **Best sequence.** PhET *Build an Atom*, "Symbol" screen and game level 3: build a particle to match a symbol, and the reverse.
3. **Recommendation.**
   - **Already strong:** the nuclide_notation figure, worked decoding and the careful box.
   - **Add:** a one-line warning that her Indian periodic-table chart may print the numbers in other positions. The *nuclear symbol* positions are fixed by IUPAC; a chart's layout is not.
4. **NCERT.** Class 9 Ch 8 (A and Z).

### 1.6 What might explain patterns?
1. **Misconceptions.**
   - The **octet framework**: atoms "want" full shells, bonding happens "to fill shells", and full shells are always stable ([Taber, octet rule framework](https://science-education-research.com/?p=5029)).
   - **"Sharing of nuclear force"**: students think the nucleus's pull is divided among the electrons ([Taber 2003](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2003/rp/b3rp90010j)).
   - Shells taken as orbits ([IOP Spark](https://spark.iop.org/many-students-hold-range-atom-models-including-hybrid-models-consisting-combinations-different)).
2. **Best sequence.**
   - Start from evidence (the ionisation energy graph), not from the rule. Teach bonding electrostatically rather than through the octet rule (Joki & Aksela 2018, cited in [Taber's octet page](https://science-education-research.com/?p=5029)).
   - A kitchen-safe hint that electrons sit at fixed energies: with an adult, a pinch of salt sprinkled into a gas-stove flame turns it yellow, the sodium colour. The MIT demo set includes a flame-colour video, "Elements on Fire" ([RES.HS-002](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-hs-002-chemistry-behind-the-magic-chemical-demonstrations-for-the-classroom/resources/elementsonfire_300k/)).
3. **Recommendation.**
   - **Already strong.** The section anticipates Taber almost exactly: "atoms never want anything", even sodium's loosest electron costs energy, and the shells careful box.
   - **Add:**
     - (a) She will meet the opposite language at school. NCERT Class 9 Ch 8 links valency to the octet rule ([iesc108](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/iesc108.pdf)), and Class 10 Ch 4 says atoms share electrons to "reach a noble gas configuration" ([jesc104](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/jesc104.pdf)). Add a short bridge sentence: "Your school book may say atoms bond *to get* a full shell. Read it as 'arrangements with full shells are often lower in energy', not as atoms wishing."
     - (b) Optional: the salt-in-flame observation as an adult-supervised :::home. It is a first glimpse that electrons in an atom have fixed energies.
4. **NCERT.**
   - Shells K, L, M and configurations such as 2, 8, 1: Class 9 Ch 8.
   - Ionisation energy: Class 11 (not checked in detail).

## Chapter 2: How atoms form substances

### 2.1 Element, compound or mixture?
1. **Misconceptions.**
   - About half of students call any particle diagram containing different atoms a "mixture", wherever the atoms sit. Some read a compound diagram as a mixture (39%) ([RSC *Beyond Appearances*](https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-ideas-about-the-differences-between-elements-compounds-and-mixtures-beyond-appearances/4017771.article)).
   - Students think a compound keeps the properties of its elements, and confuse "O₂ is a molecule" with "O₂ is a compound". Element versus compound at particle level is hard even for trainee teachers (Kahveci 2009, in [Özalp & Kahveci 2015](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2015/rp/c5rp00096c)).
2. **Best demos.**
   - NCERT Class 8 Activity 8.4: heat sugar until it chars, which shows that sweet white sugar is made of black carbon and water.
   - NCERT Class 8 Activity 8.5: an iron and sulfur mixture versus iron sulfide ([hecu108](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/hecu108.pdf)). The sugar one is kitchen-safe with an adult; the iron and sulfur one is not.
3. **Recommendation.**
   - **Already strong:** the four-sample figure (with the H₂ + O₂ mixture beside water), "a mixture of hydrogen and oxygen is not water", the salt-recovery home activity and the air check.
   - **Add** a second, adult-run :::home: heat a teaspoon of sugar in an old steel spoon over the stove. It melts, browns and then turns black, and a cold plate held above fogs with water.
     - Black solid plus water from a sweet white powder is the most vivid "a compound is not like its elements" moment available in a kitchen.
     - It also previews Chapter 4, where a chemical change makes something new.
4. **NCERT.**
   - Elements, compounds and mixtures: Class 8 Ch 8 ([hecu108](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/hecu108.pdf)).
   - Mixtures and separation: Class 9 Ch 5 ([iesc105](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/iesc105.pdf)).
   - Physical versus chemical change: Class 7 Ch 5 ([gecu105](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/gecu105.pdf)).

### 2.2 How do charges interact?
1. **Misconception.** "Twice as far means half the force", which is the book's own careful box. Inverse-square reasoning is a known difficulty in electrostatics ([SWU study, "Students' understanding of the inverse square law in electrostatics"](https://ir.swu.ac.th/entities/publication/6d55c2f8-0779-4298-831a-ded17cd2eb51)).
2. **Best analogy and demo.**
   - Light from a point source spreads over an area that grows as the square of the distance. IOP's "butter gun" image: a sprayer at twice the distance covers four times the toast, so each bit gets a quarter ([IOP Spark, inverse square law of light](https://spark.iop.org/inverse-square-law-light)).
   - The primary-level torch version is in [IET Education](https://education.theiet.org/primary/teaching-resources/how-does-the-light-from-a-torch-change-with-distance).
   - PhET *Coulomb's Law* ([sim](https://phet.colorado.edu/en/simulations/coulombs-law)).
3. **Recommendation.**
   - **Already good:** the Coulomb story, the c2_coulomb figure, the careful box and the "predict before you look" in Investigation 2.2.
   - **Add a torch-and-grid :::home** that makes "squared" *visible*.
     - Shine a torch through a small square hole cut in card onto squared paper. At distance d the spot covers 1 square; at 2d it covers 4.
     - The same light spread over four squares means a quarter per square.
     - This gives her a picture for *why* the square appears, rather than a rule to memorise.
4. **NCERT.** Coulomb's law is not in the Class 6–9 books checked. Attraction and repulsion of charges are touched on via lightning in Class 8 Ch 6.

### 2.3 How can atoms share electrons?
1. **Misconceptions.**
   - Atoms "want" or "need" bonds, often reinforced by "holding hands" analogies ([RSC *Beyond Appearances*: bonding](https://edu.rsc.org/structure-and-bonding/students-ideas-about-chemical-bonding-beyond-appearances/4017806.article)).
   - Bond breaking *releases* energy. Students blend school teaching with the everyday sense that breaking things lets energy out (Boo 1998, discussed in [Nimmermark et al. 2016, CERP](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2016/rp/c6rp00106h)).
   - Bonds are pictured as physical sticks.
2. **Best sequence and demo.** The PhET *States of Matter* "Interaction" screen lets students drag two atoms and watch them settle in an energy well ([teacher guide](https://phet.colorado.edu/files/teachers-guide/states-of-matter-html-guide_en.pdf)). A ball rolling in a valley is the natural analogy.
3. **Recommendation.**
   - **Already strong:** "a bond is not a stick, it is a balance", the energy curve, the 436 kJ "kettle" comparison and the "atoms do not want" careful box.
   - **Add:**
     - (a) Make c2_bond_energy live: a draggable atom with a ball sitting on the curve. Let go and it rolls back to 74 pm; pull hard and it escapes only if you "pay".
     - (b) Since Boo's misconception is common, add one :::check: "Does breaking the H–H bond give out energy or take it in? How do you know from the curve?"
4. **NCERT.**
   - Covalent and ionic bonds introduced: Class 9 Ch 9 ([iesc109](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/iesc109.pdf)).
   - Covalent bonding framed as "noble gas configuration": Class 10 Ch 4 ([jesc104](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/jesc104.pdf)).

### 2.4 What is a molecule?
1. **Misconceptions.**
   - Subscripts and coefficients are confused (Sanger 2005, summarised at [CLEERhub](https://stemedhub.org/groups/cleerhub/wiki/article:1129?action=pdf)).
   - Compounds are pictured as "separate atoms in formula ratio" rather than molecules ([IOP Spark, Andersson 1990](https://spark.iop.org/andersson-1990)).
2. **Best demo.** Build molecules with toothpicks and soaked chana or peas. PhET *Build a Molecule* is suggested by NCERT itself in Class 9 Ch 9 ([iesc109](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/iesc109.pdf); [sim](https://phet.colorado.edu/en/simulations/build-a-molecule)).
3. **Recommendation.**
   - **Already strong:** Gay-Lussac and Avogadro as a puzzle, "3 CO₂ is not C₃O₆" with its figure, and Investigation 2.4.
   - **Add:** make Investigation 2.4 a physical :::home with chana and toothpicks: build 3 CO₂, then try to "make" C₃O₆ from the same pieces and notice that it is a different object.
4. **NCERT.**
   - Molecules of elements and compounds: Class 8 Ch 8.
   - Formula writing: Class 9 Ch 9.

### 2.5 A formula without a molecule
1. **Misconceptions.**
   - The molecular framework, the history conjecture and the valency conjecture ("Na can bond to only one Cl because it gave one electron") ([Taber](https://science-education-research.com/publications/articles/misunderstanding-the-ionic-bond/); [RSC *Beyond Appearances*: bonding](https://edu.rsc.org/structure-and-bonding/students-ideas-about-chemical-bonding-beyond-appearances/4017806.article)).
   - Students also think salt dissolves into NaCl pairs.
2. **Best sequence.**
   - Taber recommends separating ion formation from bonding, using diagrams of ion *ensembles* rather than pairs, and showing ionic solids formed without electron transfer, such as precipitates ([Taber](https://science-education-research.com/publications/articles/misunderstanding-the-ionic-bond/)).
   - Sadoway's [3.091SC Session 8](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/3-091sc-introduction-to-solid-state-chemistry-fall-2010/pages/bonding-and-molecules/8--ionic-crystals-born-haber-cycle) frames lattice energy as the energy gained when a gas of ion *pairs* becomes a crystal. That supports our "the lattice pays the bill" argument.
3. **Recommendation.** See Top-5 #4.
   - **Already strong:** the energy account in c2_salt_energy (with sodium not "wanting"), the Braggs, "no NaCl molecule", MgCl₂ in the check, and the salt-cube home activity.
   - **Accuracy note.** The book uses a lattice energy of 787 kJ/mol. OpenStax Chemistry 2e lists 769 kJ/mol for NaCl ([OpenStax 7.5](https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/7-5-strengths-of-ionic-and-covalent-bonds)). Both appear in the literature, depending on the source and data convention, and 787 is self-consistent with the book's −640 total. No change is needed, but if a reader cross-checks OpenStax they will see 769.
4. **NCERT.**
   - Ionic bonds, formula unit mass, and conductivity of salt solution (Activity 9.4): Class 9 Ch 9.
   - Lattice enthalpy: Class 11 Unit 4 ([kech104](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kech104.pdf)).

### 2.6 Why composition is not enough
1. **Misconceptions.**
   - Pencil "lead" is lead.
   - Bonds are only either ionic or covalent, and covalent bonds are weaker than ionic ones, so covalent substances have low melting points. This is used to explain melting without thinking about network structures ([RSC *Beyond Appearances*: bonding](https://edu.rsc.org/structure-and-bonding/students-ideas-about-chemical-bonding-beyond-appearances/4017806.article)).
2. **Best demos.**
   - Graphite conducts: a soft (4B–6B) pencil line on paper completes a circuit with a 9 V battery and an LED, and a longer line gives a dimmer LED ([Science Museum Group, "Graphite circuits"](https://www.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/learning/resources/graphite-circuits); [UW CEI, "Draw a circuit"](https://www.cei.washington.edu/lesson-plans-resources/draw-a-circuit-fun-with-graphite)).
   - Diamond burning in oxygen: a Royal Institution film with Peter Wothers, where diamond and graphite both burn to CO₂ ([reported by ScienceAlert](https://www.sciencealert.com/watch-scientists-burn-a-diamond-in-pure-oxygen)). It is a modern echo of Lavoisier and Tennant.
3. **Recommendation.**
   - **Already strong:** the Lavoisier burning-glass scene, Tennant's equal-CO₂ result, "a pencil mark is sheets peeling off", and the carbon3d live figure.
   - **Add:**
     - (a) A :::home "draw a wire": a pencil line lights an LED, whereas diamond, the same element, would not conduct. It is cheap and memorable, and it makes "same formula, different structure" a thing she has seen. Tell her to use a 9 V battery and LED only, with no mains.
     - (b) One line: "pencil 'lead' has no lead in it; it is graphite mixed with clay" (stated in the Science Museum resource).
     - (c) Mention the Ri diamond-burning film as a :::story-style pointer, so she can watch it rather than read it.
4. **NCERT.** Allotropes of carbon, diamond, graphite and fullerene, with graphite conducting: Class 10 Ch 4 ([jesc104](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/jesc104.pdf)).

### 2.7 Unequal sharing and molecular dipoles
1. **Misconceptions.**
   - Students believe electrons are shared equally in all covalent bonds ([RSC *Beyond Appearances*: bonding](https://edu.rsc.org/structure-and-bonding/students-ideas-about-chemical-bonding-beyond-appearances/4017806.article)).
   - Polar bonds are taken to mean a polar molecule; the book handles this with CO₂.
   - The bending water stream is taken as proof of polarity. The book's careful box is correct: [Ziaei-Moayyed, Goodman & Williams, *J. Chem. Educ.* 77 (2000) 1520](https://instructional-resources.physics.uiowa.edu/sites/instructional-resources.physics.uiowa.edu/files/field/demos/documents/Electrical%20Deflection%20of%20Polar%20Liquid%20Streams.pdf) showed that the deflection comes from charge induced on the droplets and recommended dropping the demo as evidence for dipoles.
   - PhET found that students in interviews did not believe a real atom's electronegativity could change. Slider models therefore need flagging as models ([Molecule Polarity guide](https://phet.colorado.edu/files/teachers-guide/molecule-polarity-html-guide_en.pdf)).
2. **Best demos.**
   - ACS Lesson 5.1, "Water is a polar molecule": mark δ⁺ and δ⁻ on model water molecules, and race a drop of water against a drop of less-polar alcohol on a paper towel ([ACS activity sheet](https://www.acs.org/content/dam/acsorg/msc/downloads/chapter-5/lesson-1/ch5-l1-activity-sheet.pdf)).
   - "Fireworks in a glass": water-based food colouring stays as beads in oil and bursts only when it reaches the water ([Scientific American, "Create underwater fireworks with chemistry"](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/create-underwater-fireworks-with-chemistry/)).
3. **Recommendation.**
   - **Already strong:** the tug-of-war cancellation, the dipoles figure with molecule3d, the debye evidence box, and the honest careful box on the water stream, which most textbooks get wrong.
   - **Gap.** The section has no :::home, and having debunked the comb demo she needs a *legitimate* kitchen experience of polarity. Add "fireworks in a glass" (oil, water, food colouring). Its honest claim is modest: polar water-based colouring does not mix with non-polar oil. Do not overclaim. Many factors matter, and it is evidence for "like mixes with like", not a dipole measurement.
4. **NCERT.** Polarity, dipole moment and hydrogen bonding: Class 11 Unit 4 ([kech104](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kech104.pdf)). This is well ahead of school, so keep it qualitative.

## Chapter 3: Why matter has different states

### 3.1 One substance, three states
1. **Misconceptions.**
   - Molecules melt, are solid in ice and liquid in water (38.6%), get larger on freezing (30.5%), change shape to fit the container (24.7%), or stop moving in a solid (31.6–32%) ([Özalp & Kahveci 2015](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2015/rp/c5rp00096c)).
   - Steam molecules are larger than ice molecules: about 50% of 18-year-olds (Griffiths & Preston 1992, in [RSC](https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-ideas-about-the-particulate-nature-of-matter-beyond-appearances/4017763.article)).
   - Gases are thought to sink like liquids, and air is thought incompressible ([IOP Spark, Andersson 1990](https://spark.iop.org/andersson-1990)).
2. **Best sequence and demos.**
   - ACS presents states as a contest between motion and attraction from the very first lesson ([ACS Ch 1 reading](https://www.acs.org/content/dam/acsorg/msc/downloads/chapter-1/ch1-student-reading.pdf)).
   - PhET *States of Matter* (above).
   - The air and water syringe, NCERT Class 8 Activity 7.6 ([hecu107](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/hecu107.pdf)).
   - IOP suggests a spring-jointed physical model of a vibrating solid, or role-play. Tsai (1999) found that role-play and analogy gains persisted where the control group regressed ([IOP Spark](https://spark.iop.org/many-students-think-atoms-do-not-move-solid)).
3. **Recommendation.** See Top-5 #2 for the widget.
   - **Already strong:** the 1,700× steam figure, the careful box including why ice floats, and the Black ice-water home activity.
   - **Add:**
     - (a) Make the 3.1 check's syringe a real :::home. A medical syringe from the chemist, sealed with a fingertip: air squashes, water does not. She will also do this in Class 8.
     - (b) A 30-second role-play suggestion: hold hands and jiggle in place, let go and shuffle, then run. It is cheap and evidence-backed.
4. **NCERT.**
   - States of water, evaporation and condensation: Class 6 Ch 8 ([fecu108](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/fecu108.pdf)).
   - Particle spacing, attraction and motion: Class 8 Ch 7 ([hecu107](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/hecu107.pdf)).

### 3.2 Temperature and particle motion
1. **Misconceptions.**
   - When air is heated its particles expand, and that causes the pressure (Ayas, Özmen & Çalık, cited at [IOP Spark](https://spark.iop.org/some-students-think-when-air-heated-particles-expand-and-cause-high-pressure)).
   - Heated liquid in a closed container gains mass as it expands: 41% on an AAAS item ([AAAS news](https://www.aaas.org/news/us-students-and-science-aaas-testing-gives-new-insight-what-they-know-and-their-misconceptions)).
   - Particle number is thought to grow on heating and shrink on cooling (Ozmen 2011, in [Özalp & Kahveci 2015](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2015/rp/c5rp00096c)).
   - Temperature is confused with heat ([IOP Spark](https://spark.iop.org/many-students-have-difficulties-distinguishing-between-ideas-temperature-and-heat-and-using-these)).
2. **Best demos.**
   - ACS Lesson 1.2 sequence: food colouring in hot versus cold water, then a detergent bubble film over a bottle neck that grows when warmed and is sucked in when cooled ([ACS Ch 1 reading](https://www.acs.org/content/dam/acsorg/msc/downloads/chapter-1/ch1-student-reading.pdf)).
   - The balloon-over-bottle version is NCERT Class 8 Ch 7's own "Discover, design and debate" project ([hecu107](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/hecu107.pdf)).
   - PhET *Gas Properties* ([teacher guide](https://phet.colorado.edu/files/teachers-guide/gas-properties-html-guide_en.pdf)).
3. **Recommendation.**
   - **Already strong:** the Charles balloon scene, the gasbox live figure, the kelvin explanation with the "doubling in Celsius" trap, the balloon-on-bottle home activity, and Investigation 3.2's "without saying the particles got bigger".
   - **Add:**
     - (a) Weigh the bottle-and-balloon before and after warming on a kitchen scale. It reads the same, which directly confronts the AAAS 41% "it got heavier" idea.
     - (b) In gasbox, add an optional count readout ("molecules: 40, the same at every temperature") and a fixed molecule-size marker. That kills both the "more particles" and "bigger particles" explanations at a glance.
4. **NCERT.**
   - °C, °F and K: Class 6 Ch 7 ([fecu107](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/fecu107.pdf)).
   - Heating increases particle motion: Class 8 Ch 7.

### 3.3 Changing state is not changing identity
1. **Misconceptions.** See Top-5 #1.
   - The bubbles are air, oxygen, hydrogen or heat, and water splits on boiling ([NSTA probe](https://www.nsta.org/lesson-plan/whats-bubbles)).
   - Evaporation is decomposition: 25% in Özalp & Kahveci 2015.
   - Boiling decomposes a compound (Bridle & Yezierski 2012, in the same paper).
   - Gas is not "real" matter, and steam is a visible gas (NSTA).
2. **Best sequence.**
   - The NSTA probe sequence: predict; draw Before, During and After; ask where the water level went; contrast invisible vapour with the visible mist; reheat and recool to show reversibility ([NSTA](https://www.nsta.org/lesson-plan/whats-bubbles)).
   - The syringe "boil without heating" ([Earthlearningidea](https://www.earthlearningidea.com/PDF/338_Pressure_reduction.pdf)).
3. **Recommendation.**
   - **Already strong:** Nicholson and Carlisle as the foil, the cold-lid proof, the boil-versus-split figure with real energies, and the check.
   - **Add:**
     - (a) The syringe demo from Top-5 #1.
     - (b) One sentence distinguishing the *invisible* water vapour (the gap just above the kettle spout) from the visible white "steam", which is condensed droplets.
     - (c) In c3_boil_vs_split, draw the inside of a bubble on the left panel.
4. **NCERT.**
   - Evaporation and condensation: Class 6 Ch 8.
   - Changes of state at particle level: Class 8 Ch 7.
   - Physical change: Class 7 Ch 5.

### 3.4 Squeezing a gas
1. **Misconceptions.**
   - Pressure is caused by particles expanding or repelling each other, rather than by collisions ([IOP Spark](https://spark.iop.org/some-students-think-when-air-heated-particles-expand-and-cause-high-pressure)).
   - Gases have no mass, and forces explain gas behaviour instead of motion ([RSC *Beyond Appearances*: particles](https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-ideas-about-the-particulate-nature-of-matter-beyond-appearances/4017763.article)).
   - Air is incompressible ([IOP Spark, Andersson 1990](https://spark.iop.org/andersson-1990)).
2. **Best demos.**
   - RSC suggests pumping air into a ball and weighing it, so the mass goes up, then leaving a tyre in the sun to link pressure to motion ([RSC](https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-ideas-about-the-particulate-nature-of-matter-beyond-appearances/4017763.article)).
   - The syringe squeeze (NCERT Class 8 Activity 7.6).
   - PhET *Gas Properties*. Its design note is useful for us: the gauge reads zero until the first wall hit, a reminder that pressure is collisions ([guide](https://phet.colorado.edu/files/teachers-guide/gas-properties-html-guide_en.pdf)).
3. **Recommendation.**
   - **Already strong:** Boyle and Hooke, the thumb-on-pump "spring of the air", the gasbox volume mode, PV constancy and the butane-lighter link.
   - **Add a "weigh the air" :::home.** A deflated football or ball on a kitchen scale, pumped up hard and reweighed, gains a few grams. Gas is matter, and squeezing more in adds mass. This sets up the case at the chapter's end (clue 1: the mass did not change), so she has done the reasoning herself.
4. **NCERT.** Pressure, the pascal and atmospheric pressure: Class 8 Ch 6 ([hecu106](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/hecu106.pdf)). Boyle's law is not in the Class 6–9 books checked.

### 3.5 Where did the solute go?
1. **Misconceptions.** See Top-5 #3.
   - Sugar "melts" in water (35.2%).
   - Dissolving is a chemical reaction (23.3%) ([Özalp & Kahveci 2015](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2015/rp/c5rp00096c)).
   - The solute disappears and mass is not conserved ([RSC "Mass and dissolving"](https://edu.rsc.org/resources/mass-and-dissolving/1084.article)).
   - Dissolved particles pass "between the glass particles" (Ayas et al. 2010, in Özalp & Kahveci).
2. **Best sequence and demos.**
   - The RSC diagnostic: predict the mass before and after dissolving, before teaching.
   - NCERT Class 9 Activity 9.1: weigh salt and water before and after ([iesc109](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/iesc109.pdf)).
   - NCERT Class 8 Activity 7.7: water level after dissolving sugar ([hecu107](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/hecu107.pdf)).
   - NCERT Class 8 Activity 9.1: add salt a spoon at a time until no more dissolves (saturation) ([hecu109](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/hecu109.pdf)).
   - PhET *Sugar and Salt Solutions* ([sim](https://phet.colorado.edu/en/simulations/sugar-and-salt-solutions)) is the matching sim. Its page did not render for me, so check its micro view before citing it.
3. **Recommendation.**
   - **Already strong:** the Dandi opener, which is perfect for Mumbai; filtering and re-weighing 5.00 g; the dilution figure with its live version; and the A-versus-B bottle check.
   - **Add:** the careful box and home activities in Top-5 #3.
   - **Also add to the dilution widget:** a "salt in the whole bottle" counter that stays fixed while the "salt per sip" readout drops. It shows the two quantities she is likely to merge.
   - **Optional:** one sentence noting that salt splits into separate Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions while sugar stays as whole molecules. This links back to 2.5 and breaks the "NaCl pairs in solution" idea flagged by RSC *Beyond Appearances*.
4. **NCERT.**
   - Solute, solvent, concentration, saturation and the effect of temperature: Class 8 Ch 9 ([hecu109](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/hecu109.pdf)).
   - Concentration as a percentage: Class 9 Ch 5 ([iesc105](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/iesc105.pdf)).
   - mol/L: Class 11 Unit 1 ([kech101](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kech101.pdf)).

### 3.6 Intermolecular forces against thermal motion
1. **Misconceptions.**
   - Boiling breaks covalent bonds. Intermolecular "bonds" are placed inside molecules, or ranked as stronger than covalent bonds. Hydrogen bonds are dismissed as "not real bonds" ([RSC *Beyond Appearances*: bonding](https://edu.rsc.org/structure-and-bonding/students-ideas-about-chemical-bonding-beyond-appearances/4017806.article)).
   - "Van der Waals" is used as a catch-all explanation (same source).
2. **Best sequence and demos.**
   - OpenStax frames hydrogen bonds as roughly 5–10% of a covalent bond. It contrasts about 17 kJ to boil HCl with about 430 kJ to break H–Cl, and shows the hydride boiling-point plot ([OpenStax 10.1](https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/10-1-intermolecular-forces)). Our numbers (41 versus 463 kJ, about 20 times) are consistent with this.
   - ACS: water versus less-polar alcohol evaporating on a paper towel ([ACS 5.1 sheet](https://www.acs.org/content/dam/acsorg/msc/downloads/chapter-5/lesson-1/ch5-l1-activity-sheet.pdf)).
   - ACS Lesson 1.1: water drops hold together on wax paper ([lesson plan](https://www.acs.org/content/dam/acsorg/msc/downloads/chapter-1/lesson-1/ch1-l1-lesson-plan.pdf)).
3. **Recommendation.**
   - **Already strong:** the CH₄, NH₃ and H₂O puzzle (same mass, same electron count), the "no rivers, no monsoon" hook, "every state is a contest", the 20× scale reminder and the careful box.
   - **Add a :::home evaporation race.** On a steel plate or the back of the hand, put one drop of water and one drop of hand sanitiser, which is mostly alcohol. Predict which vanishes first and which feels colder. Alcohol molecules have weaker attractions, so they escape sooner and carry heat away faster. This is a kitchen version of the ACS activity: no flames, and an adult nearby because sanitiser is flammable.
   - **Figure.** c3_boiling_points is good. Consider adding the H₂S, H₂Se and H₂Te trend as an inset (OpenStax-style), because the "water should boil at about −80 °C" prediction is the most surprising number in the chapter and is currently text-only.
4. **NCERT.**
   - "Interparticle attraction" without the names of the forces: Class 8 Ch 7.
   - Hydrogen bonding: Class 11 Unit 4 ([kech104](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kech104.pdf)).

## Cross-cutting notes for the authors

- **Prediction before reveal is the main lever.** Most sections end with a :::try that asks for prediction, but the *text* often reveals first. The highest-value places for an in-text "pause and predict" line are:
  - the 1.1 gold foil,
  - the 2.2 double distance,
  - the 3.3 bubbles (partly done already),
  - the 3.5 mass after dissolving,
