# Research notes: Chapters 8–11 (electron transfer, periodic table, carbon, Earth and energy)

Scope: `src/ch08.md` to `src/ch11.md`. Reader: a 12-year-old in Mumbai. For each numbered section this file gives (1) likely misconceptions, with a source; (2) the best explanation sequence, analogy or demo found, with its URL; (3) a concrete recommendation, judged against what the section already does; (4) where the topic sits in NCERT.

Ground rules: every home activity suggested here uses kitchen or pharmacy materials, and an adult is present wherever there is heat, a flame or a 9 V battery. These are ideas paraphrased from the sources, not copied text. Where a source could not be fully read (paywall, JavaScript-only page), the note says so.

## Top 5 changes, in priority order

1. **8.4: make the lemon battery answer Galvani versus Volta, and ask "what gets used up?"** Before the reader builds the battery, have her predict what happens with *two copper* pieces in one lemon (nothing) compared with copper plus zinc (a voltage). That is Volta's own argument from the chapter opening, done on the kitchen table. Then add one prompt: "After an hour, what has been used up: electrons, the lemon, or the zinc?" This goes straight at the consumer-source and "used-up current" models (IOP Spark) and at the anode/cathode charge confusion found in Sanger & Greenbowe's work (summarised in Supasorn 2015). It also lines up with NCERT Curiosity Grade 8 Activity 4.6 (lemon cell), which she may already have done. In the `daniell` live figure, add a running counter ("electrons out of zinc = electrons into copper") and a salt-bridge on/off toggle, so the Riya case can be seen as well as read.
2. **11.1: turn the buffer from words into something she can see.** Replace or extend "Your breath is an acid-maker" with a two-cup race. Both cups hold cabbage indicator in water; one also has a tiny pinch of baking soda, as a stand-in for slightly basic, buffered seawater. She blows the same number of breaths into each and watches the plain cup change colour first while the buffered cup holds out. Add the ACS candle-under-a-glass step (burning makes CO₂, and the CO₂ acidifies water) so the chapter's whole chain, *fuel → CO₂ → acid → carbonate used up*, happens on her table. In `c11_carbonate`, show the net count: **one CO₂ + one CO₃²⁻ + H₂O → two HCO₃⁻**. This goes at three misconceptions: "acidification means the sea is becoming acid", "it's caused by pollution or acid rain", and "burnt carbon disappears".
3. **8.1: build the bridge to her school definition, and let her grow copper herself.** NCERT Class 10 Ch. 1 defines oxidation as *gaining oxygen* (or losing hydrogen). Add two or three sentences showing that this is the same idea: when magnesium burns, Mg gives electrons to O, so "gains oxygen" and "loses electrons" describe one event. Put a prediction before the reveal: "The copper coat appeared. Where did it come from?" (the zinc turned into copper / it was in the blue liquid all along / it came from the air). AAAS found that about 40% of answers chose the idea that atoms turn into different atoms. Add a kitchen copper-plating activity: dissolve a little copper into vinegar and salt, then let a plain steel paperclip or iron nail turn copper-coloured. The rusting race can stay as a second activity.
4. **9.1–9.2: close the "shared-out pull" loophole, and let her predict the cliff.** Taber found that about three quarters of advanced students accepted that the nucleus has a fixed amount of pull that is shared among the electrons. The RSC lists a related idea: each proton holds on to one electron. The shielding paragraph's phrase "each step adds a whole proton of pull" can be read that way. Rewrite it so that *every proton pulls on every electron, and adding electrons does not divide the pull up*. In 9.2, before showing lithium's successive ionisation energies, ask her to sketch where the "cliff" should fall for sodium (2, 8, 1). Add a home flame activity: a pinch of table salt dropped on the gas flame and looked at through her CD, with an adult. This links to the Bunsen–Kirchhoff opening, and the Duncan 2025 result (the light comes from neutral atoms formed in the flame) keeps it accurate.
5. **10.3 (and 10.2): promote "boiling doesn't break bonds" into a `:::careful` block, and add a household "alkane ladder".** The most prominent misconception in Schmidt et al.'s study of boiling points (CERP 2009) was that boiling breaks covalent bonds. Section 10.3 currently mentions this only as a passing paragraph. Add an observation-only ladder: LPG (C3–C4, gas) → baby oil or liquid paraffin (C15 and up, runny) → petroleum jelly (mainly above C25, soft) → candle wax (C20–C40, hard). Give 10.2 the ACS "sugar-coated sweet in water vs oil" test, done with Gems, so "like dissolves like" is something she has seen.

Runners-up: the silver-cleaning demo on aluminium foil for 8.3 (silver puja items and payal are in most Mumbai homes); lemon on fish for amines as bases in 10.6; the pencil-lead electrolysis for 8.2 (gas volumes in a 2:1 ratio make Faraday's electron counting visible).

## Chapter 8 — Electron transfer and electrochemistry

### 8.1 When metals and ions trade electrons

**(1) Likely misconceptions**
- *Atoms change into other atoms* ("the zinc turned into copper"). The AAAS Project 2061 middle-school testing found the idea that reactant atoms become different atoms in about 40% of answers. Ideas that new atoms are created (40%) or destroyed (36%) were similarly common. https://www.aaas.org/news/us-students-and-science-aaas-testing-gives-new-insight-what-they-know-and-their-misconceptions (item bank: http://assessment.aaas.org/)
- *Electrons pass because "bonds" between dissolved ions break and re-form* (for example, Cu²⁺–SO₄²⁻ "bonds"). Students also took any change in charge as redox, and they were confident while wrong: mean score about 6 out of 18 with about 56% confidence. RSC Education in Chemistry, "Redox revolution": https://edu.rsc.org/education-research/redox-revolution/2000327.article
- *Oxidation means adding oxygen.* This is not really a misconception at her age: it is the definition in her own Class 10 NCERT book (Ch. 1, §1.2.5). https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/jesc101.pdf. It becomes a misconception only if it is never bridged to electrons.

**(2) Best sequence, analogy or demo**
- Kitchen copper plating ("Copper Topping"): copper dissolves into vinegar and salt, and a steel nail or paperclip hung in that liquid comes out copper-coloured. Use an untreated nail as a control. https://serc.carleton.edu/sp/mnstep/activities/26583.html (Use it for the procedure only. Its particle explanation is loose.) Indian coins are no longer copper, so use stripped copper electrical wire or scraps from an old copper vessel.
- RSC microscale displacement practical. Include the "metal in its own salt solution: no change" controls. https://edu.rsc.org/download?ac=513327
- NCERT Class 10 Activity 1.9: an iron nail in copper sulphate, with Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu. https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/jesc101.pdf
- For accuracy, OpenStax 17.2 uses copper wire in silver nitrate as the spontaneous electron-transfer example. https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/17-2-galvanic-cells

**(3) Recommendation**
- The particle story, the proton-counting `careful` block and the stepwise `zncu` figure are **already strong**. Two additions:
  - **Prediction before the reveal.** Before the paragraph that begins "Nothing magic has happened", ask: *Where did the copper come from?* Offer three choices. The fading blue is the clue she should learn to read.
  - **NCERT bridge, two or three sentences**, after the OIL RIG paragraph: "In your school book, oxidation means gaining oxygen. That is the same thing seen from outside. When magnesium burns, each Mg atom hands two electrons to an oxygen atom: Mg gains oxygen *and* loses electrons." Optionally add 2 Mg + O₂ → 2 MgO with the electrons marked.
- **Home activity:** add the copper-plated nail. In the `:::home` block, have her compare it with the rusting race: both are electrons leaving iron, going to oxygen in one case and to copper ions in the other. If CuSO₄ ("neela thotha") is used instead, an adult must handle it, because it is harmful.
- **Figure `zncu`:** show the solution's blue tint fading in proportion to the Cu²⁺ ions used. That ties what she sees to what she counts.

**(4) NCERT:** Class 10 Ch. 1 (displacement §1.2.3, oxidation and reduction §1.2.5, corrosion §1.3.1) and Ch. 3 §3.2.4 (metals in salt solutions): https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/jesc103.pdf. Redox as electron transfer comes in Class 11 Unit 7, Redox Reactions. The file for that unit could not be fetched in this pass; its neighbour kech202 is Unit 8.

### 8.2 Balancing electrons as well as atoms

**(1) Likely misconceptions**
- *One Mg atom "splits" into two Ag atoms*, or atoms are made or destroyed (AAAS; link above). The section already addresses this.
- *Any change in charge counts as redox*, and *electrons are counted by bonds broken* (RSC "Redox revolution"; link above).

**(2) Best sequence, analogy or demo**
- The ACS "pencil electrolysis" kit activity: two sharpened pencils on a 9 V battery in Epsom-salt water with indicator. One pencil makes about twice as much gas as the other, and the indicator turns acid-coloured at one tip and base-coloured at the other. https://www.Acs.Org/content/dam/acsorg/education/outreach/kidszone/pencil-electrolysis.pdf. This is Faraday's counting made visible: four electrons make one O₂ and two H₂.
- MIT 5.111SC Lecture 25 (Drennan), "Oxidation-Reduction and Electrochemical Cells", covers balancing redox and has clicker questions. https://www.ocw.mit.edu/courses/5-111sc-principles-of-chemical-science-fall-2014/pages/unit-iv-transition-metals-oxidation-reduction-reactions/lecture-25

**(3) Recommendation**
- The half-equation layout, the charge check and the "lazy way" counterexample are **already strong**.
- Turn Faraday's 108 g of silver versus 32 g of copper into a **prediction**: "The same current deposits 108 g of silver. Will it deposit more copper, the same, or less?" Then reveal the answer.
- **Optional home activity** (adult present; Epsom salt is sold in pharmacies; use red cabbage juice in place of universal indicator): run the pencil electrolysis and *predict which pencil bubbles more*. Avoid table salt, which gives chlorine at the anode.
- The "same charge both sides" rule is already illustrated by `c8_electron_balance`. Add a "too many or too few electrons" warning state to the figure for the lazy equation.

**(4) NCERT:** Balancing equations is in Class 10 Ch. 1 §1.1.2. Faraday's laws are in Class 12 Electrochemistry: https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/lech102.pdf. This section runs ahead of the Indian syllabus, which is fine, but it would help to say so in a margin note.

### 8.3 Why doesn't copper dissolve in zinc sulfate?

**(1) Likely misconceptions**
- *If an equation balances, the reaction happens.* The book already names this one.
- *A metal reacts with a solution of its own salt.* The RSC practical builds in a control because students expect a change. https://edu.rsc.org/download?ac=513327
- *Reactivity means how fast something fizzes*, or atoms "want" to give electrons away. The anthropomorphic octet framework is described by Taber: https://science-education-research.com/?p=4731

**(2) Best sequence, analogy or demo**
- NCERT Class 10 Activity 3.12 is the same contrast the book uses: copper wire in iron sulphate (nothing) and an iron nail in copper sulphate (copper forms). https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/jesc103.pdf
- **Silver-tarnish cleaning (kitchen-safe, adult present for the hot water):** a tarnished silver item touching aluminium foil in hot water with baking soda and salt. The black Ag₂S turns back to shiny silver because aluminium, which is higher in the series, gives electrons to silver ions. https://www.compoundchem.com/2013/12/16/removing-tarnish-silver/ (A reader comment there disputes some equations. The core idea, Al → Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ and Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag, is sound.) Note the rotten-egg smell of H₂S: keep the room ventilated.

**(3) Recommendation**
- The `careful` block ("balances perfectly... reason is energy") and the nine-experiment `c8_series` figure are **already strong**.
- Make `c8_series` a **predict-first grid**: the reader taps a "yes" or "no" guess into each of the nine cells before the reveal.
- Add the **silver-cleaning `:::home`** as the section's demo. A Mumbai household is likely to have a blackened silver spoon, puja item or anklet. It shows a metal higher in the series (aluminium) pushing electrons onto silver, and it explains a real household trick.
- One analogy to add after "Electron transfer has a direction": *like water that runs down a staircase but never climbs it on its own*. This prepares for the "uphill" language of 8.4 and 11.2.

**(4) NCERT:** Class 10 Ch. 3 §3.2.4–3.2.5 (reactivity series).

### 8.4 Galvanic cells and recharging

**(1) Likely misconceptions**
- *Electrons travel through the solution or salt bridge*, sometimes riding on ions; *the anode is positive because it lost electrons*; *bridge ions move the wrong way*. These come from Sanger & Greenbowe (1997) and Garnett & Treagust (1992), as summarised in Supasorn, CERP 2015: https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2015/rp/c4rp00247d
- *A battery is a source that supplies something to the bulb, which uses it up* (the consumer-source model). This can include thinking one wire is enough. IOP Spark: https://spark.iop.org/some-students-hold-consumer-source-model-simple-circuit
- *Electricity comes from the lemon (or frog)*, not from the two different metals. Galvani's error, as told in the chapter opening and in NCERT Curiosity Grade 8 §4.3.1.

**(2) Best sequence, analogy or demo**
- NCERT Curiosity Grade 8, §4.3 "How Does a Battery Generate Electricity?": a voltaic cell, Activity 4.6 lemon cell (5–6 lemons, copper and iron nails, an LED), dry cells, and rechargeable Li-ion batteries. https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/hecu104.pdf. NCERT does not mention electrons at this level, so the book adds real value here.
- Coin-and-foil voltaic pile (Science in School, 2025): coin, salt-soaked paper and foil, stacked 10–12 layers, about 1.5 V measured. The coins darken as they act as electrodes. https://scienceinschool.web.cern.ch/article/2025/building-a-simple-voltaic-pile
- Household two-compartment battery: a magnesium strip in salt water inside a porous cup, with a carbon rod in vinegar and hydrogen peroxide outside. It lights a red LED and shows clearly why the barrier makes electrons take the wire. https://cei.washington.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/04/A-Battery-From-Household-Chemicals-Demo-Instructions.pdf
- PhET *Battery Voltage* shows charges being moved between the terminals: https://phet.colorado.edu/en/simulations/battery-voltage. PhET *Circuit Construction Kit: DC* lets her test whether one wire is enough: https://phet.colorado.edu/en/simulations/circuit-construction-kit-dc
- IOP suggests the rope-loop model and a "What gets used up?" lesson for ages 11–14 (same IOP link as above).

**(3) Recommendation**
- The salt-bridge `careful` block and the Riya case are **already strong**: they confront the electrons-through-the-bridge misconception directly.
- **Upgrade the lemon battery `:::home`** with a two-part prediction:
  - (a) "Push *two copper coins* into one lemon. Will the LED glow or the meter move?" It will not. Then use copper with a galvanised nail. The book's scene sets this up: Volta beat Galvani by changing the metals.
  - (b) "After the LED has glowed for an hour, what has been used up?" Answer: zinc atoms, which go into the juice as ions, and the H⁺ in the juice. Not electrons, which only go round. This makes the chemistry the "fuel", against the consumer-source model.
  - If the family owns a cheap multimeter, measure one lemon (about 0.9 V) before chaining.
  - Mention that NCERT's version uses iron nails and needs 5–6 lemons, because iron sits lower than zinc and so gives a smaller push. That connects back to 8.3.
- **Anode and cathode signs:** add one line to the anode/cathode note: "In a cell, the anode is the *negative* terminal. Electrons leave it, but that is because they are being *pushed out* of it, not because it has lost charge." This is exactly the confusion recorded by Sanger & Greenbowe (Supasorn summary).
- **`daniell` live figure:** add (i) a running tally showing electrons leaving zinc = electrons arriving at copper; (ii) a salt-bridge toggle. With the bridge removed, a "charge build-up" bar fills in each beaker and the current stops. This animates the Riya case.
- **Electrolysis paragraph:** fine as written. Note that PhET's *Battery Voltage* is a good link for "pushing electrons uphill".

**(4) NCERT:** Curiosity Grade 8 Ch. 4 §4.3 (voltaic cell, lemon cell, dry cell, rechargeable batteries). Class 12 Electrochemistry (Daniell cell, salt bridge, electrolysis): https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/lech102.pdf

### Case: The cell that would not light
**Already strong.** It isolates the salt bridge as the missing piece by elimination. Optional: ask her to *predict the ammeter reading* before clue 1 is given.

## Chapter 9 — The shape of the periodic table

### Opening (flame colours, Bunsen and Kirchhoff, Raman, Mendeleev)

**(1) Likely misconceptions**
- *The colour comes from the dissolved metal ions, or from the metal "burning".* Duncan (J. Chem. Educ. 2025) shows that the emitters are usually *neutral atoms* re-formed in the hot flame. The sodium yellow line, for example, is from neutral Na near 589 nm. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12529943
- *Mendeleev used atomic number.* Not a misconception at 12, but the Moseley scene handles the switch well.

**(2) Best demo**
- RSC "Flame colours" demonstration: Na gives yellow-orange, K lilac (often masked by sodium), Li crimson, Cu green-blue. The explanation is electrons falling between levels. https://edu.rsc.org/resources/flame-colours-a-demonstration/760.article. Its ethanol spray method is not kitchen-safe. Use only the colour list and the explanation.
- MIT RES.HS-002 "Elements on Fire" (Dolhun), with fireworks-colour teaching notes: https://live.ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-hs-002-chemistry-behind-the-magic-chemical-demonstrations-for-the-classroom/resources/elementsonfire_300k/
- MIT 3.091SC Session 2 (Sadoway): Mendeleev's patterns and eka-silicon/germanium, a parallel to the book's gallium story. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/3-091sc-introduction-to-solid-state-chemistry-fall-2010/pages/structure-of-the-atom/2-periodic-table/

**(3) Recommendation**
- The CD spectroscope `:::home` is **already strong**: it is safe and leads her to bright lines with gaps between them.
- **Add a 2-line extension** (adult present): "Ask an adult to drop a few grains of table salt onto a blue gas-stove flame. Look at the flash through your CD." She will see the same yellow as the street lamp, which is Bunsen and Kirchhoff in her kitchen.
- Optional: "low-sodium" salt contains potassium chloride, which gives a faint lilac usually swamped by sodium's yellow. This is the RSC's sodium-masking point. Present it only as a challenge, not a promise.

### 9.1 Why atoms shrink across a row

**(1) Likely misconceptions**
- *More electrons take more room, so atoms grow across a row.* The book names this one.
- *The nucleus has a fixed amount of pull, shared out among the electrons* ("conservation of force"). About three quarters of a larger group of pre-university learners judged statements built on this idea to be true, and they used it to explain both size and ionisation energy. Taber: https://science-education-research.com/?p=8727; Taber CERP 2003 (sample of over 300 students): https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2003/rp/b3rp90010j
- *Each proton attracts one electron*; *attraction is one-way*; *electrons are stationary*. RSC CPD, "Everything you need to teach atomic structure and periodicity": https://edu.rsc.org/cpd/everything-you-need-to-teach-atomic-structure-and-periodicity-at-post-16/4018373.article

**(2) Best sequence, analogy or demo**
- PhET *Coulomb's Law*: change the size of the charges and the distance, and watch the force. This is exactly the content of the book's Investigation 9.1. https://phet.colorado.edu/en/simulations/coulombs-law
- OpenStax 6.5 for accuracy. It uses effective nuclear charge: core electrons shield well and same-shell electrons shield poorly. Covalent radii: Li 167 pm, Na 190 pm, K 243 pm (Fig. 6.31); F 64 pm (Table 6.2). That supports "less than half its width". https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/6-5-periodic-variations-in-element-properties
- ACS Middle School Chemistry Ch. 4 Lesson 3 (energy-level models, card activity): https://www.acs.org/content/dam/acsorg/msc/downloads/chapter-4/lesson-3/ch4-l3-lesson-plan.pdf. It explicitly leaves out *why* sizes change, so the book goes further than the ACS here.

**(3) Recommendation**
- The size-measuring paragraph, the `careful` block and Investigation 9.1 are **already strong**.
- **Rewrite one sentence.** "Each step adds a whole proton of pull" invites the shared-out or one-proton-per-electron reading. Suggested replacement idea: *"Every proton pulls on every electron. Adding a proton makes the pull on each outer electron stronger; adding an electron to the same shell doesn't share that pull out more thinly, it just adds a small push from a neighbour that is no closer to the nucleus."* Optionally add a short `:::careful` block quoting the shared-out idea and correcting it.
- **Lamp-and-screen analogy:** keep it, but add its limit: "The screen is made of electrons that are themselves moving; it isn't a solid wall." This answers the RSC's "electrons are stationary" point.
- **Prediction before reveal:** the chapter question already does this. Make `c9_radii` hide the circle sizes until she has guessed the order of Li, C, F and Na.

**(4) NCERT:** Class 9 (Exploration, new) Ch. 8 "Journey Inside the Atom": Bohr model, K/L/M shells, valency "to complete the octet". https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/iesc108.pdf. Size and ionisation trends come in Class 11 Unit 3 §3.7: https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kech103.pdf. The current Class 10 Ch. 5 is "Life Processes" (https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/jesc105.pdf), and no periodic-classification chapter was found in the current Class 10 science book.

### 9.2 Why removing an electron always costs energy

**(1) Likely misconceptions**
- *Atoms "want" full shells; sodium releases energy when it loses an electron.* This is the octet framework. Taber found it among UK A-level students, and it appears even in a radio interview about lithium batteries. https://science-education-research.com/?p=4731. Note that NCERT Class 9 itself defines valency as electrons gained, lost or shared "to complete the octet" (iesc108, above), so she will meet this framing at school.
- *A full shell is unconditionally stable* (Taber CERP 2003; link above).
- *The second ionisation energy is larger because fewer electrons now share the nucleus's pull* (the shared-out idea again, Taber).

**(2) Best sequence or demo**
- MIT 3.091SC Session 7 (Sadoway): photoelectron spectroscopy, where peak height gives the number of electrons in a shell and energy tells you the shell. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/3-091sc-introduction-to-solid-state-chemistry-fall-2010/pages/structure-of-the-atom/7--the-aufbau-principle-photoelectron-spectroscopy/
- MIT 5.111SC Lecture 8, "The Periodic Table and Periodic Trends", with a clicker-question PDF: https://www.ocw.mit.edu/courses/5-111sc-principles-of-chemical-science-fall-2014/pages/unit-ii-chemical-bonding-structure/lecture-8
- OpenStax 6.5 (above) for the B and O dips, which the book defers to orbitals.

**(3) Recommendation**
- The `careful` block on "sodium wants a full shell", the "Be Mendeleev" cards and the "element with a cliff" case are **already strong**. They are among the best-targeted parts of the four chapters.
- **Predict-the-cliff:** before `c9_successive_li`, ask "Sodium is 2, 8, 1. Sketch the bars for removing its first three electrons. Where is the big jump?" Then show lithium as the check. Make the figure toggle to a log scale so the second and third bars fit on the screen.
- In the check answer for 9.2, the phrase "from an ion that is already positive" is good. Add "not because the pull is now shared among fewer electrons", which names the misconception rather than leaving it implicit.
- **A small accuracy point:** "outer electrons are held loosely enough that the heat of a flame can lift them" ties flame colour to *ionisation* ease. The colour actually depends on the energy gaps of the excited states of the (neutral) atom (Duncan 2025). Suggest: "...a flame is hot enough to lift an outer electron to a higher level without tearing it away".

### Case: The element with a cliff
**Already strong.** Clue 1, "every value is positive", is a neat way to plant the "always costs energy" idea.

## Chapter 10 — Carbon chains and the chemistry of life

### Cross-chapter misconception: properties of the bulk given to the particles
In a CERP 2015 diagnostic of grades 6–11 (N = 696), 38.6% chose "ice molecules are solid and water molecules are liquid", 35.2% that sugar "melts" in water, and 24.7% that water molecules change shape to fit their container. Rates did not fall reliably with grade. https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2015/rp/c5rp00096c. Taber calls this macro-micro confusion; one of his examples is "butter is soft because its molecules are soft". https://science-education-research.com/?p=47683
**Implication for Ch. 10:** phrases like "ethane is a gas" and "a liquid you could pour" are fine for substances. Watch for any wording that says a *molecule* is liquid or solid. In 10.5 especially, say "ghee is solid because its molecules pack and cling", not "saturated molecules are solid".

### 10.1 Chains, branches and rings

**(1) Likely misconceptions**
- *A bent drawing of a chain is a different molecule (or a new isomer).* Orientation gets confused with identity. RSC, "Teaching isomerism": https://edu.rsc.org/teaching-16-18-chemistry/teaching-isomerism-at-post-16/4020424.article. OpenStax 20.1 also shows three drawings of n-butane that are the same molecule: https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/20-1-hydrocarbons
- *Molecules are flat, as drawn.* The book answers this with van 't Hoff and the 3D model.

**(2) Best sequence or demo**
- The RSC recommends building and rotating physical models: alkanes show free rotation, and alkenes show the locked C=C (same RSC link).
- PhET *Molecule Shapes* is the obvious simulation link. Its page did not render in this pass, so check the URL before citing it.

**(3) Recommendation**
- The van 't Hoff scene, the 2n+2 rule, the skeletal-formula note and `organic3d` are **already strong**.
- **Investigation 10.1 is weakly matched to the section.** It compares diamond, water and salt rather than chains. Suggest instead: "Make butane from 4 large and 10 small balls (peas or soaked chana with toothpicks). Bend the chain into a U. Is it a new molecule?" The answer is no: same connections, and the C–C bonds just rotated. This deals with the orientation-versus-identity confusion before 10.4 needs it.
- **Home activity:** the toothpick and chana model is kitchen-safe and has a strong cultural fit.

**(4) NCERT:** Class 10 Ch. 4 §4.2.2 "Chains, Branches and Rings" (the same title), §4.2.1 saturated/unsaturated, §4.2.4 homologous series. https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/jesc104.pdf. Her school book also uses butane to introduce isomers.

### 10.2 The part that does the chemistry

**(1) Likely misconceptions**
- *Hydrogen bonds exist only when O and H are both present*, or only in dipolar molecules. Schmidt et al., CERP 2009: https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2009/rp/b920829c
- *–OH makes the molecule boil higher because it is heavier.* The book's `careful` block addresses this.
- *Oil floats on water only because it is lighter* (density mistaken for the reason they don't mix). The book's petrol sentence already separates "don't mix" from "floats". Keep it.

**(2) Best demo**
- ACS Middle School Chemistry Ch. 5 L4, "Why does water dissolve sugar?": identical sugar-coated sweets in water, rubbing alcohol and mineral oil. The coating dissolves in water, barely in alcohol, and not in oil. The ACS uses models of sucrose's –OH groups to explain why. https://www.acs.org/content/dam/acsorg/msc/downloads/chapter-5/lesson-4/ch5-l4-lesson-plan.pdf
- A cultural extension: turmeric colours the oil of a tadka strongly but hardly dissolves in plain water. Curcuminoids have poor water solubility: https://www.nutraingredients.com/Article/2025/01/21/study-provides-insights-into-enhancing-curcumin-absorption/

**(3) Recommendation**
- The orange-peel balloon is **already strong**: surprising, safe and directly on point. "Oil, water and soap" is also good.
- **Add a "Gems test" to the soap `:::home`, or as a new short one:** one sugar-coated sweet each in water and in cooking oil. Predict first which will lose its colour. It connects to the chapter question (sugar into chai) with something she can eat afterwards (the dry ones).
- **Wording:** in the balloon scene, "Water, whose molecules have strong charged ends" would be more accurate as "*slightly* charged ends", matching the later O–H paragraph.
- **Table:** add a "Where you meet it" column (vinegar, sanitiser, fish smell, ghee and oils) to make the functional groups feel familiar.

### 10.3 Longer chains, higher boiling points

**(1) Likely misconceptions**
- *Boiling breaks covalent bonds inside molecules.* This was the most prominent alternative conception in Schmidt et al. (CERP 2009, German students aged 16–19). https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2009/rp/b920829c
- *Non-polar molecules don't attract each other at all*; and the macro-micro confusion (see above).

**(2) Best sequence or demo**
- OpenStax 10.1: the n-pentane, isopentane and neopentane boiling points with surface-area reasoning, and **geckos sticking by dispersion forces**, a vivid hook. https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/10-1-intermolecular-forces
- OpenStax 20.1, Table 20.1: boiling points rise with carbon number; petrol is C5–C9. https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/20-1-hydrocarbons
- Household alkanes for a ladder: LPG is mainly propane and butane (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquefied_petroleum_gas); mineral oil has 15 carbons and up (https://www.chemicool.com/definition/mineral_oils.html); petroleum jelly is mainly above C25 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_jelly); paraffin wax is C20–C40 and melts at about 46–68 °C (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paraffin_wax).

**(3) Recommendation**
- The scene, `c10_chain_bp` and the refinery explanation are good.
- **Promote "Notice what does not happen when an alkane boils" to a `:::careful` block** with the student quote: *"When butane boils, its molecules break apart into carbon and hydrogen."* This is the single best-documented misconception for the section, and right now it is buried in a paragraph.
- **New `:::home` "The alkane ladder"** (observation only; no heating, no petrol or kerosene handling): line up the LPG cylinder (just point at it), baby oil or liquid paraffin, Vaseline and a candle. Put them in order of chain length and ask her to predict the order from runny to hard before reading the carbon counts. Then rub a little Vaseline and a little baby oil between her fingers: same kind of molecule, different length, different stickiness.
- **Gecko hook:** one sentence ("a gecko hangs from a ceiling by the same flickering attractions") would make dispersion forces memorable. Geckos are common on Mumbai walls.
- **Figure `c10_chain_bp`:** add room temperature (about 30 °C in Mumbai) as a line, so gas and liquid can be read off directly.

**(4) NCERT:** Class 10 Ch. 4 §4.2.4 (homologous series; trend in physical properties) (jesc104). Petroleum refining appeared in the old Class 8 "Coal and Petroleum". Its place in the new Curiosity series was not verified.

### 10.4 Same formula, different molecule

**(1) Likely misconceptions**
- *Same formula, same substance*, and *a boiling-point difference means one is heavier*. The book's case and `careful` block address both.
- *A rotated drawing is a different isomer* (RSC isomerism; link above).

**(2) Best sequence or demo**
- The RSC recommends naming and comparing C6H14 isomers so that students focus on connectivity, and building models (same link).
- OpenStax 10.1: the pentane isomers by shape and surface area (link above). These are the same three as the book's check question, with matching values.

**(3) Recommendation**
- **Already strong**: the Liebig and Wöhler scene (a friendship rather than a feud), the car-or-rickshaw analogy, the pentane check and the two-clear-liquids case.
- **Optional packing demo:** a handful of uncooked spaghetti strands (straight chains) next to a handful of crumpled paper balls (compact isomers). Which pile has more surface touching? It makes "contact area" tangible.
- **Figure `organic3d isomers`:** add a "surface contact" shading when two neighbouring molecules are drawn side by side. That shows the reason for the boiling-point difference, not just the structures.

**(4) NCERT:** Class 10 Ch. 4 §4.2.2 (structural isomers of butane) (jesc104).

### 10.5 Double bonds and locked shapes

**(1) Likely misconceptions**
- *Oils are liquid because their molecules are liquid or soft* (macro-micro; Taber's butter example: https://science-education-research.com/?p=47683).
- *"Unsaturated" means missing atoms or lighter.* No direct source was found for this. Treat it as a plausible risk, not a documented one.

**(2) Best demo**

**(3) Recommendation**
- The **fridge test is already strong**, and so is the stearic-versus-oleic comparison. Local hook: coconut oil in a Mumbai kitchen often goes solid on December mornings. Mention it.
- **Packing analogy:** "pencils in a box" (straight) is in the text. Add the kinked counterpart: "*bent* hairpins or bent straws, which tangle and won't stack". A quick kitchen trial: stack 10 straight straws, then 10 bent straws.
- **Wording:** check that "saturated chains are solid" never appears. The text says the fat is solid; keep it that way.

**(4) NCERT:** Class 10 Ch. 4 §4.2.1 saturated and unsaturated; §4.3.3 addition reactions, including hydrogenation of oils (jesc104).

### 10.6 Acids, amines and the links of life

**(1) Likely misconceptions**
- *Atoms are destroyed when molecules join, so the product is lighter.* The book has this as a `careful` block. AAAS found about 36% of answers chose "atoms destroyed" (AAAS link above).
- *Every –OH is acidic or basic.* Students may carry over "hydroxide" ideas from acids and bases. The book's contrast of carboxyl –OH with ethanol's –OH handles this well. No specific survey statistic was found.

**(2) Best sequence or demo**
- **Lemon on fish (kitchen-safe):** the fishy smell is trimethylamine, an amine. Acid such as lemon juice turns it into an odourless salt. That is an amino group acting as a base. Cosmos: https://cosmosmagazine.com/chemistry/tips-to-keep-fish-in-your-fridge-smelling-less-fishy; ACS *Reactions* video page: https://www.labmanager.com/how-to-make-fish-less-fishy-chemistry-life-hacks-9137
- Vinegar and baking soda for the carboxyl group giving up H⁺ (the CO₂ fizz). ACS Middle School Chemistry Ch. 6 L7 uses this reaction for energy change: https://www.acs.org/content/dam/acsorg/msc/downloads/chapter-6/lesson-7/ch6-l7-ccela.pdf

**(3) Recommendation**
- The stories (Fischer, Hodgkin, Asima Chatterjee), the ester `careful` block and the glycine-dimer check are **already strong**.
- **Add a short `:::home` "The fish and the lemon"**: smell a piece of fish (or dried Bombay duck, bombil) and then squeeze lemon on it. The amine is picked up by H⁺ and can no longer escape into the air. This makes the "–NH₂ picks up H⁺" note something she has smelled.
- **Peptide-chain model:** paperclips as amino acids. Each time two are linked, she sets aside one "water" token (two small and one big bead). After joining five, count: four waters removed. This makes "one water molecule at a time" countable and repeats the book's *count everything* theme.
- Ester-making itself needs concentrated acid and is **not** kitchen-safe. Keep it on the page, and point to smelling a ripe banana or pineapple instead.

**(4) NCERT:** Class 10 Ch. 4 §4.4 (ethanol and ethanoic acid; esterification) (jesc104). Amino acids and proteins come in Class 12 Biomolecules (not verified in this pass).

### Opening (soda water)
**Already strong** as a hook. ### 11.1 The ocean and the carbon dioxide we add

**(1) Likely misconceptions**
- *"Ocean acidification" means the sea is (or will become) acidic.* ACS Lesson 6.10 lists this explicitly and notes the ocean is basic, about pH 8.2 moving to 8.1: https://www.acs.org/content/dam/acsorg/msc/downloads/chapter-6/lesson-10/ch6-l10-lesson-plan.pdf. The MBARI lesson targets the related idea that organisms are harmed only once the water turns acidic: https://www.mbari.org/lesson-plan/the-war-for-carbonate-ions-in-ocean-acidification/
- *Acidification is caused by chemical pollution or acid rain*, not CO₂. This was found even among science undergraduates (19–28% by major). CBE—Life Sciences Education (2015): https://www.lifescied.org/doi/10.1187/cbe.14-11-0209
- *Burnt fuel disappears; gases have no mass; "what goes in must come out" while ignoring oxygen.* Kind, *Beyond Appearances*, via RSC: https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-ideas-about-open-system-chemical-events-beyond-appearances/4017791.article; Science Learning Hub: https://www.sciencelearn.org.nz/resources/796-alternative-conceptions-about-fire
- *The greenhouse effect is trapped sunlight bouncing back, or a literal glass layer; ozone confusion.* In Shepardson et al. 2011 (grades 7–12), about a quarter drew a literal greenhouse, and only about 25% named CO₂ as a greenhouse gas. https://sciencepolicy.colorado.edu/students/envs_4800/shepardson_2011.pdf

**(2) Best sequence or demo**
- ACS Middle School Chemistry Ch. 6 L10, "Carbon dioxide can make a solution acidic": breath, soda water, vinegar + baking soda and a **candle-heated flask** all turn bromothymol blue yellow. A vinegar-only control shows that it is the CO₂, not the vinegar. (ACS link above.)
- Cabbage-juice ocean-acidification versions: Pacific Shellfish Institute, with a control cup and a "purplish-pink" result: https://www.pacshell.org/pdf/ACTIVITY_OA_CabbageJuice.pdf; California Academy of Sciences, which advises a darker starting colour: https://Calacademy.org/sites/default/files/assets/images/Education_Images/sah_ocean-acidification-experiment_201123.pdf
- NOAA: pH down 0.1 units, about 30% more acidity; the ocean takes up about 30% of CO₂; carbonate binding reduces shell-building; pteropod shells dissolved over 45 days in 2100 conditions. https://www.noaa.gov/education/resource-collections/ocean-coasts/ocean-acidification
- OpenStax 14.6 for the buffer logic: a buffer holds until one partner runs out (the carbonic acid/bicarbonate pair). https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/14-6-buffers
- PhET *Greenhouse Effect* (https://phet.colorado.edu/en/simulations/greenhouse-effect) and *Molecules and Light* (https://phet.colorado.edu/en/simulations/molecules-and-light) show CO₂ absorbing infrared, against the "bouncing sunlight" model.

**(3) Recommendation**
- **Already strong:** the Keeling scene ("the planet breathing"), the `careful` block on "turning into acid", the 10^0.1 worked example, the eggshell-in-vinegar activity with its honest "sped-up picture" caveat, and Station ALOHA as a second record.
- **Upgrade "Your breath is an acid-maker" into a buffer race:**
  - Two glasses of cabbage indicator in water. Into one, stir a very small pinch of baking soda until it is blue-green: "your pretend sea".
  - Predict which will change colour first, then blow 10 breaths into each, then 10 more.
  - The plain glass shifts to purple-pink first. The "sea" holds out, and then gives way if she keeps going. That is buffering, and buffer capacity being used up, in two glasses.
  - Optional ACS step, with an adult: hold an upturned glass over a lit tealight for 10 seconds, then pour in cabbage juice and swirl. Burning made CO₂, and the CO₂ made acid. This closes the loop from the gas stove to the sea.
  - Note on the existing activity: with plain tap water, red cabbage's change from breath is subtle. Starting slightly basic makes it far more visible (CalAcademy and Pacshell tips).
- **`c11_carbonate` figure: show the net count.** Add the combined equation **CO₂ + H₂O + CO₃²⁻ → 2 HCO₃⁻**, animated: one CO₂ drops in, one carbonate disappears, two bicarbonates appear, the pH needle barely moves and a "carbonate for shells" counter ticks down. This makes "the buffer is not free" countable and uses the book's *count everything* idea.
- **One sentence on how CO₂ traps heat**, aimed at the Shepardson finding. Something like: "the ground gives off invisible infrared light, and CO₂ molecules absorb it and send it off in all directions, some back down." Link the PhET sims.
- **Pre-empt the pollution and acid-rain mix-up** with half a line in the `careful` block: "This isn't caused by factory waste or acid rain. It's the same CO₂ that is in your soda."

**(4) NCERT:** Curiosity Grade 7 Ch. 2 "Exploring Substances: Acidic, Basic and Neutral": litmus, red rose and turmeric indicators, and purple cabbage mentioned. https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/gecu102.pdf. ### 11.2 A battery you can refill

**(1) Likely misconceptions**
- *Charging fills the battery with electrons* and *a dead battery has run out of electrons or charge*. These are consumer-source and "used up" ideas (IOP Spark; link above). The book's `careful` block addresses them.
- *Electrons cross the electrolyte or separator* (Sanger & Greenbowe via Supasorn 2015; link above).
- *Energy is produced or used up* in the reaction (RSC, "Chemical energetics: words matter"): https://edu.rsc.org/cpd/chemical-energetics-words-matter/2000004.article

**(2) Best sequence or demo**
- Nobel Prize 2019 popular information: intercalation, meaning lithium ions stored in atom-sized spaces between layers. Whittingham used TiS₂; Goodenough's cobalt oxide doubled the voltage to about 4 V; Yoshino used petroleum coke. Ions flow back and forth without wearing down the electrodes. https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/2019/popular-information/
- OpenStax 17.5: LiCoO₂ and LiC₆ electrodes, about 3.7 V per cell, primary versus secondary cells. https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/17-5-batteries-and-fuel-cells
- MIT 3.091SC (Sadoway) hosts his talk "The Missing Link to Renewable Energy" on the course page: https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/3-091sc-introduction-to-solid-state-chemistry-fall-2010/resources/introduction-to-solid-state-chemistry-1/. It is suitable as a "go further" link. The talk is not covered by OCW's Creative Commons licence, so link to it rather than reuse it.
- NCERT Curiosity Grade 8 §4.3.3 already introduces Li-ion, wear-out after many cycles, lithium and cobalt mining, and solid-state batteries (hecu104; link above).

**(3) Recommendation**
- **Already strong:** the Goodenough, Whittingham and Yoshino scene, the "short path and long path" contrast, the 15-times worked example with its honest "not fifteen times" correction, and the `careful` block on electrons and mass.
- **Analogy to add: "notes between the pages of a book".** Graphite's sheets are pages; lithium atoms are slips of paper tucked between them. Discharging slides each slip out, across, and into a second book (the oxide). Charging pushes them back. No pages are torn, which is why it can repeat hundreds of times. This matches the Nobel description of intercalation.
- **`licell` figure:** add the same two counters as `daniell` (electrons through the wire = Li⁺ through the electrolyte per step; total lithium constant). Add a "charger" button that visibly does the uphill work, with an energy bar that rises on charge and falls on discharge. The animation should show that what is stored is *energy*, not electrons.
- **Link back to NCERT:** one line saying that her Grade 8 book calls this a rechargeable battery that "wears out", and that this section shows *why* (the side-reaction crust).
- No kitchen-safe lithium demo exists. The lemon or coin cell from 8.4 is the hands-on anchor. Say so with a back-reference rather than inventing one.

**(4) NCERT:** Curiosity Grade 8 Ch. 4 §4.3.3 (rechargeable and Li-ion). Class 12 Electrochemistry covers secondary batteries (lead storage, Ni-Cd) but not Li-ion (lech102).
